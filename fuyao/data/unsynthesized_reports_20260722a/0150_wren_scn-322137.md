### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

**F1 — Thermodynamic stability.**  
The target has E_hull = −0.02057 eV/atom, which is within the most favorable ≤0.05 eV/atom scoring bin and suggests it is predicted to be on or slightly below the convex hull. CS1–CS4 include experimentally reported compounds with E_hull = 0, and CS5 is only 0.0024 eV/atom above hull, supporting the idea that low-hull Pt-rich intermetallics are synthesizable. SS/TS contain low-hull materials, but their chemistry is mostly unrelated to Ti–Al–Pt, so they provide insufficient chemical signal and are treated as neutral.

**F2/F3 — Route 1 arc-melting.**  
CS2 provides the strongest procedural evidence: elemental refractory/noble-metal mixtures can be arc-melted in purified Ar and remelted several times to achieve homogeneous, single-phase products. The severity score is reduced because arc melting exceeds 1000–1400 °C and requires multiple operations, but it avoids vacuum halide chemistry and very long ampoule anneals. Kinetics are limited by refractory Pt/Ti, but melting largely bypasses solid-state diffusion, which is why this is the best route.

**F2/F3 — Route 2 sealed solid-state reaction.**  
CS3 and CS4 show Pt-rich ternaries can be synthesized by direct reaction/ampoule annealing, but both highlight long times, repeated grinding, and phase-purity difficulty. The target avoids volatile P/As, making it easier than CS3/CS4 in one respect [Reasonable Inference], but Ti/Pt interdiffusion remains slow. This route is feasible but lower-scoring because condition severity and ordering kinetics are worse than for arc melting.

**F2/F3 — Route 3 CVT.**  
CS5 strongly supports iodine-assisted Pt–Al transport and AlPt₃ formation, which is chemically relevant to the Pt–Al sublattice of TiAlPt₆. However, CS5 also reports sensitivity to transport conditions and competing Pt–Al phases, and it gives no direct Ti transport evidence; therefore Ti incorporation is a major uncertainty. This route may be useful for crystals or phase screening but is not the primary bulk route.

**F2/F3 — Route 4 flux growth.**  
CS1 demonstrates that Pt-rich compounds can be grown as single crystals from NaCl/KCl flux at 1100 °C under Ar. The route may reduce diffusion barriers through liquid-mediated growth, but CS1 is a phosphide with a different structure, so the transferability to TiAlPt₆ is only moderate. Flux growth is plausible as a secondary crystal-growth route after arc-melted precursor preparation.

**F4 — amorphization tendency.**  
Only mixed connectivity is triggered because the target description explicitly contains corner-, edge-, and face-sharing cuboctahedra. The cation size mismatch is not expected to exceed 15% for Ti/Al/Pt metallic radii [Reasonable Inference], and the P4/mmm TiAlPt₆ formula does not imply a >40 atom cell. Thus amorphization risk is low-to-medium rather than high.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

TiAlPt₆ is likely a ground-state or near-ground-state phase because the target E_hull is negative, −0.02057 eV/atom. The strongest synthesis support comes from chemically similar Pt-rich intermetallics: CS2 validates arc-melting of refractory noble-metal-rich compounds, CS4 validates P4/mmm Pt-rich ternary ordering, and CS5 validates Pt–Al cuboctahedral intermetallic chemistry. The most critical enabling factor is the very favorable predicted thermodynamic stability combined with dense metallic bonding and no volatile nonmetal anion. The most critical limiting factor is kinetic/phase competition: Pt/Ti are refractory, and CS3–CS5 show Pt-rich ternaries often require long annealing or suffer competing phases. SS/TS provide little Ti–Al–Pt-specific guidance and are treated mainly as neutral background.
