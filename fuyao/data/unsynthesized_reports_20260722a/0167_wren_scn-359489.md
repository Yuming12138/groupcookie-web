### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target’s E_hull is reported as −0.060081 eV/atom, so it is scored as effectively on-hull and thermodynamically favorable [Target]. Chemically related intermetallics in CS include stable or near-stable examples, such as Nb₃Ru₅B₂ at E_hull = 0.0 (CS1) and Hf₉Mo₄Co at E_hull = 0.0 (CS3). SS/TS entries have poor chemical match and often NaN similarity metadata, so there is insufficient evidence from SS/TS for target-specific thermodynamic chemistry; neutral signal = 50.

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 uses high-temperature arc melting under Ar, matching CS1/CS2/CS5 and requiring no high pressure. The main severity penalties are very high arc temperature and the safety/reactivity of Zr/V/Os handling [Reasonable Inference]. CS1 and CS2 show that repeated remelting under Ar is standard and not unusually exotic, so the route is not scored as extreme.

#### F3 — Route 1

The kinetics are limited by refractory elemental precursors and ordering of a ternary intermetallic from a melt [Reasonable Inference]. CS1/CS2 show repeated remelting is used to improve homogeneity, while CS2 and CS3 indicate long annealing may be needed or phase purity may remain difficult. The liquid phase during arc melting improves cation mixing relative to a purely solid-state route [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 2

Route 2 is more severe than Route 1 because it relies on vacuum or sealed-tube handling, powder pressing, possible regrinding, and long annealing; this is consistent with CS3 and CS4. CS3 specifically uses high vacuum melting and sealed quartz annealing at 1000 °C for 70 h. The absence of high pressure moderates the severity score.

#### F3 — Route 2

Powdered metals improve contact but do not remove the slow-diffusion problem for refractory Zr/V/Os [Reasonable Inference]. CS4’s 700 h anneal and CS3’s homogeneity/foreign-phase comments indicate slow ordering and phase-control challenges. Therefore, the kinetics score is lower than for direct arc melting.

#### F2 — Route 3

Melt growth requires high-temperature specialized equipment and multiple operations, supported by SS2/TS2 tri-arc Czochralski growth and CS1 single-crystal observations. It avoids high pressure and reactive gases, but the thermal and equipment burden is higher than routine arc melting [Reasonable Inference]. SS/TS evidence is only partially transferable because the closest melt-growth example is chemically different from Zr₂V₃Os.

#### F3 — Route 3

Directional solidification can bypass long-range solid-state diffusion and may give better crystallinity if Zr₂V₃Os melts congruently [Reasonable Inference]. However, congruent melting is not established by CS/SS/TS, and SS2/TS2 report phase/crystal-quality challenges for related melt-grown intermetallic families. Thus, kinetics are better than powder annealing but still uncertain.

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

The target has mixed corner/edge/face-sharing cuboctahedral connectivity, which triggers the connectivity check [Target]. Zr is appreciably larger than V and Os, triggering the radius-mismatch check by metallic-radius reasoning [Reasonable Inference]. The compact formula and lack of evidence for a >40-atom unit cell avoid the atom-count trigger [Target], giving a medium amorphization/disorder risk.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Zr₂V₃Os is likely a thermodynamic ground-state or near-ground-state phase because its reported E_hull is negative, effectively on-hull [Target]. The strongest synthesis support comes from CS: chemically similar refractory and Zr-containing intermetallics are commonly made by elemental arc melting and annealing under Ar or vacuum (CS1–CS5). SS/TS evidence is weaker because many entries are chemically unrelated hydrides, phosphates, halides, or nitrides with NaN similarity metadata, so those sources mostly provide neutral rather than decisive support. The key enabling factor is that the target is an all-metal intermetallic with no volatile anion, making direct alloying feasible [Reasonable Inference; supported by CS1/CS2/CS5]. The key limiting factor is slow homogenization/ordering among refractory metals and possible competing binary/nearby ternary phases, as related systems show incomplete phase purity or homogeneity issues (CS2/CS3/CS5).
