### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target E_hull is −0.06106832 eV/atom, placing it in the ≤0.05 eV/atom stability bin and justifying a high F1 score of 97. Similar synthesized entries in CS include near-hull or hull-stable ruthenates such as Sr₂Ru₃O₁₀ with E_hull = 0.0395 eV/atom and Sr(RuO₃)₂/SrRu₂O₆-related material with E_hull = 0.0 eV/atom, supporting that low-hull Sr–Ru oxides can be experimentally accessible (CS1, CS3). Remaining uncertainty is that computed negative hull values do not guarantee kinetic accessibility or correct oxidation-state stability [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 is conditionally severe because CS1 uses 480–650 °C and 1800–2100 bar supercritical water in a gold capsule, while CS2 uses 600 °C and 150 MPa in a gold tube. The required rubric does not penalize sub-5-GPa hydrothermal pressure, but the route still loses points for multi-step capsule/autoclave handling and hazardous Ru/oxidant chemistry (CS1, CS2; [Reasonable Inference]). The remaining uncertainty is whether the oxidizing power is sufficient to stabilize Ru(VI) tetrahedral SrRuO₄ rather than octahedral Sr–Ru phases (CS1).

#### F3 — Route 1

RuO₂ is a chemically appropriate Ru source because it is used in CS1 and CS2, but it is structurally unlike the tetrahedral RuO₄ target and is kinetically less active than soluble high-valent ruthenates [Reasonable Inference]. CS1’s non-homogeneous product containing two crystalline phases indicates phase selection and ordering are difficult under this family of conditions. Therefore, Route 1 has moderate-to-poor kinetics despite hydrothermal enhancement.

#### F2 — Route 2

Route 2 is milder in temperature because CS3 uses only 200 °C for 24 h in Teflon-lined steel autoclaves. It is still penalized for sealed oxidizing operation, multiple handling steps, and hazardous oxidizing precursors such as KRuO₄ and SrO₂ (CS3; [Reasonable Inference]). The uncertainty is whether the autogenous-pressure Teflon route can crystallize the denser zircon-like SrRuO₄ phase rather than the phases reported in CS3.

#### F3 — Route 2

Route 2 has the best kinetic outlook because KRuO₄ and SrO₂ are reactive oxidizing precursors used directly for high-valent alkaline-earth ruthenate hydrothermal synthesis (CS3). The KRuO₄ precursor is especially attractive because the target contains tetrahedral RuO₄ with short Ru–O bonds, making local structural inheritance plausible [Reasonable Inference]. The main kinetic uncertainty is phase competition and possible metastability/decomposition, which CS3 reports for related products at 300–500 °C.

#### F2 — Route 3

Route 3 avoids extreme temperature and multi-GPa pressure, but it requires a sealed oxidizing or evacuated ampoule analogous in procedure to CS4/CS5. It is penalized for controlled atmosphere, volatile/gas-evolving oxidizers, multi-step grinding/annealing, and hazardous Ru/oxidizer handling [Reasonable Inference]. Its evidence base is weaker because CS4/CS5 are alkaline-earth nitrate/hydroxynitrate systems, not Sr–Ru–O.

#### F3 — Route 3

Solid-state kinetics are less favorable because RuO₂ is refractory and the target requires high oxidation-state Ru in tetrahedral coordination [Reasonable Inference]. CS1–CS3 suggest Sr–Ru high-valent phases are more reliably accessed hydrothermally than by dry solid-state methods. The remaining uncertainty is whether a strong oxygen chemical potential buffer could compensate for sluggish diffusion and stabilize SrRuO₄ before decomposition or competing phase formation.

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

The target has one amorphization trigger: large size mismatch between Sr²⁺ and high-valent tetrahedral Ru, inferred from the target’s SrO₈ and RuO₄ coordination environments [Reasonable Inference]. The structure is simple ABO₄ zircon-like rather than a very large mixed-connectivity framework, so the unit-cell and connectivity triggers are not activated [Reasonable Inference from target structure]. SS/TS provide insufficient target-specific evidence for amorphization tendency because their closest entries are chemically unrelated.

The CS evidence supports Sr–Ru oxide synthesis under hydrothermal oxidizing conditions, especially using Sr/Ru precursors directly in CS1 and high-valent KRuO₄/SrO₂ chemistry in CS3. The most critical enabling factor is maintaining a high oxygen chemical potential while using reactive high-valent Ru precursors, as demonstrated by KRuO₄/peroxide hydrothermal chemistry (CS3). The most critical limiting factor is phase competition with octahedral Ru–O Sr ruthenates and multiphase products, explicitly observed in CS1 and implied by the diverse products in CS3. SS and TS provide little direct chemical transferability, so the assessment relies primarily on CS.
