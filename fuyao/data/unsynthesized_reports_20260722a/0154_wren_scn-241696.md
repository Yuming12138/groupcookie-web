### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target’s reported E_hull is −0.025609254 eV/atom, placing it in the highest stability bin by the supplied rubric. Chemically similar Eu₂Pt₃Si₅ and LaPt₂Si₂ have E_hull = 0.0 eV/atom, showing that electropositive-cation Pt–Si ternaries can be true ground-state phases (CS1; CS2). The main uncertainty is that Sr(PtSi)₄ itself is not experimentally reported in the supplied data; SS/TS entries are mostly chemically unrelated, so they provide insufficient direct chemical stability evidence and are treated as neutral source signal 50.

#### F2 — Route 1 Flux Growth

Flux synthesis is moderately severe because it requires sealed vacuum/Ar handling, slow cooling, flux decanting, and air-sensitive Sr handling; analogous In/Sn flux syntheses used sealed/vacuum ampoules and post-growth flux removal (CS1; CS4; [Reasonable Inference]). The temperature is near 1000 °C for In flux and up to 1050 °C for Sn flux, which is demanding but below ultra-high-temperature sintering (CS1; CS4). The uncertainty is whether Sr reacts with In/Sn flux or volatilizes sufficiently to require tighter stoichiometry control, which is [Reasonable Inference].

#### F3 — Route 1 Flux Growth

Flux growth improves kinetics by providing liquid transport, as Eu₂Pt₃Si₅ was successfully obtained from In flux and Pu₂Pt₃Si₅ from Sn flux (CS1; CS4). The target’s Pt–Si distances match those in known Pt–Si analogues, supporting favorable local bonding during crystallization (CS1–CS3). However, CS4 reported Si/PtSi/ternary mixtures and CS1 noted direct-heating impurities, so phase competition remains a real kinetic/thermodynamic uncertainty (CS1; CS4).

#### F2 — Route 2 Arc/Induction Melting + Anneal

Arc/induction melting is severe because it involves very high local temperature, controlled Ar atmosphere, and sealed-tube annealing; CS2 used cold-crucible induction melting and CS3 used arc melting under Ti/Zr-gettered Ar followed by evacuated-quartz heat treatment. Sr introduces additional air-sensitivity and possible volatility not present for La, which is [Reasonable Inference]. The route is still plausible because CS2/CS3 demonstrate that elemental RE/Pt/Si melts can form related Pt–Si ternary phases.

#### F3 — Route 2 Arc/Induction Melting + Anneal

Kinetics are moderate-to-poor because dense elemental Pt and Si must homogenize, and CS3 required very long anneals of 170 h at 900 °C and 350 h at 500 °C for related alloys. Similar Pt–Si bond lengths in the target and LaPt₂Si₂ analogues support local structural compatibility (CS2; CS3). The uncertainty is whether Sr loss during melting shifts the composition away from Sr(PtSi)₄, which is [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 3 Sealed-Tube Solid-State Sintering

Solid-state sintering requires controlled atmosphere, sealed/vapor-tight containment, repeated grindings, and likely high temperatures near 1000–1100 °C; CS5 used 1400 °C, BN/Ta containment, crushing, recompaction, and final heat treatment for a related tetragonal 1:4:4 material. CS3 also supports sealed evacuated annealing for Pt–Si alloys. The uncertainty is that no direct Sr–Pt–Si solid-state analogue is given, so transferability is weaker than for flux or melt routes.

#### F3 — Route 3 Sealed-Tube Solid-State Sintering

The kinetic score is lowest because Pt is refractory and solid-state diffusion into an ordered Pt–Si framework is likely slow; CS3 and CS5 both imply long or repeated thermal treatments are needed for related dense intermetallic/boride systems. Pre-reacted Sr silicide may improve reactivity and Sr retention, but that is [Reasonable Inference]. The absence of a same-structure precursor or directly reported Sr(PtSi)₄ analogue creates substantial uncertainty.

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

The target has a large size contrast between Sr and the Pt–Si framework atoms, triggering one amorphization/geometric-mismatch check; the structural description places Sr in a large 8-coordinate Sr–Pt environment while Pt–Si bonds are short, 2.45–2.52 Å (target structure; [Reasonable Inference]). There is insufficient evidence that the unit cell contains more than 40 atoms, and insufficient evidence for mixed vertex/edge/face-sharing polyhedral connectivity from CS/SS/TS. Known Pt–Si ternaries crystallize by flux, arc melting, or induction melting, suggesting crystallization is feasible rather than strongly amorphous (CS1–CS3).

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Sr(PtSi)₄ is likely a ground-state or very near-ground-state phase because the target E_hull is reported as −0.0256 eV/atom, and chemically similar Pt–Si ternaries such as Eu₂Pt₃Si₅ and LaPt₂Si₂ are reported on the hull (CS1; CS2). The strongest synthesis support comes from CS: multiple electropositive-cation Pt–Si compounds were synthesized by In/Sn flux, induction melting, or arc melting/annealing (CS1–CS4). SS/TS are much less chemically transferable because most nearest entries are hydrides, phosphates, halides, nitrides, or actinide borides, so they mainly support broad method classes rather than Sr–Pt–Si chemistry. The most critical enabling factor is liquid-phase transport in flux growth, which should overcome slow Pt/Si diffusion and favor crystallization (CS1; CS4). The most critical limiting factor is phase competition and stoichiometry control, especially formation of PtSi/Si impurities and possible Sr loss, as related flux/direct-heating systems reported impurities or multiphase products (CS1; CS4; [Reasonable Inference]).
