### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The reported E_hull of −0.0266 eV/atom places Cs₂SF₆ effectively on the convex hull, so the thermodynamic stability score is very high. Cs₂SnF₆, the closest chemical/structural analogue, is also hull-stable with E_hull = 0.0 eV/atom (CS1). The TS entries are near-hull but chemically unrelated; therefore, there is insufficient evidence from TS for specific Cs–S–F stability and TS is treated as neutral.

#### F2/F3 — Route 1: Sealed-tube CsF + SF₄

The severity score is reduced because SF₄ is volatile, toxic, moisture-sensitive, and requires sealed fluorination hardware [Reasonable Inference], while CS2 shows that CsF + molecular fluoride reactions require controlled Ar/evacuated conditions. Kinetically, this route is favorable because molecular SF₄ should react readily with dry CsF [Reasonable Inference], analogous to CsF reacting with BrF₃ at low temperature in CS2. The strongest structural support is Cs₂SnF₆, which is essentially isostructural with the target in P-3m1 (CS1).

#### F2/F3 — Route 2: High-temperature sealed solid-state

This route is more severe because it combines sealed-tube handling, volatile sulfur fluoride chemistry, and possible repeated anneals [Reasonable Inference]. CS1 and CS5 show that sealed-tube fluoride solid-state synthesis at 600–873 K/600 °C is feasible for Cs fluorides, but CS1 also notes incomplete phase purity for the Cs₂SnF₆-related sample. Kinetics should be acceptable because CsF is not refractory, but sulfur fluoride volatility may limit solid-gas contact and stoichiometry control [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2/F3 — Route 3: Diluted-F₂ fluorination

CS3 directly supports the use of dried Ar handling followed by diluted F₂ tempering at 300–500 °C for alkali fluorometallates. The major penalty is the hazardous reactive atmosphere and the need to control fluorine chemical potential. The kinetic advantage is high precursor reactivity, but the key uncertainty is whether sulfur stops at the S(IV) [SF₆]²⁻ state rather than forming volatile neutral SF₆ [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2/F3 — Route 4: Solution/evaporation

CS4 shows that A₂MF₆ crystals can be grown by reacting an alkali carbonate with H₂[MF₆] acid and slow evaporation at room temperature. This is attractive kinetically because solution chemistry bypasses solid-state diffusion. However, no CS/SS/TS source establishes an accessible or stable H₂[SF₆] or [SF₆]²⁻ solution precursor, so this route remains speculative and receives a lower practical score.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has a large size/coordination mismatch between CsF₁₂ cuboctahedra and SF₆ octahedra, triggering the ionic-radius-difference criterion [Reasonable Inference]. However, the structure has a small atom count and lacks the full vertex+edge+face-sharing mixture required for the highest amorphization risk. The existence of an isostructural Cs₂SnF₆ analogue in P-3m1 (CS1) also argues against an intrinsically glass-forming topology.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Cs₂SF₆ is likely a thermodynamically stable or near-ground-state phase because its reported E_hull is slightly negative, effectively on the hull. The strongest support comes from CS1: Cs₂SnF₆ has the same A₂MF₆ stoichiometry, the same P-3m1 structure, and very similar Cs–F framework connectivity. The most critical enabling factor is preorganization into isolated MF₆ octahedra within a CsF₁₂ matrix, which is directly demonstrated for Cs₂SnF₆ (CS1). The most critical limiting factor is sulfur-fluoride precursor control: SF₄ is volatile/reactive, and fluorination routes risk forming gaseous SF₆ or other sulfur fluorides [Reasonable Inference]. SS and TS provide insufficient chemically specific evidence for Cs₂SF₆ synthesis, so the recommendation relies primarily on CS evidence.
