### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target’s reported energy above hull is −0.088296 eV/atom, so it lies well inside the highest stability bracket and is treated as very likely thermodynamically stable within numerical uncertainty (target data). Chemically similar rare-earth carbonate phases in CS have low-to-moderate positive hull energies, e.g., YCO₄ at 0.0716 eV/atom and GdCO₄ at 0.0581 eV/atom, indicating the RE–carbonate structural family is not strongly destabilized (CS-1, CS-3). SS and TS entries are chemically unrelated and mostly repeat the same non-carbonate examples, so they provide insufficient target-specific evidence and are scored neutral 50 for source-specific relevance (SS, TS).

#### F2 — Route 1 hydrothermal severity

The hydrothermal route is moderate in severity because CS reports only 220 °C, but requires sealed pressure equipment, Pt ampoules, and 50–500 kg/cm² pressure (CS-1, CS-3, CS-4). The route avoids >1000 °C temperatures, GPa pressure, and reactive H₂/NH₃/vacuum atmospheres, so the main deductions are sealed volatile solvent/reagent and multi-step processing (CS-1, CS-3, CS-4). Remaining uncertainty is whether the exact Y₂O₂CO₃ phase forms directly or through a hydroxycarbonate intermediate [Reasonable Inference].

#### F3 — Route 1 hydrothermal kinetics

Hydroxide gels and formic acid are reactive precursors, and CS demonstrates well-crystallized rare-earth carbonate phases at 220 °C over 4 days (CS-1, CS-3, CS-4). Solution/gel transport should greatly reduce diffusion limitations relative to dry oxide reactions [Reasonable Inference]. The kinetic uncertainty is the structural conversion from hydroxycarbonate-like chemistry to the target oxycarbonate stoichiometry [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 2 precipitation/calcination severity

Route 2 has mild equipment and pressure requirements because it uses ambient precipitation and moderate calcination, comparable in temperature scale to the 500 °C precipitation-calcination example in SS/TS (SS-3, TS-3; Reasonable Inference). It does not require a sealed hydrothermal bomb or specialized reactive atmosphere, but it is multi-step due to precipitation, washing/drying, and calcination [Reasonable Inference]. The main uncertainty is the exact CO₂ partial pressure and temperature window needed to isolate Y₂O₂CO₃ before further conversion to Y₂O₃ [Reasonable Inference].

#### F3 — Route 2 precipitation/calcination kinetics

Fine precipitated yttrium carbonate/hydroxycarbonate should have high precursor activity and short diffusion distances [Reasonable Inference]. CS supports that Y/RE carbonate motifs are accessible from wet-chemical hydroxide/carboxylate chemistry, while SS/TS support that precipitated oxoanion materials can crystallize on calcination at around 500 °C, though not specifically for Y₂O₂CO₃ (CS-1, CS-3, CS-4; SS-3, TS-3). The limiting kinetic issue is that calcination must remove H₂O/CO₂ in a controlled way without collapsing to amorphous material or Y₂O₃ [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 3 flux severity

The flux route avoids extreme temperature if kept below 900 °C, but it needs a carbonate melt, slow cooling, and post-growth washing, so it carries moderate severity [Reasonable Inference]. CS-2 supports controlled-cooling flux methodology but has contradictory chemical evidence for the listed LaCO₄ entry, so it is not strongly weighted for target chemistry (CS-2). CO₂ buffering or sealing may be required to avoid carbonate loss [Reasonable Inference].

#### F3 — Route 3 flux kinetics

Molten carbonate flux should improve mass transport relative to direct solid-state reaction [Reasonable Inference], and CS-2 demonstrates flux growth with slow cooling as a general crystallization method (CS-2). However, starting from Y₂O₃ requires more extensive structural reconstruction than starting from a hydroxycarbonate precursor [Reasonable Inference]. The route is therefore less kinetically favorable than hydrothermal or precipitation-calcination routes.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has only one cation, Y³⁺, so cation-size mismatch is not a meaningful amorphization driver (target data). The structure contains carbonate trigonal units and Y–O polyhedra with edge/corner sharing, but no reported face-sharing coexistence and no evidence for a very large >40 atom unit cell, so none of the three amorphization checks is triggered (target data). The remaining uncertainty is that carbonate loss during heating could create transient amorphous yttrium oxycarbonate, but that is a processing risk rather than an intrinsic structural amorphization flag [Reasonable Inference].

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

The target is likely a ground-state or very near-ground-state phase because its reported hull energy is negative, −0.088296 eV/atom, within numerical uncertainty of strong stability (target data). The strongest analogue support comes from CS, especially the Y and neighboring rare-earth carbonate hydrothermal examples with very similar carbonate C–O distances and RE/Y–O coordination environments (CS-1, CS-3, CS-4). SS and TS analogues are not chemically similar to Y–C–O and therefore provide little direct synthetic guidance beyond generic examples of stable materials and precipitation/calcination methods; they are treated as neutral evidence where not applicable (SS, TS). The key enabling factor is the accessibility of rare-earth carbonate/hydroxycarbonate frameworks from reactive wet-chemical precursors (CS-1, CS-3, CS-4). The key limiting factor is phase control during dehydration/decarbonation, because Y₂O₂CO₃ can plausibly compete with hydroxycarbonate precursors and Y₂O₃ decomposition products [Reasonable Inference].
