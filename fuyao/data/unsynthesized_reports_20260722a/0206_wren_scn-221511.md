### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target E_hull is −0.009407141 eV/atom, placing it in the highest stability bin and suggesting a ground-state or numerically degenerate ground-state phase [target data]. YHg₃, the closest exact chemical analogue, is reported with E_hull = 0.0 eV/atom in CS rank 3, SS rank 1, and TS rank 1. Thermodynamic similarity also identifies stable RE/Sc–Hg phases with E_hull = 0.0 eV/atom (TS ranks 1–5), supporting a high thermodynamic feasibility score. Remaining uncertainty is that calculated negative hull values can reflect numerical tolerance, and the exact Y₃Hg₅ phase is not experimentally listed in the references [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 uses moderate temperatures of 200–550 °C, which are directly supported by RE–Hg syntheses in evacuated tubes and sealed quartz ampoules (SS ranks 2–5; CS rank 3; TS rank 1). The main severity penalties are evacuated sealed-tube operation, volatile Hg, and toxic/reactive elemental precursors [SS ranks 2–5; Reasonable Inference]. No high pressure or >1000 °C step is needed, making the condition severity moderate rather than severe. The uncertainty is the internal Hg pressure for a 3:5 stoichiometry and the exact ampoule safety margin [Reasonable Inference].

#### F3 — Route 1

Kinetics are aided by liquid Hg and fine Y filings, as RE–Hg phases form at 200–500 °C in evacuated hard-glass tubes (SS ranks 2–5). Although Y is a refractory metal, analogous YHg₃ forms at ~550 °C in sealed quartz ampoules, implying that Hg-mediated reaction can overcome slow bulk diffusion (CS rank 3; SS rank 1; TS rank 1). Structural reconstruction is not trivial because Y₃Hg₅ is orthorhombic Cmcm while the closest analogues include hexagonal YHg₃ and P6/mmm REHg₂ phases (CS rank 3; SS ranks 1–5). The score is therefore moderate.

#### F2 — Route 2

Route 2 inherits the Ta-container melting strategy from rare-earth alloy syntheses under pure Ar (CS ranks 1,2,4; TS rank 4). It is penalized for probable >1000 °C melt treatment, volatile Hg containment, multistep processing, and toxic/reactive precursors [CS ranks 1,2,4; TS rank 4; Reasonable Inference]. It avoids high pressure and can improve homogenization, but Hg vapor pressure makes this route more hazardous than low-temperature sealed annealing [Reasonable Inference]. The evidence is transferable but less direct because the most detailed Ta-melt examples involve Tl-rich or general RE–M alloys rather than the exact Y–Hg target (CS ranks 1,2,4; TS rank 4).

#### F3 — Route 2

Melt processing improves diffusion and compositional homogenization, consistent with RE alloy preparation by melting and rapid cooling in Ta tubes (CS ranks 1,2,4). However, elemental metal precursors are not intrinsically high-activity precursors, and high-temperature melting may favor known competing Y–Hg phases such as YHg₃ if composition drifts [CS rank 3; Reasonable Inference]. Structural selection after melt cooling is uncertain because no reference reports the exact Cmcm Y₃Hg₅ structure. The kinetics are slightly better than Route 1, but phase control and Hg retention are worse [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 3

Route 3 uses low temperatures comparable to direct RE–Hg sealed-tube synthesis at 200–500 °C (SS ranks 2–5), so it avoids high-temperature penalties. It is more severe than Route 1 because it uses excess Hg and adds flux-removal/annealing operations [Reasonable Inference]. Vacuum/sealed containment and Hg toxicity remain major penalties (SS ranks 2–5; Reasonable Inference). There is insufficient direct evidence from CS/SS/TS for Hg-flux growth of Y₃Hg₅, so the route is treated as plausible but extrapolated.

#### F3 — Route 3

Kinetics should be favorable because liquid excess Hg can wet and transport Y species, and RE–Hg phases are known to form at 200–500 °C in sealed tubes (SS ranks 2–5; Reasonable Inference). Diffusion barriers are likely lower than for a dry metal-metal reaction [Reasonable Inference]. However, composition control after excess-Hg flux is challenging, and no CS/SS/TS entry demonstrates this exact flux route for Y₃Hg₅. The result is a relatively high kinetic score but with significant uncertainty.

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

The target is an intermetallic with only two elements and no anion sublattice, which generally lowers amorphization risk compared with complex multication oxides [Reasonable Inference]. One likely trigger is the >15% size mismatch between Y and Hg metallic radii [Reasonable Inference]. The unit-cell atom count and polyhedral-sharing topology are not fully specified in the provided CS/SS/TS entries, so those source signals are insufficient and neutral-scored at 50. The overall amorphization risk is low–medium rather than high.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Y₃Hg₅ is likely thermodynamically stable or essentially ground-state because its reported E_hull is −0.009407141 eV/atom [target data]. The strongest support comes from exact-binary Y–Hg and RE–Hg analogues: YHg₃ is experimentally synthesized in sealed quartz ampoules, and multiple REHg, REHg₂, and REHg₃ phases are made by direct reaction of rare-earth filings with Hg at 200–500 °C (CS rank 3; SS ranks 1–5; TS ranks 1,3). The most critical enabling factor is Hg-assisted low-temperature reaction, which allows formation of RE–Hg intermetallics far below the Y melting point (SS ranks 2–5; CS rank 3; Reasonable Inference). The most critical limiting factor is control of Hg chemical potential and stoichiometry in a sealed system, because Hg is volatile and competing Y–Hg stoichiometries such as YHg₃ are known (CS rank 3; SS rank 1; Reasonable Inference). Overall, the target is a credible synthetic candidate, with the direct sealed-ampoule route preferred.
