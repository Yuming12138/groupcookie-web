### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0000 eV/atom, so it is predicted to be on the convex hull and receives a high thermodynamic score. Thermodynamic analogues PtSiTe, PtGeSe, Rh₃Te₄, RuSbTe, and Ba(PtGe₃)₄ are also reported at E_hull = 0.0, supporting the idea that related chalcogenide/tetrel frameworks can be true ground-state phases (TS1–TS5). CS also includes several E_hull = 0.0 analogues, while the exact Pbca RhGeTe analogue is only 0.0475 eV/atom above hull, still within the highly synthesizable range (CS1). Remaining uncertainty is polymorphism: the computed P1 target may compete with the known Pbca RhGeTe-like structure [Reasonable Inference from CS1/SS1].

#### F2 — Route 1 CVT

CVT uses moderate temperature, 700 °C for 6 days, and standard silica ampoules, which is favorable (CS1, SS1). The deductions arise from vacuum sealing, volatile/toxic iodine and tellurium, and multi-step transport/crystal-growth control (CS1/SS1; [Reasonable Inference]). The main uncertainty is contradictory evidence: CS1/SS1 list RhGeTe-like material as CVT, while CS2/SS2 notes unsuccessful RhGeTe attempts, so the severity is not purely operational but also reproducibility-related.

#### F3 — Route 1 CVT

The strongest kinetic advantage is that iodine transport can move material through the vapor phase and has direct precedent in the closest RhGeTe structural analogue (CS1, SS1). However, composition control in CVT is sensitive to transport rates and temperature gradient [Reasonable Inference], and CS2/SS2 explicitly warns about RhGeTe synthesis difficulty. Therefore, the route is plausible but not the top recommendation for targeting a specific P1 polymorph.

#### F2 — Route 2 Direct Solid-State

Sealed-tube solid-state reactions at 600–900 °C are widely supported by SS/TS analogues, including PtSiTe, PtGeSe, and Rh₃Te₄ (SS3–SS5, TS1–TS3). The route requires vacuum sealing to retain Te and avoid oxidation, plus likely grinding/reheating to overcome diffusion limitations (SS4/SS5, TS2/TS3). It avoids high pressure and >1000 °C conditions, but the volatile Te ampoule chemistry and multiple processing steps keep it moderate rather than easy.

#### F3 — Route 2 Direct Solid-State

Elemental Rh is refractory and likely slow to react by pure solid-state diffusion [Reasonable Inference], which lowers precursor activity and diffusion scores. Analogues show that sealed-tube reactions can form related phases, but several require long anneals, intermediate grinding, or repeated heating (SS4, SS5, TS2, TS3). The structural match to RhGeTe is favorable (SS1), yet achieving the low-symmetry P1 ordering may require extended annealing [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 3 Flux

Flux growth has moderate operational severity: it avoids high pressure and extreme temperature, but requires sealed ampoules, volatile Te control, slow cooling, and decanting or centrifugation (TS5; SS3/TS1). Te-rich PtSiTe synthesis and Bi/Pb flux crystal growth provide the main basis for this route (SS3, TS1, TS5). Toxicity and flux incorporation are practical risks, especially if Pb is used [Reasonable Inference].

#### F3 — Route 3 Flux

The flux route scores best kinetically because a molten Te-rich or Bi/Pb-rich medium should enhance Rh and Ge transport relative to dry powders [Reasonable Inference]. TS5 demonstrates that slow-cooling flux methods can grow high-quality Ge/Pt-containing intermetallic crystals, and SS3/TS1 shows Te-rich synthesis of a closely related PtSiTe framework. The main uncertainty is that no CS/SS/TS entry directly reports flux-grown RhGeTe, so transferability is inferred rather than proven.

#### F2 — Route 4 High Pressure

High-pressure synthesis is operationally challenging because CS5 uses 35–50 kbar, specialized anvil equipment, BN capsules, graphite heaters, and controlled thermal cycles. The formal deduction table gives no penalty for pressure at or below 5 GPa, but the route is still experimentally demanding by equipment and setup [Reasonable Inference from CS5]. Because ambient-pressure analogues exist, high pressure is not necessary unless polymorph control fails.

#### F3 — Route 4 High Pressure

High pressure may stabilize dense or otherwise inaccessible ternary chalcogenide frameworks, as shown for OsSbTe and related compounds in CS5. However, the target has E_hull = 0 and close ambient-pressure structural analogues, so pressure could be unnecessary or even bias formation toward another dense polymorph [Reasonable Inference]. Kinetics are only moderate because elemental Rh remains refractory and CS5 notes recycling/grinding can be needed.

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

CS/SS/TS provide insufficient direct evidence on amorphization tendency and are treated as neutral for that specific factor. The target’s P1 structure is low symmetry but has only a 12-atom described cell and lacks face-sharing in the provided connectivity, reducing amorphization risk. The main trigger is the Rh/Ge cation size and coordination mismatch [Reasonable Inference], giving a low–medium risk rather than a high risk.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

The target is likely a thermodynamic ground-state or very near-ground-state phase because its reported E_hull is 0.0000 eV/atom, and many CS/SS/TS analogues are also on the hull (Target; CS3–CS5, TS1–TS5). The analogue support is strong chemically and structurally: the closest analogue is exact-composition RhGeTe with very similar RhGe₃Te₃ octahedra and Ge/Te tetrahedral motifs (CS1, SS1). The most critical enabling factor is the existence of well-precedented sealed-tube/flux/CVT chemistry for M–Ge/Si–Te and Rh–Te systems at 600–900 °C (SS3–SS5, TS1–TS3). The main limiting factor is polymorph and phase control, especially because the known close RhGeTe analogue is orthorhombic Pbca while the target is triclinic P1, and CS2/SS2 notes RhGeTe synthesis attempts can fail. Overall, synthesis is plausible, with flux or carefully annealed sealed-tube methods preferred for equilibrating the low-symmetry ordered phase.
