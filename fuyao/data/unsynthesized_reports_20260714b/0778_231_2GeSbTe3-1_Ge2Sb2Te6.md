### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0060 eV/atom, which strongly indicates thermodynamic accessibility or only slight metastability [Target]. This is consistent with many close layered telluride analogues being on or near the hull, including GeSb4Te7 at 0.00385 eV/atom [CS-5/SS-2/TS-1], CrGeTe3 at 0 eV/atom [SS-4], and PbBi6Te10 at 0.00678 eV/atom [TS-3]. The main uncertainty is that chemical analogues include at least one Ge–As–Te compound at 0.142 eV/atom [CS-1], showing that not every chemically related layered telluride is equally stable.

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 avoids extreme temperature and high pressure because the closest structural analogue CrGeTe3 was synthesized at 700 °C in an evacuated sealed quartz tube [SS-4]. The severity penalty comes from vacuum sealing and volatile/toxic Te/Sb-containing charges [SS-4; TS-3; Reasonable Inference]. CS analogues support similar 700 °C plus 350 °C processing [CS-1–4], while insufficient evidence from TS defines an exact GeSbTe3 temperature window, so TS is treated as supportive but not exact.

#### F3 — Route 1

The kinetics are moderate: elemental precursors are less reactive than solution precursors, but Ge, Sb, and Te are not refractory oxide precursors [SS-4; CS-2–4]. Diffusion should be manageable because cation melting points are below or near 1000 °C and chalcogenide formation can proceed at 650–700 °C [SS-4]. Structural reconstruction is favorable because CrGeTe3 has the same Ge–Ge tetrahedral motif and octahedral layer topology [SS-4], but the 20 d synthesis time indicates slow ordering [SS-4].

#### F2 — Route 2

Melt/Bridgman growth uses moderate temperatures compared with oxide synthesis, with analogues processed around 923–1000 K or 930–950 K [SS-3; SS-5; TS-5]. The penalties arise from vacuum-sealed ampoules, volatile Te/Sb, and the multi-step nature of homogenization, directional solidification, and annealing [SS-3; TS-5; Reasonable Inference]. CS gives insufficient direct evidence for Bridgman growth of GeSbTe3 itself, so the route relies mainly on SS/TS analogues.

#### F3 — Route 2

Melt homogenization improves kinetic access because diffusion occurs in a liquid or partially liquid telluride charge [SS-3; TS-5]. Structurally similar layered tellurides such as SnSb2Te4 and PbBi4Te7 can be grown by Bridgman-type routes [SS-3; TS-5]. The uncertainty is whether the P-31m Ge–Ge dumbbell ordering survives equilibrium melt growth or requires low-temperature annealing [SS-4; Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 3

The thin-film route operates at relatively low temperatures, consistent with evaporation-deposition data for GeSb4Te7-type layered tellurides [CS-5/SS-2/TS-1]. It still requires high vacuum and suffers from volatile Te/Sb loss risks [CS-5; Reasonable Inference]. The route is not high pressure and does not require >1000 °C conditions [CS-5].

#### F3 — Route 3

Vapor precursors are available because elemental Ge, Sb, and Te are standard evaporation/sputtering sources, and GeSb4Te7 is linked to evaporation deposition [CS-5]. However, post-deposition annealing is likely required because CS-5 describes heating after condensation rather than direct epitaxial crystallization. Composition control is a major uncertainty because volatile Te can be lost and related phases show disorder/reproducibility issues [CS-5/TS-1].

#### F2 — Route 4

CVT uses moderate temperatures similar to Bi8Te9 growth at 770–820 K [TS-4], so the thermal burden is not severe. Penalties arise from evacuated sealed ampoules, iodine vapor, volatile telluride species, and a multi-step pre-reaction/transport process [TS-4; Reasonable Inference]. There is insufficient evidence from CS/SS for direct GeSbTe3 CVT, so this is exploratory.

#### F3 — Route 4

The CVT route can grow layered telluride crystals according to TS-4, but transport rates may differ for Ge, Sb, and Te [Reasonable Inference]. Crystallization may be slow because TS-4 reports two-month CVT or long annealing for related Bi–Te phases. Composition control is the limiting kinetic factor, and insufficient CS/SS evidence prevents a higher score.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target contains chemically and geometrically distinct GeGeTe3 tetrahedra and SbTe6 octahedra, creating a cation size/coordination mismatch that triggers one amorphization-risk criterion [Target; Reasonable Inference]. The target description does not indicate a >40-atom unit cell, and no face-sharing polyhedral network is described [Target]. Ge–Sb–Te materials are known as phase-change compositions, so amorphization risk is non-negligible, but the required checklist gives only one trigger [Reasonable Inference].

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

GeSbTe3 is likely a near-ground-state or very weakly metastable phase because its E_hull is only 0.0060 eV/atom [Target]. The synthesis case is strongly supported by structurally close CrGeTe3, which shares the Ge–Ge tetrahedral motif and is accessible by sealed-tube solid-state reaction [SS-4], and by Ge–Sb–Te layered analogues such as GeSb4Te7 that are near the hull [CS-5/SS-2/TS-1]. The most important enabling factor is the low-temperature telluride chemistry: Ge/Sb/Te form layered chalcogenides at 500–700 °C without high pressure [CS-1–4; SS-4; TS-5]. The most important limiting factor is phase selectivity against GeTe, Sb2Te3, and related Ge–Sb–Te homologues, especially because analogues report phase-control and reproducibility challenges [CS-5/TS-1]. Overall, the material is realistically synthesizable, with sealed-ampoule annealing being the most defensible first attempt.
