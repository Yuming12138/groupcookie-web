### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

Au₂Te has E_hull = 0.0000 eV/atom, so the thermodynamic score is maximal by the required rubric [Target]. This is reinforced by multiple stable Au-containing analogues, including Au₄Tl₂S₃ with E_hull = 0.0 (SS1/TS1), AuBr with E_hull = 0.0 (SS2/TS2), and Au₆Hg₅ with E_hull = 0.0 (TS4). CS evidence is also favorable because AuTe₂ is near the hull at 0.0182 eV/atom (CS2), although CS1 includes a less stable Tl–Te phase at 0.143 eV/atom, showing that not all chemically related tellurides are equally stable. Remaining uncertainty is kinetic, not thermodynamic.

#### F2 — Route 1 direct sealed-ampoule solid-state

The severity is moderate because analogous sealed-tube reactions use evacuated silica/quartz tubes and controlled heating for chalcogenides (SS5, TS5), and alloy heat treatments in quartz ampoules under Ar are reported for Pt/Sb/Te systems (CS5). The main deductions come from vacuum/sealed atmosphere, volatile Te, and multiple operational steps. There is insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS that Au₂Te requires high pressure, so high-pressure deductions are not applied; the high-pressure conditions in CS2 are associated with AuTe₂ experiments rather than mandatory synthesis.

#### F3 — Route 1 direct sealed-ampoule solid-state

Kinetics are only moderate because elemental Au is dense and not very reactive, while Te can help by melting/vapor transport [Reasonable Inference]. SS5 and TS5 show that elemental sealed-tube chalcogenide reactions are feasible, but they often require high temperature, long annealing, or controlled cooling. CS4 reports very long annealing for Bi–Te layered phases, supporting the expectation of slow ordering. Insufficient evidence from SS/TS exists for the exact Au₂Te P4₂12 sheet, so structural reconstruction is scored conservatively.

#### F2 — Route 2 Te flux / iodine CVT

This route avoids >1000 °C temperatures but requires evacuated sealed ampoules, volatile Te and possibly I₂, and multiple steps. CS4 directly supports I₂-assisted CVT for Bi–Te layered phases, while SS1/TS1 support sealed-tube flux growth of Au–chalcogen material at 650 °C. The route is penalized for toxic/corrosive iodine and volatile chalcogen handling. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS indicates no need for external high pressure.

#### F3 — Route 2 Te flux / iodine CVT

Flux/CVT improves kinetics by increasing mass transport and reducing reliance on Au solid-state diffusion [Reasonable Inference]. This is supported by Au₄Tl₂S₃ flux growth at 650 °C with slow cooling (SS1/TS1) and Bi–Te CVT at 770–820 K using I₂ (CS4). Structural ordering is plausible because the target is layered and CS4 involves layered Bi–Te phases, but no source proves Au₂Te forms by CVT, so the reconstruction score is moderate rather than high. The main uncertainty is phase selectivity between Au₂Te and other Au–Te compositions such as AuTe₂ (CS2).

#### F2 — Route 3 Bridgman / melt growth

Bridgman growth is directly supported by AuTe₂ crystal growth from Au + Te, with better-quality crystals obtained by Bridgman (CS2). The severity score is reduced by possible operation above 1000 °C, sealed/vacuum ampoule requirements, Te volatility, and slow-cooling/annealing steps. TS5 supports high-temperature elemental chalcogenide synthesis with controlled cooling and long annealing. High pressure is not penalized because CS2’s high-pressure conditions refer to experiments on AuTe₂, not necessarily the crystal-growth step.

#### F3 — Route 3 Bridgman / melt growth

Melt growth gives the best kinetic outlook because diffusion occurs in liquid or partially molten material rather than solely through solid Au [Reasonable Inference]. CS2 is the strongest evidence because the chemically closest phase, AuTe₂, is grown by Bridgman from the same elements. TS5 shows that controlled cooling and annealing can select ordered chalcogenide phases from high-temperature elemental mixtures. The remaining uncertainty is whether Au₂Te is the primary crystallizing phase or whether AuTe₂/elemental Au compete.

#### F2 — Route 4 thin-film co-evaporation

CS1 supports vacuum evaporation and subsequent heating for chemically similar materials under 10⁻⁵–10⁻⁶ Torr and 300–700 °C. The route is penalized for high-vacuum operation, Te volatility, and post-deposition annealing steps. It avoids high pressure and very high temperature. There is insufficient evidence from SS/TS for thin-film synthesis of Au₂Te specifically, so the route is not preferred despite acceptable severity.

#### F3 — Route 4 thin-film co-evaporation

Au and Te are available as deposition sources [Reasonable Inference], and CS1 supports evaporation plus heating for related materials. However, Te re-evaporation makes composition control difficult, and CS1 notes phase-control difficulties, defect structures, and narrow composition ranges for some evaporated phases. Crystallization likely requires post-deposition annealing rather than direct epitaxial formation. The uncertainty is high because no CS/SS/TS example directly demonstrates Au–Te thin-film Au₂Te formation.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has only one metal species, Au, so there is no mixed-cation ionic-radius mismatch trigger [Target]. The structure description does not indicate a >40-atom unit cell or mixed edge/corner/face-sharing polyhedral complexity [Target]. Several analogues with complex polyhedra and phase-control issues exist, such as Bi₄I (CS3) and Bi₈Te₉ (CS4), but those complexities are not stated for the target. Thus, amorphization risk is low, with remaining uncertainty only from possible thin-film quenching in Route 4.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Au₂Te is likely a thermodynamic ground-state phase because its E_hull is 0.0000 eV/atom [Target]. The CS evidence is especially supportive because the closest chemical analogue, AuTe₂, is synthesized directly from Au and Te by Bridgman growth (CS2), while SS/TS analogues show that Au–chalcogen and related chalcogenide phases can be made in sealed tubes, fluxes, or controlled-cooling treatments (SS1/TS1, SS5, TS5). The most critical enabling factor is the simple binary chemistry with commercially available elemental precursors and strong thermodynamic stability [Target, CS2]. The most critical limiting factor is phase and stoichiometry control in the Au–Te system, especially avoiding Te loss and competing AuTe₂ or Au-rich products [CS2, CS4, Reasonable Inference]. Overall, bulk melt/Bridgman synthesis is the best first attempt, with Te-flux/CVT as a useful optimization route if crystal quality or diffusion is limiting.
