### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

**F1 — Thermodynamic stability.**  
The target has E_hull = 0.0500 eV/atom, so it is exactly at the threshold where many inorganic phases are experimentally accessible but may be metastable [Target]. Chemically/structurally close Sm–Pt–P phases such as Sm₂(Pt₂P)₃ and SmPtP are stable in the reference data [CS4/SS1, SS3], supporting feasibility. Thermodynamically similar Pt intermetallics with E_hull ≈0.043–0.071 eV/atom were synthesized by arc melting/annealing [TS1–TS5], suggesting that the target’s small positive hull energy is not prohibitive.

**F2 — Route 1.**  
Direct sealed-tube synthesis uses common solid-state conditions but requires high temperature, sealed Ar/vacuum handling, P volatility management, and repeated grinding [SS3, SS5, CS5]. The major severity penalties come from T near/above 1000 °C, volatile P, and air-sensitive Sm [SS3, CS1; Reasonable Inference]. No high-pressure requirement is supported by CS/SS/TS data.

**F3 — Route 1.**  
Kinetics are limited by refractory Pt and solid-state diffusion, as REPtP analogues require 2–4 weeks and intermediate grinding [SS3, SS5, CS5]. The existence of SmPtP supports local Sm–Pt–P bonding compatibility [SS3], but the target’s Pt-rich tetragonal structure differs from the hexagonal 1:1:1 phase, so reconstruction/ordering is nontrivial [Target, SS3].

**F2 — Route 2.**  
The precursor route is more severe because it adds alloy synthesis before phosphidation and may require evacuated tubes at high temperature [CS1, SS4]. CS1 specifically reports repeated grinding, pressing, annealing up to ten times for REIr₂P₂-type phosphides, indicating a demanding multi-step process [CS1]. The benefit is better Sm–Pt homogenization before adding volatile P [Reasonable Inference based on CS1/SS4].

**F3 — Route 2.**  
Preforming Sm–Pt alloy should improve metal mixing relative to elemental Pt powder [Reasonable Inference; CS1, SS4]. However, conversion of a Sm–Pt alloy into the exact Sm₂Pt₄P₃ structure still requires P diffusion and structural rearrangement [CS1, SS4, Target]. Phase impurities in related ceramic phosphide routes reduce the kinetic score [CS1].

**F2 — Route 3.**  
Flux synthesis avoids >1000 °C in many cases but still requires sealed ampoules, volatile P control, flux removal, and toxic Pb or possible Sn contamination [SS2, CS3]. It is less thermally severe than ceramic precursor routes because ScPtP was grown at 1000 °C and Ru/Os phosphides at 880 °C [SS2, CS3]. No high-pressure evidence is present in CS/SS/TS.

**F3 — Route 3.**  
Flux provides liquid-phase transport, improving Pt and Sm mobility and enabling crystallization below the melting point of Pt [SS2, CS3]. Pt–P flux success is directly supported by ScPtP single-crystal growth in Pb [SS2]. The remaining uncertainty is whether the target Sm₂Pt₄P₃ phase, rather than SmPtP or Sm₂(Pt₂P)₃, is the phase selected by the flux composition [SS2, SS1/CS4, SS3].

**F2 — Route 4.**  
Arc melting uses specialized equipment and very high local temperature under gettered Ar, followed by sealed-tube annealing [TS1–TS5]. The route is severe for phosphides because P is volatile and not directly represented in the TS silicide/stannide arc-melting analogues [TS1–TS5; Reasonable Inference]. Therefore, direct arc melting of all elements is not recommended; arc melting should only make Sm–Pt alloy before P addition [Reasonable Inference].

**F3 — Route 4.**  
Arc melting strongly improves Sm–Pt mixing and is successful for RE–Pt–Si analogues with similar thermodynamic metastability [TS1–TS5]. However, the target requires phosphidation after alloying, and P incorporation can reconstruct the alloy substantially [CS1; Reasonable Inference]. This route has good kinetic potential but higher volatility and composition-control risk than flux synthesis.

**F4 — Amorphization/ordering tendency.**  
The target has chemically dissimilar large Sm and smaller Pt/P sublattices, consistent with large bond-length contrast in analogues [CS1, SS1, SS3]. Similar structures show mixed polyhedral sharing, which can increase ordering complexity and metastable defect trapping [CS1, CS3, TS1]. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS is available to confirm a >40-atom target unit cell, so that check was not triggered.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Sm₂Pt₄P₃ is likely a borderline metastable or very weakly metastable phase because its E_hull is exactly 0.0500 eV/atom [Target]. The synthesis outlook is favorable because chemically close Sm–Pt–P phases and structurally related RE–T–P phosphides are experimentally accessible by sealed-tube, ceramic, and flux routes [CS1, CS4, CS5, SS1–SS3]. The strongest enabling factor is the robust formation of RE–Pt–P coordination networks with Sm–Pt distances near 3.1–3.3 Å and Pt–P distances near 2.3–2.5 Å across analogues [CS1, SS1, SS3]. The main limiting factor is phase competition and slow ordering in a Pt-rich phosphide system, since related phosphides often require long annealing, repeated grinding, flux assistance, or show impurity phases [CS1, SS2, CS3]. Flux-assisted growth is the best route because it lowers diffusion barriers while maintaining sealed conditions for volatile P [SS2, CS3].
