### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

**F1 — Thermodynamic stability.**  
The target E_hull is 0.023 eV/atom, which lies well inside the ≤0.05 eV/atom range assigned 90–100, so the thermodynamic score is high. Synthesized TS analogues include phases at similar metastability, such as Eu₂Ni₁₂P₇ at 0.0233 eV/atom and K₂Ni₁₂As₇ at 0.0344 eV/atom (TS1, TS5). CS analogues show that related tellurides can be stable or modestly metastable and still accessible, including multiple 0 eV/atom Cu–Ti/Zr/Sn/Er tellurides and NaCu₃Te₂ at 0.057 eV/atom (CS1–CS5). Remaining uncertainty is that no exact Ti–Ni–Te experimental phase is provided, so a low computed hull distance may still compete with nearby binaries or ternaries [Reasonable Inference].

**F2 — Route 1 condition severity.**  
Route 1 uses 650–900 °C sealed-ampoule flux conditions, which are moderate and directly parallel Cu₂TiTe₃/Cu₂ZrTe₃ (CS1, CS2). Deductions arise from vacuum sealing, volatile Te, multi-step slow-cooling flux growth, and Te toxicity; these requirements are common in CS/TS telluride syntheses (CS1–CS5, TS2, TS3). SS gives insufficient direct evidence for Te volatility because SS analogues are mostly arsenides/phosphides, so SS is neutral for this specific factor. The absence of high pressure or >1000 °C heating keeps the severity from being extreme.

**F3 — Route 1 kinetics.**  
Flux growth improves kinetics because CS1/CS2 produced Ti-containing tellurides at 650–900 °C, far below the Ti melting point, indicating liquid-mediated transport. The target still requires ordering of Ti, three inequivalent Ni sites, and two Te sites in a monoclinic layered structure, so structural reconstruction is nontrivial [Reasonable Inference from target structure and CS1/CS2 long schedules]. TS5 also supports flux as a way to obtain well-crystallized Ni-rich phases, though TS5 is phosphide rather than telluride. Remaining uncertainty is whether Ni–Te/Ti–Te competing phases dominate the melt.

**F2 — Route 2 condition severity.**  
Stoichiometric sealed-tube synthesis has similar temperature and atmosphere severity to Route 1, with vacuum, volatile Te, and multiple grinding/annealing steps. CS3 explicitly uses 650 and 850 °C with regrinding/reannealing for Cu–Sn–Te, while CS5 uses 750 °C for one week in evacuated ampoules. TS2 and TS3 support evacuated or inert high-temperature ampoule synthesis for transition-metal chalcogenides. The route is not high-pressure and need not exceed 1000 °C, but Te vapor pressure and repeated steps impose penalties.

**F3 — Route 2 kinetics.**  
Solid-state kinetics are weaker than flux because Ti and Ni are dense metallic precursors and atomic diffusion must occur through solid interfaces [Reasonable Inference]. SS2/SS3/SS5 and TS2/TS3 show that Ni-containing intermetallic pnictides/chalcogenides commonly require grinding, reannealing, and homogenization. CS3 reports that related telluride phase purity can remain problematic even after extended annealing. Therefore the F3 score is lower than Route 1.

**F2 — Route 3 condition severity.**  
Arc/induction melting adds a >1000 °C processing step, which increases severity relative to Routes 1–2 [Reasonable Inference; SS4 used arc melting for some Ni pnictide samples]. Te volatility makes direct melting risky, so a sealed post-anneal is still needed, retaining the vacuum/volatile-precursor penalties. SS4 supports arc melting plus annealing as a route for refractory Ni-containing compounds, but CS/TS telluride data favor sealed ampoules instead. Thus this route is feasible but conditionally more severe.

**F3 — Route 3 kinetics.**  
Melting or alloying Ti–Ni can reduce cation diffusion barriers, which improves precursor homogenization compared with purely solid-state mixing [Reasonable Inference; SS4]. However, the target telluride must still form during a Te-containing anneal, and Te loss during melting can produce off-stoichiometry [Reasonable Inference; CS1–CS5 consistently use sealed ampoules for Te]. Structural reconstruction into monoclinic TiNi₃Te₂ remains uncertain. The route scores between flux and conventional solid-state.

**F4 — Amorphization tendency.**  
One amorphization risk is triggered because Ti and Ni differ significantly in metallic/ionic size, which can increase local strain and disorder [Reasonable Inference]. The target description does not provide a unit-cell atom count >40, and the explicit polyhedral sharing topology is not given, so C2 and C3 have insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS and are treated as not triggered. CS/SS/TS analogues are crystalline under high-temperature anneal/flux conditions, which argues against severe amorphization under the recommended bulk synthesis routes (CS1–CS5, SS1–SS5, TS1–TS5). Remaining uncertainty is higher for thin films or rapid quenching, which are not the preferred routes [Reasonable Inference].

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

TiNi₃Te₂ is likely a low-energy metastable phase rather than a strict ground state because its E_hull is 0.023 eV/atom. The analogue support is reasonably strong chemically because Cu₂TiTe₃ is synthesized by Te-rich flux from elements under sealed quartz conditions, although the exact Ti–Ni–Te composition is not represented (CS1). Structurally similar Ni pnictides show that Ni-rich frameworks can crystallize by solid-state or flux methods, but SS evidence is chemically indirect because As/P differ from Te (SS1–SS5). The most critical enabling factor is a sealed Te-rich flux, which should enhance Ti/Ni transport while maintaining Te chemical potential (CS1, CS2). The most critical limiting factor is phase control against Ti–Te and Ni–Te binaries, especially because related chalcogenides report impurity phases and repeated annealing requirements (CS3, TS2, TS3).
