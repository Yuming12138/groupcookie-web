### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0000 eV/atom, which directly places it in the highest stability category. CS supports the general feasibility of stable sulfide/halide solids because CS-1, CS-4, and CS-5 have E_hull = 0, while CS-2 and CS-3 are only slightly above hull. SS/TS also support this because SS-1/TS-1 and several Mo sulfide cluster analogues are on the hull, and the Re–Se–Cl sheet analogue SS-3 is near hull at 0.0016 eV/atom. Remaining uncertainty is kinetic, not thermodynamic, because no reference directly reports Re₃S₄F.

#### F2 — Route 1 condition severity

Route 1 uses moderate high temperature, about 800–900 °C, which is supported by SS-3 for Re–chalcogen–halide sheets and is less severe than the 1300 °C SS-1/TS-1 Mo crucible synthesis. However, it requires sealed vacuum/inert conditions like CS-2, SS-2, and SS-3, and it likely uses volatile/air-sensitive Re fluoride by **[Reasonable Inference]**. The score is lowered because multi-step handling, prereaction, and possible regrinding are supported by SS-2/TS-2. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS exists for exact Re fluoride handling, so that uncertainty is penalized rather than assumed benign.

#### F3 — Route 1 reaction kinetics

The Re–S framework is diffusion-limited because Re metal is refractory; this is consistent with high-temperature needs in SS-1/TS-1 and CS-2. The structural reconstruction burden is reduced by the close SS-3 analogue, which has Re₃ chalcogenide-halide sheets and square-pyramidal Re coordination. However, SS-3 also indicates difficult phase control and low yields, while SS-2/TS-2 show repeated heat treatments may be required. Thus kinetics are feasible but not easy.

#### F2 — Route 2 condition severity

Route 2 avoids >1000 °C temperatures, consistent with flux growth CS-3 and Re–chalcogen–halide synthesis SS-3 near 900 °C. It still requires sealed, dry, vacuum/inert operation, because CS-3 used Ar loading and evacuation to 10⁻⁴ Torr and SS-3 used N₂ grinding plus vacuum-sealed ampoules. Flux drying/removal adds procedural complexity, supported by CS-3 and SS-3. Fluoride flux corrosivity is a **[Reasonable Inference]**, and insufficient direct fluoride evidence from CS/SS/TS keeps the severity score moderate-low.

#### F3 — Route 2 reaction kinetics

Flux growth should improve precursor activity and mass transport, supported by CS-3 where CsCl flux enabled chalcogenide crystal growth. Slow cooling is strongly supported by SS-3 for layered Re–chalcogen–halide products. Re diffusion remains intrinsically difficult, but molten-salt transport partly bypasses this limitation by **[Reasonable Inference]** from flux chemistry. The main uncertainty is whether F can be incorporated as a terminal Re–F bond rather than lost to flux or volatile ReFₓ species.

#### F2 — Route 3 condition severity

Route 3 uses >1000 °C annealing, supported by SS-1/TS-1 and CS-2, so it receives a temperature penalty. It requires sealed dry Ar/vacuum refractory containment, as in SS-1/TS-1, and it likely needs volatile fluoride control by **[Reasonable Inference]**. Although high-temperature solid-state reaction is a known route for related sulfides, the combination of F volatility and container compatibility makes conditions severe. Insufficient evidence from TS for fluoride retention at high temperature is a major negative signal.

#### F3 — Route 3 reaction kinetics

High temperature helps overcome diffusion barriers, but Re remains refractory and Re/ReS₂ precursors are not highly reactive. SS-1/TS-1 show that related Mo sulfide cluster phases can form at 1300 °C, but SS-1 also reports non-stoichiometry/peritectic complications, which implies phase-control difficulty. Structural similarity to Mo sulfide clusters is useful but less direct than SS-3 because the target is a Re–S–F layered phase. The main kinetic risk is that high temperature accelerates F loss faster than it accelerates ordered Re₃S₄F formation.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has only one cation species, Re, so the cation-radius mismatch criterion is not triggered; CS/SS/TS do not contradict this. The target description gives edge- and corner-sharing ReS₅/ReS₄F square pyramids but no face-sharing coexistence, and similar edge/corner-sharing motifs are crystallographically realized in SS-1, SS-3, and TS-1. The provided target formula suggests a small primitive composition rather than a >40-atom complex cell; insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS for a larger disordered supercell is treated as neutral. Therefore crystallization risk is more about phase competition than amorphization.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Re₃S₄F is likely a ground-state phase because the target E_hull is 0.0000 eV/atom, and several CS/SS/TS analogues are also stable or near-stable. The strongest synthetic support comes from SS-3, which demonstrates a closely related triclinic P-1, two-dimensional Re–chalcogen–halide sheet made under sealed, slow-cooled conditions. CS and TS support the broader feasibility of sealed-tube sulfide and cluster-chalcogenide synthesis, but they provide insufficient direct evidence for Re–S–F fluoride chemistry. The most critical enabling factor is halide/fluoride-assisted transport in a sealed flux or ampoule, which can overcome refractory Re diffusion and stabilize terminal Re–F coordination. The most critical limiting factor is controlling fluorine chemical potential without losing volatile ReFₓ or attacking the container.
