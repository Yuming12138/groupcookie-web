### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1: Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0000 eV/atom, so it is predicted to be a ground-state phase by the supplied target data. TS analogues Sb₂Te₃, SnSb₂Te₄, CrSiTe₃, and SnBi₄Te₇ are also reported as E_hull = 0.0 eV/atom, supporting the general stability of layered telluride ground states [TS1, TS2, TS4, TS5]. CS analogues are mixed, with some stable and some mildly metastable phases, so CS provides favorable but less decisive thermodynamic evidence [CS1–CS5]. Remaining uncertainty is that computed ground-state stability does not guarantee a wide experimental phase field.

#### F2/F3 Route 1: Evacuated-ampoule solid-state

Condition severity is moderate because the best structural analogue CrSiTe₃ forms at only 500 °C in an evacuated sealed tube, with no high pressure [TS4]. Deductions come from the need for vacuum sealing and volatile Te control, both consistent with telluride ampoule syntheses [TS4, SS4/TS3]. Kinetically, elemental Si is the main limitation because it is refractory, but TS4 proves that a Si–Si telluride framework can form at moderate temperature over long time [TS4]. Remaining uncertainty is whether SbTe₆ octahedra order cleanly with SiSiTe₃ units without competing Sb₂Te₃.

#### F2/F3 Route 2: Melt / Bridgman

SnSb₂Te₄ can be grown from the melt by Bridgman methods in evacuated quartz ampoules, showing that Sb–Te layered phases can tolerate melt processing [SS3/TS2]. TS5 similarly supports melt homogenization followed by long annealing for layered Sn–Bi–Te phases [TS5]. The limiting uncertainty is Si: unlike Sn, Sb, and Te, Si has a high melting point and may not dissolve or diffuse readily in a Te-rich melt [Reasonable Inference]. Thus F3 is good but not excellent.

#### F2/F3 Route 3: Binary-chalcogenide two-stage route

CS analogues show layered trigonal Ge–As–Te tellurides can be assembled from binary tellurides using 700 °C/24 h plus 350 °C/two-week annealing [CS1–CS5]. This supports the idea that preformed telluride precursors can lower kinetic barriers compared with elements [CS1–CS5]. However, no CS source contains Si or Sb together in the exact target topology, and CS sources also warn of metastable phases, narrow phase windows, and difficulty isolating pure products [CS1–CS5]. Therefore the route is plausible but phase-control-limited.

#### F2/F3 Route 4: Thin-film deposition

SS2 provides a structurally similar Ge–Sb–Te layered telluride associated with vacuum evaporation and post-heating, so thin-film synthesis is plausible [SS2]. Thin-film deposition can bypass bulk diffusion limitations, but SS2 also indicates disorder/defect and reproducibility issues in related deposited phases [SS2]. There is insufficient evidence from CS and TS for SiSbTe₃ film crystallization, especially for formation of Si–Si bonded tetrahedra. Composition control is expected to be difficult because Te is volatile and the ternary stoichiometry is specific [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2/F3 Route 5: Solvothermal / microwave

SS1/TS1 demonstrates very mild and rapid synthesis of Sb₂Te₃ nanosheets at 200 °C using SbCl₃, Na₂TeO₃, hydrazine, and ethylene glycol. This gives a high F2 score because temperature and time are mild [SS1/TS1]. However, there is insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS that Si can be incorporated into a Si–Si bonded ternary telluride framework solvothermally. The likely need for post-annealing and the uncertain Si chemistry reduce F3 substantially.

#### F4: Amorphization tendency

The main amorphization risk is the strong cation mismatch: Si prefers tetrahedral Si–Si/Si–Te bonding in CrSiTe₃-like structures, whereas Sb forms SbTe₆ octahedra in Sb₂Te₃ and SnSb₂Te₄ analogues [TS4, SS1/TS1, SS3/TS2]. The target unit cell appears small, and no source indicates a >40-atom cell for this target; insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS for a large-cell amorphization trigger. The connectivity includes corner and edge sharing but not face sharing, so the full mixed-connectivity trigger is not met [TS4]. Remaining uncertainty is that Si–Sb–Te compositions may also form amorphous phase-change-like telluride glasses, especially in thin films [Reasonable Inference].

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

SiSbTe₃ is likely a thermodynamic ground-state phase because the supplied target E_hull is 0.0000 eV/atom, and several thermodynamic analogues are also on the hull [TS1, TS2, TS4, TS5]. The strongest synthesis support comes from CrSiTe₃, which shares the SiSiTe₃ tetrahedral framework and forms by a simple evacuated-tube reaction at 500 °C [TS4]. Sb–Te octahedral chemistry is strongly supported by Sb₂Te₃ and SnSb₂Te₄ analogues [SS1/TS1, SS3/TS2]. The most critical enabling factor is the existence of a close CrSiTe₃-type Si–Si telluride framework that can form under moderate sealed-ampoule conditions [TS4]. The most critical limiting factor is phase purity, especially avoiding Sb₂Te₃, residual Si, Si–Te binaries, or Te loss during long annealing [CS1–CS5, SS4/TS3, Reasonable Inference].

### SECTION 7 — Synthesis Challenges

| # | Route | Challenge | Severity | Mitigation Strategy |
|:--:|:--|:--|:--:|:--|
| 1 | All | Te volatility and stoichiometry drift | High | Use evacuated sealed ampoules, slow initial heating, small free volume, and slight Te excess; related telluride syntheses emphasize slow heating and sealed quartz ampoules [SS4/TS3, TS4]. |
| 2 | All | Competing Sb₂Te₃ formation | High | Use exact stoichiometry, long annealing, and PXRD monitoring; Sb₂Te₃ is a stable layered phase and easy to form [SS1/TS1]. |
| 3 | Route 1 / Route 3 | Slow Si incorporation and diffusion | Medium–High | Use fine Si powder, pre-reacted Si–Te precursor, repeated grinding/annealing; CrSiTe₃ required 10 days at 500 °C, indicating slow kinetics [TS4]. |
| 4 | Route 2 | Incomplete Si dissolution in melt | Medium–High | Use Te-rich prereaction, rocking/shaking, and long low-temperature anneal; melt homogenization and shaker platforms are used in layered tellurides [TS5]. |
| 5 | Route 3 | Binary precursor phase mismatch | Medium | Prepare and verify Sb₂Te₃ and Si–Te precursors before final reaction; CS analogues successfully use preformed binary tellurides but report phase-control challenges [CS1–CS5]. |
| 6 | Route 4 | Thin-film composition control, especially Te loss | High | Use Te-rich targets, Te capping/overpressure, low-temperature deposition plus anneal, and quantitative composition calibration; vacuum film analogues show phase/disorder issues [SS2]. |
| 7 | Route 5 | Lack of validated solvothermal Si incorporation | High | Treat solvothermal product as precursor only and follow with sealed Te-rich anneal; Sb₂Te₃ solvothermal synthesis is validated, but SiSbTe₃ formation is not [SS1/TS1]. |
| 8 | All | Amorphous or poorly ordered ternary telluride formation | Medium | Favor slow annealing near 350–500 °C and slow cooling; CS analogues use long 350 °C anneals to improve ordering [CS1–CS5]. |
| 9 | Route 1 / Route 2 | Ampoule rupture from Te vapor pressure | Medium | Use thick-walled silica/Pyrex ampoules, conservative fill fractions, and slow ramp through Te melting/boiling-related pressure buildup; ampoule and quenching considerations appear in CS and SS/TS data [CS1–CS5, SS4/TS3]. |
