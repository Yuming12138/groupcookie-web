### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target E_hull is 0.0500 eV/atom, placing it at the favorable boundary where the prescribed rubric gives 90–100 [target data]. CS analogues include experimentally synthesized phases with E_hull values of 0–0.037 eV/atom [CS1–CS5], while SS Pt–Ge analogues include essentially on-hull materials [SS1, SS4, SS5]. TS analogues show experimentally accessible metastable phases around 0.055–0.071 eV/atom [TS1, TS2, TS5], supporting the idea that the target is synthetically plausible but likely not deeply thermodynamically stable. Remaining uncertainty is whether Rb(PtGe)₄ competes with stable Rb–Ge, Pt–Ge, or Rb–Pt binaries, which is not directly resolved by CS/SS/TS.

#### F2 — Route 1 condition severity

Route 1 uses high but standard intermetallic temperatures, consistent with CS syntheses at 900–1050 °C and SS1 at 1273 K [CS1–CS5, SS1]. The penalties arise from T > 1000 °C, sealed-system need due to Rb volatility, multiple heating/grinding steps, and Rb air sensitivity [CS2, CS3, CS5; Reasonable Inference]. CS/SS routes do not require high pressure or exotic reactive gases [CS1–CS5, SS1], so no deductions were assigned there. Uncertainty is mainly capsule compatibility with Rb, for which the provided sources give insufficient direct Rb evidence.

#### F3 — Route 1 kinetics

Elemental solid-state synthesis is kinetically moderate to slow because Pt is refractory and CS analogues require prolonged annealing and grinding [CS2, CS3, CS5]. Pt–Ge bond distances in the target closely match those in Ca(PtGe)₂ [SS1], which improves structural transferability and reduces the reconstruction penalty. However, the target introduces Rb rather than Ca/Sr/Ba/Eu, and no source directly demonstrates Rb insertion into this Pt–Ge framework, so the kinetics score remains moderate rather than high [insufficient direct Rb evidence from CS/SS/TS].

#### F2 — Route 2 condition severity

Route 2 benefits from established arc/induction melting protocols for Pt–Ge intermetallics [SS1, SS4, SS5] and Pt–Si thermodynamic analogues [TS2, TS5]. It is more severe than Route 1 because arc/induction melting locally exceeds very high temperatures and then still requires sealed annealing [SS4, SS5; Reasonable Inference]. Rb volatility makes direct melting with Rb risky and imposes an additional sealed-anneal constraint [Reasonable Inference]. The provided sources do not include volatile alkali metals in arc-melted Pt–Ge systems, so transferability is less certain than for rare-earth/Ca analogues.

#### F3 — Route 2 kinetics

Prealloying Pt and Ge should improve reaction kinetics by reducing the diffusion distance for Pt–Ge network formation [Reasonable Inference], and SS1/SS4/SS5 show melt-derived Pt–Ge phases can be homogenized by annealing. This gives Route 2 better kinetic scoring than Route 1. The limiting kinetic issue is late-stage Rb incorporation and ordering into the tetragonal Rb(PtGe)₄ phase, which has insufficient direct evidence from CS/SS/TS. Competing Pt–Ge binaries may also persist if Rb activity is not well controlled [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 3 condition severity

HPHT synthesis is the most severe route because TS1 uses pressures up to 7.7 GPa and temperatures up to 1350 °C. The method avoids open-system Rb volatilization but requires specialized high-pressure equipment and capsule loading [TS1; Reasonable Inference]. The severity score is reduced by high pressure, high temperature, multistep capsule processing, and air-sensitive Rb handling [TS1; Reasonable Inference]. No CS/SS source indicates that pressure is required for the target, so this is a backup route rather than the primary recommendation.

#### F3 — Route 3 kinetics

TS1 shows germanide phases can form rapidly under HPHT conditions in 5–30 min, which supports reasonable diffusion under pressure and temperature. However, short HPHT dwell times may be insufficient for complete ordering of a ternary Rb–Pt–Ge phase [Reasonable Inference]. HPHT may stabilize metastable structures near the target’s E_hull, but there is insufficient direct structural evidence from CS/SS/TS that Rb(PtGe)₄ is pressure-stabilized. Thus, the kinetic score is moderate.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has a large size/chemical mismatch between Rb and the Pt–Ge framework, which triggers the radius-mismatch check [Reasonable Inference]. The target description does not establish a >40-atom unit cell, and CS/SS/TS do not provide enough direct evidence to trigger that check. The target description also lacks explicit mixed vertex/edge/face connectivity, even though some analogues show mixed sharing [SS4, SS5, TS5], so that check remains untriggered. The resulting amorphization risk is low–medium rather than high.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Rb(PtGe)₄ is likely a borderline metastable or barely accessible near-ground-state phase because its E_hull is 0.0500 eV/atom, exactly at the favorable threshold but not clearly zero [target data]. The strongest support comes from SS1, where the very similar Ca(PtGe)₂ Pt–Ge framework is synthesized from elements under Ar, and from CS1–CS5, which show Pt-containing electropositive-metal frameworks can be made by long sealed-tube annealing. TS1/TS2/TS5 further indicate that phases with similar metastability can be experimentally realized, although sometimes with specialized processing. The most critical enabling factor is strong Pt–Ge framework compatibility, reflected in matching Pt–Ge bond lengths between the target and SS1. The most critical limiting factor is Rb retention and stoichiometry control at 900–1050 °C, which is not directly addressed by CS/SS/TS and requires inferred sealed Ta/Nb containment.
