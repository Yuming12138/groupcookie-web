### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target E_hull is 0.0500 eV/atom, placing it at the boundary of the highest rubric category, so F1 = 90. TS1–TS4 show experimentally synthesized Bi-containing oxides with E_hull values around 0.058–0.084 eV/atom, indicating that slightly metastable Bi oxide phases are accessible. CS3–CS5 and SS1–SS5 include synthesized analogues at or near the hull, supporting the general plausibility of oxide formation. Remaining uncertainty is that CS/SS/TS do not directly report LiTiBiTeO6, so exact decomposition competition is unknown.

#### F2 — Route 1 hydrothermal

Hydrothermal severity is moderate because SS1/SS3/SS4 use only 230 °C for 4 days, and CS1 uses 453–503 K, far below high-temperature solid-state conditions. The route requires sealed autoclaves and toxic Te/Bi handling, but no >1000 °C treatment, no reactive gas, and no >5 GPa pressure are required. The key uncertainty is whether Ti4+ can be sufficiently mobilized under the same hydrothermal conditions; TiO2 solubility is low, so soluble Ti precursors or strong base may be needed by [Reasonable Inference]. Insufficient evidence from TS for hydrothermal target synthesis; TS is scored neutral for this route-specific method signal.

#### F3 — Route 1 hydrothermal

Kinetics are favorable because hydrothermal solution transport reduces diffusion limitations, as shown by SS1/SS3/SS4 tellurite crystals grown at 230 °C. CS1 also indicates basic aqueous hydrothermal media can crystallize Bi-containing oxyanion frameworks. Structural assembly is still only moderately certain because CS1 and SS3/SS4 mention byproducts or impurities in related systems. The Ti component remains the largest kinetic uncertainty because no hydrothermal Ti–Bi–Te–Li analogue is directly given.

#### F2 — Route 2 solid-state

Solid-state synthesis in air is experimentally common for related oxides: CS3 uses air at 973 K, SS2/SS5 use 600–800 °C, and TS1 uses 1173 K. Severity is increased by repeated grinding/refiring and likely Te/Li volatility control by [Reasonable Inference]. No special pressure or reducing atmosphere is needed, which keeps the route feasible. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS on direct LiTiBiTeO6 volatility, so volatility handling is an inferred risk.

#### F3 — Route 2 solid-state

Kinetics are moderate to poor because TiO2 is refractory and the target requires complete reconstruction into a low-symmetry P1 structure. SS2, SS5, and TS1 explicitly required multiple regrinding/refiring steps, indicating sluggish homogenization or phase purification in related Bi oxides. CS3 shows Te-containing oxides can form by heating at 973 K for 6 h, which partially offsets this risk. Remaining uncertainty is whether the target decomposes or loses Te/Li before full cation ordering is achieved.

#### F2 — Route 3 sol–gel/soft chemistry

CS4/CS5 support multistep citrate-resin synthesis with drying, decomposition, and final firing, so the process is operationally more complex than direct solid-state synthesis. The recommended target route avoids the reducing H2/Ar atmospheres used in CS4/CS5 because the target likely requires Ti4+, Bi3+, and Te4+, but this atmospheric choice is a [Reasonable Inference]. Te/Bi toxicity and volatile-component control remain severity penalties. Insufficient evidence from SS/TS for sol–gel synthesis of this exact structural family, so their route-specific signal is neutral.

#### F3 — Route 3 sol–gel/soft chemistry

Kinetics are better than conventional solid-state because CS4/CS5 show citrate routes produce homogeneous multication precursors. High precursor activity should reduce diffusion lengths and lower firing time/temperature. Structural ordering remains uncertain because the target is a four-cation triclinic oxide rather than the perovskite-related phases in CS4/CS5. The main uncertainty is maintaining Te(IV) and Li stoichiometry during organic burnout and calcination.

#### F2 — Route 4 flux growth

Flux growth is moderate in severity: TS4 reports Bi2O3 flux growth after 500 °C pre-sintering, while TS3 shows sealed-tube flux growth with controlled cooling. The method avoids very high temperature and high pressure but adds flux handling, slow cooling, and volatile Te control. Toxic Bi/Te compounds and flux separation increase practical difficulty. Insufficient evidence from CS for flux growth of Li–Ti–Bi–Te oxides, so CS route signal is neutral.

#### F3 — Route 4 flux growth

Flux transport improves kinetics by dissolving components and reducing solid-state diffusion limitations, consistent with TS4 and TS3. A Bi2O3/TeO2-rich flux is chemically compatible with the target’s Bi–O and Te–O substructure by [Reasonable Inference]. However, the same flux may bias crystallization toward Bi–Te binary or ternary impurity phases, analogous to impurity/byproduct issues in SS3/SS4 and phase-purity challenges in SS2/SS5. Composition control, especially Li incorporation, is the main uncertainty.

#### F2 — Route 5 high-pressure fallback

TS5 demonstrates that Te oxides can be synthesized under 3.5 GPa and 1100 °C in sealed Pt capsules, but this is much more demanding than CS/SS ambient or hydrothermal routes. Under the scoring table, the pressure is below the >5 GPa penalty threshold, but sealed-capsule operation and controlled atmosphere/containment still reduce F2. This route is therefore a fallback rather than first choice. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS that high pressure is required for the target; those source signals weigh against needing this route.

#### F3 — Route 5 high-pressure fallback

High pressure and temperature can accelerate reaction and stabilize dense metastable oxides, as TS5 shows for NaU(TeO4)2. However, TS5 is chemically less similar because it contains U and Na rather than Ti/Bi/Li, so transferability to LiTiBiTeO6 is limited. The route may help if ambient-pressure synthesis yields competing phases, but it can also access different high-pressure polymorphs. The uncertainty is high because no CS/SS/TS source reports high-pressure synthesis of a direct Li–Ti–Bi–Te analogue.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target combines small Li+, strongly bonded Ti4+, lone-pair-active Bi3+, and Te(IV), producing very different coordination environments; this supports C1 and is evident from the target structural description and related mixed-polyhedral analogues in CS1/SS2/SS5. The primitive target description has only one Li, Ti, Bi, Te, and six O sites, so C2 is not triggered; insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS suggests a >40 atom cell. Mixed corner/edge sharing is explicitly present in the target and common in CS1, SS2, and TS4, so C3 is triggered. The resulting medium amorphization/kinetic-trapping risk is most relevant for low-temperature sol–gel and flux routes.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

LiTiBiTeO6 is likely a low-energy metastable or near-ground-state phase rather than an obviously unstable compound, because its E_hull is exactly 0.0500 eV/atom and TS analogues show synthesized Bi oxides can exist at comparable hull energies. The strongest synthesis support comes from SS1/SS3/SS4, which demonstrate hydrothermal crystallization of Te(IV) oxides, and from CS1, which demonstrates basic hydrothermal growth of Bi-containing oxyanion crystals. The most critical enabling factor is solution or flux transport, because it can overcome slow Ti/Bi/Te/Li solid-state diffusion and help assemble low-symmetry polyhedral networks. The most critical limiting factor is phase selectivity: Li, Bi, Ti, and Te can form many competing binary/ternary oxides, and TeO2/Li volatility or Te redox changes can shift stoichiometry. Overall, hydrothermal synthesis followed by sol–gel/flux backup is the most plausible strategy.
