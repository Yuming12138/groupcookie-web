### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target’s E_hull = 0.0500 eV/atom places it at the favorable edge of the rubric’s ≤0.05 eV/atom category. CS provides strong support because several chemically similar W-containing perovskite-derived oxides have E_hull = 0 or near-zero and were experimentally synthesized. TS supports feasibility because thermodynamic analogues around 0.055–0.084 eV/atom were synthesized, showing that slightly metastable oxides can be accessible; SS is mixed because one high-similarity oxide required fused-salt electrolysis and had a higher hull energy. The main uncertainty is whether the target’s exact P1 W–Ir arrangement is the thermodynamically preferred polymorph or a metastable ordered variant.

#### F2 — Route 1

CS directly supports the Route 1 condition window: soft-chemistry W perovskites were made at 850–900 °C under H2/Ar or H2/N2 after resin decomposition. The route avoids high pressure and temperatures above 1000 °C, but it incurs penalties for controlled atmosphere and multi-step gel processing. SS gives insufficient direct signal for citrate synthesis, and TS gives insufficient direct synthesis-route signal, so CS is weighted most heavily. The remaining uncertainty is whether H2 atmospheres that work for Fe/W analogues will over-reduce Ir or W in the target.

#### F3 — Route 1

CS strongly supports high precursor activity because the citric acid route produces homogeneous cation-containing resins. The diffusion score is still limited because W and Ir are refractory cations, and no CS/SS/TS source directly demonstrates W–Ir ordering by sol-gel. Structural reconstruction is moderately favorable because CS analogues are W perovskite-derived oxides, but the target’s P1 symmetry and four-coordinate Ir environment are not directly represented. Thus Route 1 has the best kinetic balance but is not trivial.

#### F2 — Route 2

CS supports a straightforward ceramic route for W perovskite-derived oxides, including NaLaMgWO6 at 1050 °C/16 h in air and broader 800–1200 °C ceramic synthesis windows. The main severity penalty is temperature above 1000 °C; otherwise, the route uses standard furnace conditions and ambient pressure. SS and TS provide general support for high-temperature solid-state oxide synthesis but not direct SrLaWIrO6 chemistry. The uncertainty is whether air firing stabilizes the correct Ir oxidation state and avoids competing tungstates.

#### F3 — Route 2

The kinetic score is lower because oxide/carbonate precursors are less reactive than citrate gels, as implied by CS comparing ceramic and soft-chemistry approaches. W/Ir diffusion and B-site ordering are likely slow, and repeated grinding/firing may be needed. CS supports formation of W double perovskite-related phases by ceramics, but no source proves the target’s specific P1 W–Ir order forms this way. The route is experimentally simple but kinetically less favorable.

#### F2 — Route 3

SS and TS show that sealed-tube or vacuum/Ar solid-state synthesis is a viable route for low-symmetry La–Re/Mo oxides at 700–1050 °C. The penalties arise from vacuum or sealed oxygen-potential control, possible internal pressure/volatile oxygen issues, and multi-step preparation. CS gives insufficient direct evidence for sealed W–Ir synthesis but does show atmosphere control can be important. The route is therefore more severe than open ceramic firing.

#### F3 — Route 3

SS supports that long sealed-tube reactions can form complex low-symmetry transition-metal oxides, but the precursors are still dense oxides/metals with limited reactivity. Oxygen-buffered Ir/W chemistry may help phase selection but does not remove diffusion barriers. TS and SS analogues often require 24–48 h at high temperature, indicating moderate-to-slow kinetics. The route is useful for redox control but not kinetically ideal.

#### F2 — Route 4

TS provides the only strong basis for high-pressure synthesis, particularly dense Sr/Ba 5d perovskite-like oxides made under multianvil conditions. This route is heavily penalized for pressure >5 GPa, high temperature, and multistep processing. CS and SS provide insufficient evidence that high pressure is required for W–Ir target formation. It is a fallback route rather than a preferred route.

#### F3 — Route 4

High pressure can promote dense perovskite frameworks, as TS Os oxide examples show, but short high-pressure dwell times and refractory W/Ir cations limit reaction kinetics. A prereacted precursor would improve homogeneity, but this is a [Reasonable Inference] rather than directly supported for SrLaWIrO6. Structural reconstruction may be aided if the target is a dense metastable polymorph. The kinetic score is moderate but does not offset equipment severity.

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

The target contains large A-site cations and much smaller transition-metal cations, so the ionic-radius contrast check is triggered by physicochemical reasoning, consistent with CS/SS/TS perovskite-derived analogues. The target description does not indicate a >40 atom cell, so C2 is not triggered. The structure includes corner- and edge-sharing motifs but no face-sharing, so the full mixed vertex/edge/face criterion is not triggered. The amorphization risk is therefore low-to-medium, mainly due to cation size/coordination mismatch rather than extreme structural complexity.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

SrLaWIrO6 is likely a near-threshold metastable or marginally stable oxide rather than a deeply stable ground-state phase, because its E_hull is exactly 0.0500 eV/atom. CS analogues give the strongest support: W-containing perovskite-derived oxides with La/rare-earth A-site chemistry are experimentally accessible by both soft-chemistry and ceramic routes. SS and TS analogues support the broader idea that low-symmetry transition-metal oxides near this energy range can be synthesized, but they are less chemically direct because many involve Re, Mo, Tc, or Os rather than Ir/W. The most critical enabling factor is homogeneous cation mixing, best provided by the citrate route demonstrated in CS. The most critical limiting factor is redox/phase control of Ir and W, especially avoiding Ir metal, SrWO4, La tungstates, or incorrect perovskite polymorphs.
