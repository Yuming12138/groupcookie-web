### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target is calculated at E_hull = 0.0000 eV/atom, placing it in the strongest stability bin [Target]. Several chemically and thermodynamically related chalcogenide-halides are also reported on or extremely near the hull, including NbSe₂Cl₂, Nb₃Se₅Cl₇, CrSBr, NbSeCl₃, and TS analogues [CS2–CS5; TS1–TS5]. CS and TS therefore provide strong positive evidence; SS provides supportive but partly indirect evidence because some structurally similar phases are chemically different [SS1–SS5].

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 uses moderate temperature because the isostructural Nb(SBr)₂ analogue forms at 500 °C [CS1]. The score is reduced for evacuated/sealed-tube operation and volatile/toxic halogen and sulfur sources, consistent with sealed halogen syntheses in CS1 and CS4 [CS1; CS4]. No high pressure or >1000 °C treatment is required, so the severity remains moderate rather than severe [CS1].

#### F3 — Route 1

The kinetic advantage is the very close structural match to Nb(SBr)₂, which has the same layered M(SX)₂ motif and forms in 2 days at 500 °C [CS1]. The main kinetic penalty is refractory Zr metal and possible Zr oxide passivation [Reasonable Inference]. Overall, halogen-assisted vapor chemistry should improve transport relative to conventional refractory solid-state synthesis [CS1; CS4].

#### F2 — Route 2

CVT conditions are more operationally demanding because NbX₂Y₂ analogues require evacuated sealed tubes, a small thermal gradient, and 3–4 weeks of reaction time [CS2]. The temperature is still moderate, around 400–515 °C for CS2 systems, so there is no penalty for extreme temperature [CS2]. The use of volatile halides and controlled vacuum/sealed ampoules drives the F2 score below Route 1 [CS2; CS4].

#### F3 — Route 2

CVT improves kinetics by forming mobile halide/chalcogenide vapor species, as shown by NbX₂Y₂ and CrSBr syntheses [CS2; CS4]. However, long transport times and phase-window sensitivity are documented for related compounds [CS2], so crystal growth is not expected to be fast. The pathway is excellent for single crystals but less ideal for rapid bulk phase screening [CS2; CS4].

#### F2 — Route 3

Binary-precursor synthesis uses reactive ZrCl₄-like chemistry by analogy with NbCl₄/NbCl₅ routes [CS3; CS5]. Its severity is lowered by moderate temperatures around 480–550 °C, matching Nb–Se–Cl sealed-tube syntheses [CS3; CS5]. Penalties arise from air-sensitive volatile chlorides, evacuated ampoules, and possible extra precursor-preparation/regrinding steps [CS5; Reasonable Inference].

#### F3 — Route 3

Using ZrS₂ could reduce the amount of structural reconstruction compared with starting from all elements [Reasonable Inference]. However, the NbSeCl₃ analogue was obtained as a side product in small amount with inhomogeneous products [CS5], and Nb₃Se₅Cl₇ showed poor reproducibility and narrow stability [CS3]. Therefore the kinetic score is lower than Route 1 despite plausible precursor reactivity [CS3; CS5].

#### F2 — Route 4

Flux synthesis avoids very high temperatures but adds multiple handling steps: flux drying, sealed-tube loading, controlled cooling, and flux removal, as in TS5 [TS5]. It also requires vacuum/sealed silica tubes and potentially air-sensitive chlorides such as ZrCl₄, paralleling chloride solid-state syntheses [SS5; TS3]. The lack of direct CS evidence for M(SCl)₂ flux growth adds uncertainty but does not directly alter F2 [insufficient direct evidence from CS].

#### F3 — Route 4

Molten halide flux can substantially improve mass transport and crystal growth, as shown for Ta–Ge–S compounds in chloride fluxes [TS5]. The main uncertainty is phase selectivity: fluxes may stabilize alkali-containing zirconium chlorides or other Zr–S–Cl products rather than neutral Zr(SCl)₂ sheets [Reasonable Inference]. Because no CS example directly shows flux synthesis of the target structure type, F3 is moderate [insufficient direct evidence from CS].

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

The target has one cation and a relatively simple layered structure, so cation size mismatch and large-cell disorder are not expected to drive amorphization [Target; CS1]. The target description does not report complex coexistence of vertex-, edge-, and face-sharing polyhedra, unlike some SS/TS cluster or framework analogues [Target; SS1; SS5]. Remaining uncertainty is the exact crystallographic atom count and disorder tendency under non-equilibrium cooling, but the close crystalline analogue in CS1 supports low amorphization risk [CS1].

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Zr(SCl)₂ is likely a thermodynamic ground-state phase because its calculated energy above hull is 0.0000 eV/atom [Target]. The strongest support comes from the chemically and structurally close Nb(SBr)₂ analogue, which has the same C2/m layered M(SX)₂ architecture and was synthesized at 500 °C in a sealed tube [CS1]. Additional support comes from related Nb–Se–Cl and Cr–S–Br chalcogenide-halide syntheses using sealed-tube or CVT methods [CS2; CS4]. The most critical enabling factor is halogen-assisted transport/reactivity, which allows layered chalcogenide-halides to form at moderate temperatures [CS1; CS2]. The main limiting factor is controlling chlorine and sulfur chemical potentials to avoid volatile loss, overchlorination, or competing Zr chloride/sulfide phases [CS3; CS5; Reasonable Inference].
