### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0500 eV/atom, exactly at the high-synthesizability threshold specified in the prompt. Similar thermodynamic cases were synthesized, including Sr₂YSbO₆ with E_hull ≈ 0.0519 eV/atom [TS1] and Ca₂LaSbO₆ with E_hull ≈ 0.0504 eV/atom [TS2]. This suggests the target is likely accessible, although possibly slightly metastable depending on temperature, oxygen chemical potential, and structural distortion. Remaining uncertainty comes from the target being triclinic P-1 while the direct references include monoclinic and cubic descriptions [CS1; CS2; SS1; TS1].

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 uses high-temperature ceramic processing up to 1275 °C, which triggers the >1000 °C penalty [CS1; SS1]. It also requires multiple heating/regrinding steps [CS1; SS1], and Sb₂O₃ introduces a toxicity concern by chemical knowledge **[Reasonable Inference]**. Air atmosphere and ambient pressure reduce severity, and there is insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS that sealed ampoules are required.

#### F3 — Route 1

Kinetics are moderate because the route uses oxide/carbonate powders and refractory Y₂O₃, yet the same composition was successfully synthesized [CS1; SS1]. Repeated regrinding and long high-temperature holds imply diffusion and B-site ordering are not instantaneous [CS1]. Structural reconstruction is favorable because the product is the same double-perovskite composition and very similar tilted octahedral network [CS1; SS1].

#### F2 — Route 2

Route 2 uses slightly lower temperature, 1090–1100 °C, but still exceeds 1000 °C [CS2; TS1]. It is multi-step, with mixing, pelletizing, calcining, regrinding, pressing, and 135 h sintering [CS2; TS1]. SrO handling sensitivity and Sb₂O₃ toxicity are **[Reasonable Inference]**, while air atmosphere and ambient pressure are directly supported [CS2; TS1].

#### F3 — Route 2

The 135 h sinter indicates slower kinetics than Route 1 despite direct same-composition success [CS2; TS1]. The use of SrO may improve Sr reactivity, but Y₂O₃ remains refractory and cation ordering requires long annealing [CS2; TS1]. The reported cubic Fm-3m structure lacks the target’s octahedral tilts [SS5; TS1], so producing the triclinic distortion may require additional thermal-history control **[Reasonable Inference]**.

#### F2 — Route 3

Route 3 is more severe because analogous protocols reach 1400 °C or 1770 K/~1497 °C [CS3; CS4; SS2–SS4; TS2]. It also requires repeated grinding and multiple heating cycles [CS3; CS4; SS4]. The benefit is that it remains a standard air/ambient-pressure ceramic process with common crucibles and no special atmosphere [CS3; CS4; TS2].

#### F3 — Route 3

Kinetics are aided by the higher firing temperature, and many Sr₂LnSbO₆ analogues form successfully as single phases or near-single phases [SS2–SS4]. However, the starting materials are still solid carbonates/oxides, so precursor activity is only moderate [CS3; CS4]. Some impurity formation is reported for Sr₂SmSbO₆ [SS4], so phase purity is not guaranteed.

#### F2 — Route 4

Route 4 is not directly reported in CS/SS/TS; the severity assessment is therefore based on **[Reasonable Inference]**. It likely still needs a final anneal above 1000 °C to form the ordered perovskite, consistent with the ceramic analogues [CS1; TS1]. It avoids high pressure and special atmospheres, but adds chemical-processing steps and antimony precursor hazards **[Reasonable Inference]**.

#### F3 — Route 4

The sol–gel/Pechini approach should improve precursor activity and reduce diffusion length relative to oxide/carbonate mixing **[Reasonable Inference]**. This directly addresses the repeated regrinding and long sintering seen in direct ceramic routes [CS1; CS2; TS1]. The uncertainty is significant because CS/SS/TS provide insufficient direct evidence for sol–gel crystallization of Sr₂YSbO₆, and B-site ordering may still require prolonged annealing.

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

The main amorphization risk is the large difference in cation sizes and bonding environments, visible from the target’s Sr–O, Y–O, and Sb–O bond-length ranges and mirrored in CS1/SS1. The structure is nevertheless a relatively conventional corner-sharing double perovskite, which lowers amorphization risk compared with mixed-connectivity frameworks [CS1; SS1]. There is insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS that the target has >40 atoms per cell or extensive edge/face-sharing connectivity, so only one amorphization trigger is counted.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Sr₂YSbO₆ is likely synthesizable, but the target triclinic P-1 polymorph should be viewed as a low-energy distorted or metastable variant rather than an unequivocal unique ground-state phase, because CS/SS/TS include monoclinic and cubic Sr₂YSbO₆ descriptions [CS1; CS2; SS1; SS5; TS1]. The chemical, structural, and thermodynamic analogues strongly support formation of the Sr₂YSbO₆ double-perovskite framework by air-fired solid-state methods [CS1; CS2; SS1; TS1]. The most critical enabling factor is the robust tolerance of Sr₂MSbO₆/Sr₂LnSbO₆ double perovskites to conventional high-temperature air synthesis [CS1; SS2–SS4]. The most critical limiting factor is slow cation diffusion/B-site ordering, evidenced by repeated regrinding, stepwise firing, and sintering times up to 135 h [CS1; CS2; TS1]. Control of Sb stoichiometry and oxidation state is an additional practical concern, although direct evidence for sealed-system necessity is insufficient from CS/SS/TS.
