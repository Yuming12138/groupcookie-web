### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0500 eV/atom, placing it exactly at the high-synthesizability boundary specified in the prompt. TS analogues with similar thermodynamic distances include phases at E_hull ≈0.056–0.078 eV/atom that have reported syntheses, such as RbPrNb2O7 by ceramic reaction and H2Ti3O7 by ion exchange (TS-3, TS-4). However, TS-1 also shows a Na–Ta oxide at E_hull ≈0.067 eV/atom formed with phase mixture, so near-hull does not guarantee single-phase synthesis. Insufficient evidence from CS for target-specific hull tolerance because most CS compounds are stable at E_hull = 0.

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 is mild because CS-2 synthesized the closest Na–Mg–Te analogue in air at 773 K then 973 K for 6 h. TS-1/SS-3 indicate Ta incorporation may require higher temperature near 1000 °C, but still in air and ambient pressure. The main severity penalties are multi-step decomposition/firing and Te precursor toxicity [Reasonable Inference]. Insufficient evidence from SS for this exact nitrate/hydroxide Na–Mg–Ta–Te route.

#### F3 — Route 1

The nitrate/hydroxide route has high precursor activity because CS-2 used NaNO3, Mg(OH)2, and Te(OH)6. Kinetics are limited by Ta2O5, since TS-1/SS-3 use Ta2O5 for Na–Ta oxide formation and report competing Na3TaO4 in TS-1. Structural reconstruction is moderate because the target P1 MgO4/TaO5/TeO4 network differs from the perovskite-derived CS-2 analogue. Insufficient evidence from TS for Te-containing Ta oxide kinetics.

#### F2 — Route 2

The ceramic route is experimentally routine, supported by CS-1/CS-5 and TS-1/SS-3 using standard furnaces, air/forming gas, and oxide/carbonate precursors. It is more severe than Route 1 because CS-1/CS-5 allow 800–1200 °C and TS-1 uses ~1000 °C, so Na/Te volatility and refractory Ta2O5 become concerns. No high pressure or specialized equipment is indicated by CS/TS. Insufficient evidence from CS for Te4+ stability under prolonged high-temperature ceramic firing.

#### F3 — Route 2

The oxide/carbonate route has slower kinetics because MgO and Ta2O5 are less reactive than nitrates or sol-gel powders [Reasonable Inference], and TS-1 shows a Na–Ta solid-state reaction can yield a phase mixture. CS-1/CS-5 ceramic syntheses are successful for related Na complex oxides but often require high temperatures and careful conditions. Structural reconstruction from binary oxides to triclinic P1 mixed coordination is substantial, so repeated grindings are likely. SS-3 supports Ta oxide formation but not the exact Te/Mg chemistry.

#### F2 — Route 3

The soft-chemistry route has moderate severity: CS-3/CS-4 use resin formation, drying at 120 °C, decomposition at 600 °C, and final heating at 850–900 °C. The target probably does not require H2/Ar or H2/N2 because the CS-3/CS-4 reducing atmosphere was tied to Fe/W redox chemistry, while the target contains Ta5+ and Te likely as Te4+ [Reasonable Inference]. The process is multi-step and may involve toxic Te compounds or moisture-sensitive Ta alkoxide. Insufficient evidence from CS that a soluble Ta precursor will behave identically to the W precursor in CS-3/CS-4.

#### F3 — Route 3

The soft-chemistry route scores best kinetically because CS-3/CS-4 explicitly used citric-acid resins to obtain homogeneous cation distributions. This should reduce diffusion lengths for Mg/Ta/Te/Na and help avoid incomplete reaction with refractory Ta2O5 [Reasonable Inference]. The final structure still requires crystallization into a low-symmetry P1 phase, so the structural reconstruction score is not maximal. TS provides no direct soft-chemistry Ta–Te analogue, so uncertainty remains.

#### F2 — Route 4

Flux growth is moderately severe because SS-2 required staged heating and extremely slow cooling, while TS-2 used melt growth/quenching for thermodynamically related oxides. No high pressure is required, but sealed or covered conditions may be needed to suppress Na/Te loss [Reasonable Inference]. Flux removal and toxic Te handling add complexity. Insufficient evidence from CS for flux synthesis of Na–Mg–Ta–Te oxides.

#### F3 — Route 4

Flux improves kinetics through liquid-phase transport, supported by SS-2 for Mg-containing oxide crystal growth. It may also help form low-symmetry crystals by slow cooling, as in SS-2. However, Ta2O5 solubility, Te volatility, and Na partitioning in the flux are unknown [Reasonable Inference]. TS-2 supports melt-based growth in related thermodynamic space but does not provide target-specific composition control.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target contains cations with very different sizes and preferred coordinations, triggering the ionic-radius mismatch criterion [Reasonable Inference from the target’s Na 5-coordinate, Mg tetrahedral, Ta trigonal-bipyramidal, and Te see-saw environments]. The target description reports corner and edge sharing between MgO4 and TaO5 units, but no face sharing, so the full mixed-connectivity trigger is not met. The exact unit-cell atom count is not provided; therefore, insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS supports triggering the >40 atom criterion. Overall amorphization risk is low–medium rather than high.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

NaMgTaTeO6 is likely a near-ground-state or mildly metastable phase because its E_hull is 0.0500 eV/atom, exactly at the prompt’s high-synthesizability threshold. The CS data strongly support Na–Mg–Te oxide synthesis under mild air firing, especially CS-2, while TS/SS data support Na–Ta oxide formation from Ta2O5 but warn of competing tantalate phases. The most critical enabling factor is homogeneous precursor mixing, best achieved by citrate/sol-gel or nitrate/hydroxide chemistry, because Ta2O5/MgO diffusion would otherwise be slow. The most critical limiting factor is simultaneous control of Na volatility, Te oxidation/volatility, and Ta-containing competing phases. Overall, synthesis is plausible, with the best chance via a soft-chemistry route followed by moderate-temperature crystallization.
