### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0000 eV/atom, so it receives the maximum thermodynamic score under the required rubric. This is reinforced by chemically or thermodynamically related stable analogues such as Y₂NCl₃ (CS-1), La₂PBr₂ (CS-3), LaSeCl (CS-4), YS (TS-1), and La₂TeI₂ (TS-5). SS gives a mixed signal because several structurally similar rare-earth chlorides are above hull, e.g., SmCl₂ and NdCl₂ entries are metastable in SS-1 and SS-3; therefore SS is treated as less decisive for thermodynamics. Remaining uncertainty is mainly that DFT hull stability does not guarantee a wide synthesis window against YS/Y₂S₃/YCl₃ or polytypic layered variants.

#### F2 — Route 1: sealed-ampoule solid-state reaction

The condition severity is moderate-to-high because CS-1, CS-3, CS-4, and TS-5 all require sealed ampoules, high temperatures around 800–900+ °C, and inert or evacuated environments. SS analogues also repeatedly use argon gloveboxes, Ta ampoules, and arc welding for rare-earth chlorides (SS-1, SS-3, SS-5). The main deductions arise from controlled atmosphere, volatile/moisture-sensitive YCl₃, multi-step handling, and air-sensitive Y metal/YCl₃. There is insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS that high pressure is needed, so no high-pressure penalty is applied.

#### F3 — Route 1: sealed-ampoule solid-state reaction

Kinetics are moderate because the route uses refractory Y–S precursors, and YS itself required high temperature when prepared from Y + Y₂S₃ (SS-2/TS-1). However, halide-containing analogues form at 800–950 °C in sealed ampoules (CS-1, CS-4, TS-5), implying that YCl₃ or halide-rich intermediates can improve transport. The structural reconstruction burden is reduced by the exact layered P-3m1 analogue La₂PBr₂ (CS-3) and related La₂TeI₂ (TS-5), but long anneals and side phases in the analogues reduce confidence. Remaining uncertainty is whether Y₂SCl₂ nucleates directly or competes strongly with YS and YCl₃-derived binaries.

#### F2 — Route 2: chloride-flux / self-flux growth

The flux route still requires sealed, rigorously dry ampoules because rare-earth halide/chalcogenide materials are air- or moisture-sensitive in CS-1 and CS-2, and Ta/Ar ampoules are used for related layered phases in CS-3 and TS-5. It avoids >1000 °C conditions in the proposed window, but volatile YCl₃ flux and multistep crystal growth/separation justify the same condition-severity deductions as Route 1. SS provides supporting evidence for sealed Ta chloride chemistry but insufficient direct evidence for Y–S–Cl flux growth. The route is operationally moderate rather than easy because molten YCl₃ is corrosive and must be isolated from moisture.

#### F3 — Route 2: chloride-flux / self-flux growth

Kinetically, Route 2 is the best route because molten halide flux should accelerate dissolution, diffusion, and recrystallization. CS-2 directly shows a rare-earth sulfide halide crystallizing in a molten rare-earth halide flux, while TS-5 shows a layered rare-earth chalcogenide halide synthesized from a rare-earth trihalide/metal/chalcogen mixture. The structural target is well supported by layered M₂ZX₂ analogues in CS-3 and TS-5. Remaining uncertainty is that excess flux may stabilize chloride-rich side phases or dissolve/etch the desired product.

#### F2 — Route 3: chemical vapor transport

CVT receives substantial severity deductions because it requires evacuated sealed ampoules, volatile I₂ or halide transport agents, and toxic/corrosive halogen chemistry, as supported by TS-4. CS and SS do not provide direct CVT evidence for Y₂SCl₂, so transferability is uncertain. The temperature is not necessarily extreme, and no high pressure is required. The route is therefore feasible as a crystal-growth exploration but not the primary synthesis route.

#### F3 — Route 3: chemical vapor transport

The kinetics benefit from vapor-phase mass transport, as TS-4 demonstrates for Sc sulfide using I₂ at 950 °C. However, there is insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS that Y, S, and Cl transport congruently to preserve Y₂SCl₂ stoichiometry. Structural reconstruction is uncertain because the directly isostructural analogues in CS-3 and TS-5 were made by solid-state reactions, not CVT. The main uncertainty is whether iodine or chlorine chemical potentials produce iodide-substituted phases, YS, or YCl₃-rich deposits.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target is a simple single-cation crystalline layered compound, so the cation-size disorder check is not triggered. The reported P-3m1 structure has a small formula-unit cell and only edge/corner sharing, with no face-sharing coexistence, so the amorphization risk is low. CS-3 and TS-5 show that closely related layered M₂ZX₂ structures can crystallize as ordered solids, supporting a high F4 score. Remaining uncertainty is not amorphization but stacking disorder/polytypism, which is reported for related layered phases in CS-3.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Y₂SCl₂ is likely a true ground-state phase because its reported energy above hull is 0.0000 eV/atom, and several chemically related rare-earth halide/chalcogenide analogues are also stable or synthesizable (CS-1, CS-3, CS-4, TS-5). The strongest analogue support comes from the isostructural layered M₂ZX₂ compounds La₂PBr₂ (CS-3) and La₂TeI₂ (TS-5), which share the same motif of rare-earth octahedra bonded to a pnictide/chalcogenide and halide. The most critical enabling factor is the use of sealed Ta/Nb ampoules with halide-rich chemistry, which controls volatile chloride activity and promotes transport. The most critical limiting factor is phase control: analogues report moisture sensitivity, impurity phases, polytypism, and long annealing requirements (CS-1, CS-2, CS-3, TS-5). Overall, the target is synthetically plausible, with the best chance via chloride-flux or halide-assisted sealed-ampoule synthesis.
