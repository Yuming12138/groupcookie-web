### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0500 eV/atom, placing it at the ≤0.05 eV/atom threshold for a high thermodynamic score. CS analogues include stable or near-stable materials, such as Ca3ScCoO6 at 0.0 eV/atom (CS-2), NaCa3RuO6 at 0.0 eV/atom (CS-5), and NaFeTiO4 at ~0.060 eV/atom (CS-3), supporting accessibility of nearby metastable Na/Ca oxides. TS analogues around 0.059–0.074 eV/atom were synthesized, but sometimes required flux, sealed tubes, or high pressure (TS-1 to TS-4), so the score is high but not perfect.

#### F2/F3 — Route 1: Solid-state

F2 is favorable because CS-3 and CS-5 demonstrate ambient-pressure oxide synthesis near 950–1000 °C using standard grinding/firing, with no high pressure. The deduction comes from the required multi-step nature, since both CS-3 and CS-5 use regrinding/reheating or staged firing. F3 is lower because solid-state formation from carbonates/oxides requires full structural reconstruction, and the target’s O–O bonds are more consistent with peroxide chemistry seen in SS-1/SS-3 than with high-temperature oxide ceramic routes.

#### F2/F3 — Route 2: Sol-gel

F2 is high because CS-2 shows citrate-gel synthesis using standard temperatures and no high pressure, though gelation, drying, combustion, and annealing make it multi-step. F3 is better than Route 1 because soluble nitrate/acetate/citrate precursors provide high precursor activity, consistent with CS-2. Remaining uncertainty is whether a sol-gel-derived oxide anneal can retain or form the target O–O units, since SS peroxide analogues were prepared under low-temperature peroxide-rich conditions rather than ordinary calcination.

#### F2/F3 — Route 3: Alkaline peroxide solution/hydrothermal

F2 is high because SS-1 and SS-3 demonstrate peroxide compounds formed at boiling/low-temperature or cooling-crystallization conditions, without high pressure. F3 is highest because solution chemistry bypasses slow solid-state diffusion and directly supplies peroxide species through H2O2, as shown in SS-1, SS-2, and SS-3. The uncertainty is compositional: SS analogues are simpler peroxide salts, while the target requires simultaneous Na, Ca, and Al incorporation.

#### F2/F3 — Route 4: High-pressure sealed capsule

F2 is poor because TS-2, TS-4, and CS-4 show high-pressure oxide synthesis requiring multi-GPa equipment, sealed capsules, and quenching. F3 is moderate because high pressure can stabilize metastable dense phases and reduce volatilization, but TS-2 and TS-4 also show phase-window and impurity challenges. This route is a fallback if ambient-pressure peroxide routes fail.

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

The target contains cations with substantially different sizes, which triggers C1 by ionic-radius reasoning **[Reasonable Inference]**. The target description shows corner and edge sharing, but not face sharing, so C3 is not triggered; insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS supports a >40-atom target cell, so C2 is neutral/not triggered. The amorphization risk is therefore low–medium rather than high.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Na2CaAlO6 is likely a borderline stable or mildly metastable phase because its E_hull is 0.0500 eV/atom. Chemically similar Na/Ca oxides are synthesizable by standard ceramic or sol-gel routes (CS-2, CS-3, CS-5), but the target’s short O–O contacts make peroxide-like low-temperature chemistry more compelling, as supported by SS-1 to SS-3. The most important enabling factor is the availability of alkaline peroxide solution routes that can generate O–O-containing oxides at low temperature. The most important limiting factor is phase selectivity: Na, Ca, and Al may instead form hydroxides, aluminates, carbonates, or simple peroxides unless pH, CO2 exclusion, and H2O2 activity are tightly controlled. If the intended structure is not peroxide-stabilized, then sol-gel or ceramic routes become more plausible, but O–O retention would remain questionable.
