### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0500 eV/atom, placing it at the favorable boundary for likely synthesizability by the provided rubric. CS analogues include stable or nearly stable Re/Os double perovskites, including Sr2LiReO6 at E_hull = 0 and 0.0108 eV/atom and Ca2ScOsO6 at E_hull = 0 (CS-1, CS-2, CS-3). SS analogues also include stable Ba2NaOsO6 and Na3OsO5 at E_hull = 0 (SS-4, SS-5). TS analogues at E_hull ≈0.06–0.08 eV/atom are reported synthesized, so the target’s 0.05 eV/atom metastability is not prohibitive (TS-1 to TS-5).

#### F2 — Route 1 condition severity

Route 1 is penalized because CS-3 uses vacuum-sealed silica tubes, glovebox handling, and controlled oxidation with PbO2 at 900–950 °C. SS-5 shows Na–Os oxides involve reactive Na2O2 and oxygen flow, and reports hygroscopic/air-sensitive behavior, supporting the toxic/air-sensitive and volatile-risk deductions. CS-1/CS-2 show simpler ceramic routes at 600–800 °C, but those are Re/Sr/Li systems and do not fully capture Os(VII)/Na volatility, so they are weighted less for condition severity. Remaining uncertainty is whether Os(VII) can be stabilized without over-forming volatile OsO4.

#### F3 — Route 1 kinetics

Solid-state diffusion is expected to be slow because Y2O3 and OsO2/Os are refractory; CS-3 required two 24 h high-temperature steps for Ca2ScOsO6. CS-1/CS-2 show that double perovskite ceramics can form with regrinding at 600–800 °C, supporting structural feasibility but also indicating repeated processing. The target’s tilted corner-sharing double-perovskite topology is close to CS-3, improving the structural-reconstruction score. The largest uncertainty is B-site ordering and complete reaction of Y2O3 under conditions that preserve Na.

#### F2 — Route 2 condition severity

Route 2 scores best because SS-4, TS-3, and TS-5 demonstrate Os/Ir oxide flux growth at only 600–700 °C, avoiding high-pressure and >1000 °C penalties. The main deductions arise from corrosive/reactive hydroxide flux processing, possible sealed-tube containment, Os toxicity, and product sensitivity; SS-5 explicitly reports Na–Os oxide hygroscopicity and OsO4-related concerns. SS-4 shows air flux can work for Ba2NaOsO6, but TS-3/TS-5 show sealed silver tubes are often used, so the severity is moderate rather than easy. Insufficient evidence from CS alone for hydroxide flux transferability to Na2YOsO6, so CS is neutral for this route’s condition severity.

#### F3 — Route 2 kinetics

Flux growth improves kinetics by dissolving and transporting refractory species; SS-4 directly grew Ba2NaOsO6 from hydroxide flux at 600 °C. TS-3 shows Os metal + Ln2O3 can react in NaOH flux at 600 °C, and TS-5 shows KOH flux can yield ordered Ln2MgIrO6 double perovskite crystals at 700 °C. This supports a higher precursor-activity and structural-pathway score than solid-state synthesis. The uncertainty is that Y2O3 solubility and Na2YOsO6 nucleation may differ from Ba2NaOsO6 and Ln3OsO7.

#### F2 — Route 3 condition severity

Route 3 is strongly penalized because TS-1 uses ca. 4 GPa and SS-1/CS-4 uses 20 kbar high-pressure equipment, Au capsules, and specialized apparatus. If pressure above 5 GPa is needed for Na2YOsO6, the high-pressure deduction applies; even at 4 GPa, the route is practically challenging. SS-1 also reports mixed products and hygroscopic crystals, supporting added phase/control difficulty. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS that high pressure is required for the target, so this route is a fallback rather than preferred.

#### F3 — Route 3 kinetics

High pressure can stabilize dense perovskite-derived oxides, as TS-1 demonstrates for Sr2CuMoO6, but short 60 min dwell times may not fully equilibrate refractory Y/Os ordering. SS-1/CS-4 shows high-pressure alkali rhenates can form, but with mixed products, indicating that high pressure does not guarantee clean kinetics or phase purity. The structural relation to the target is weaker than Route 2 because SS-1 is not a simple rock-salt B-site double perovskite. The remaining uncertainty is whether high pressure stabilizes the desired Na coordination or instead favors denser competing Na–Os phases.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has chemically dissimilar cations, with Na in low/medium coordination and Y/Os in octahedral sites, so the ionic-radius mismatch criterion is triggered; this type of cation size contrast is also present in double perovskites such as CS-3 and SS-4. The target structure is not described as having mixed edge/face/vertex-sharing octahedral networks; it is primarily corner-sharing YO6/OsO6, which lowers amorphization risk and is consistent with CS-3. The unit-cell atom count is not explicitly provided, and insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS supports a >40 atom cell, so that check is treated as not triggered/neutral. Overall, amorphization risk is low–medium rather than high.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Na2YOsO6 is likely a mildly metastable but accessible phase because its E_hull is 0.0500 eV/atom, and TS shows several synthesized oxides in the 0.06–0.08 eV/atom range (TS-1 to TS-5). The strongest synthesis support comes from SS-4, where Ba2NaOsO6, a Na–Os double perovskite, was grown by hydroxide flux, and from CS-3, where a tilted Os double perovskite required controlled oxidation. The most critical enabling factor is high Na/oxygen activity in a flux or sealed oxidizing environment, supported by SS-4, SS-5, and CS-3. The most critical limiting factor is maintaining Os in a high oxidation state without losing Os as volatile OsO4 or losing Na during heating, as suggested by SS-5 and the controlled-oxidation requirement in CS-3. Overall, the target is reasonably synthesizable, with flux growth the best first attempt.
