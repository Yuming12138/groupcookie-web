### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target E_hull of 0.0500 eV/atom is at the high-synthesizability threshold, giving a score of 90. CS provides strong support because chemically close U double perovskites such as Sr2MgUO6 and Sr2ZnUO6 have low-to-moderate E_hull values and reported solid-state syntheses (CS-2, CS-3). TS also supports that phases in the ~0.06–0.08 eV/atom metastability range can be synthesized experimentally, such as Ce2O3, LaTiO3, YTiO3, and LiLa2ReO6-related chemistry (TS-1, TS-3, TS-4, TS-5). SS provides structural but less chemical support; insufficient evidence from SS alone to prove Mg–La–U thermodynamic stability.

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 is penalized because it likely needs >1000 °C, repeated grinding/firing, and a controlled reducing or inert atmosphere; these requirements are consistent with CS ceramic U double perovskite syntheses and SS/TS reduced oxide syntheses (CS-1, CS-3, SS-3, SS-4, TS-3). Uranium precursor handling adds toxicity and air/oxidation-control risk (SS-5). There is insufficient evidence from CS that La2MgUO6 specifically can be made in air, because CS Sr2MgUO6 uses Sr2+ and is consistent with U(VI), whereas the target charge balance requires U(IV) [Reasonable Inference]. Thus Route 1 is feasible but moderately severe.

#### F3 — Route 1

The ceramic route has moderate kinetics because CS Mg–U perovskites form by solid-state reaction, but only after long firing and repeated grinding (CS-1, CS-3). Dense La2O3, MgO, and UO2 are less reactive than nitrate/acetate precursors, and reduced UO2 can be slow to react [Reasonable Inference supported by SS-5]. Structural reconstruction is not prohibitive because the MgO6/UO6 corner-sharing double-perovskite motif is directly supported by Sr2MgUO6 (CS-3). The main uncertainty is whether La3+/U4+ ordering forms cleanly before competing La–U–O or MgO residual phases persist.

#### F2 — Route 2

Route 2 still requires controlled atmosphere and uranium handling, but it can reduce the maximum crystallization temperature relative to a fully ceramic route [Reasonable Inference]. CS use of uranium acetate and carbonate/oxide precursors supports chemically compatible precursor chemistry (CS-2, CS-5). TS shows La-containing double perovskite synthesis in Ar flow with preheated La2O3 and staged heating (TS-5). There is insufficient direct evidence from CS/SS/TS for a Pechini synthesis of MgLa2UO6 itself, so this is an extrapolated but chemically sensible route.

#### F3 — Route 2

The reactive precursor route receives the highest kinetic score because nitrate/acetate/citrate mixing should reduce diffusion distances compared with CS long ceramic firings [Reasonable Inference supported by CS-1/CS-3 long firing requirements]. Uranium acetate use in CS Sr2B′UO6 syntheses supports solution-compatible U chemistry (CS-2, CS-5). The route must still crystallize an ordered oxide framework and reduce U(VI) to U(IV), so structural/valence evolution remains nontrivial (SS-3, TS-3). The main uncertainty is whether organic burnout leaves the correct oxygen stoichiometry without producing UO2-rich or La-rich impurities.

#### F2 — Route 3

Flux synthesis can operate below or around 1000 °C, supported by rare-earth oxide flux growth in SS/TS (SS-1, SS-2, TS-1). However, it needs sealed or inert handling, anhydrous flux, and uranium precursor control, so the severity is not low (SS-1, SS-2, TS-1; SS-5). There is insufficient evidence from CS for flux synthesis of Mg–U double perovskites, so the route is more exploratory. The flux route is therefore rated challenging despite potentially milder temperatures.

#### F3 — Route 3

Flux improves ion transport and can yield crystalline rare-earth oxides at lower temperature, as shown by BaLn2O4 and Ce2O3 flux growth (SS-1, SS-2, TS-1). This supports a favorable diffusion score relative to purely ceramic synthesis. Structural formation is less certain because no direct MgLa2UO6 or Mg–La–U double-perovskite flux analogue is provided; insufficient evidence from CS for this route. Contamination by alkali/chloride flux or stabilization of competing rare-earth uranates is a significant uncertainty [Reasonable Inference].

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target contains very different cation sizes/coordination environments, with MgO6, UO6, and lower-coordinate La–O sites, so ionic-size mismatch is a real disorder/amorphization risk [Reasonable Inference from target structure]. Mixed corner- and edge-sharing oxygen-centered polyhedra are present in the target and are also common in structurally similar SS oxides (SS-1, SS-3, SS-5). The unit-cell atom count criterion cannot be confirmed from the provided target text, and there is insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS to trigger that check. Overall, F4 is medium risk rather than high because CS double perovskites with related octahedral networks are experimentally crystallizable (CS-1, CS-3).

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

MgULa2O6 is likely a near-hull metastable phase rather than an unambiguously deep ground-state phase because its E_hull is 0.0500 eV/atom, exactly at the favorable metastability boundary. The strongest support comes from CS, especially Sr2MgUO6 and related Sr2B′UO6 uranium double perovskites synthesized by solid-state routes, although those analogues differ in A-site charge and likely uranium oxidation state (CS-1, CS-3). SS and TS mainly support the need for reducing/inert conditions when stabilizing lower-valent cations such as Ce3+, Ti3+, or reduced U oxides (SS-3, SS-4, SS-5, TS-3, TS-4). The most critical enabling factor is the strong structural/chemical analogy to ordered Mg/U octahedral perovskite frameworks in CS. The most critical limiting factor is oxygen-potential control: La3+ and Mg2+ require U4+ for MgLa2UO6, so air synthesis conditions successful for Sr2MgUO6 are unlikely to transfer directly [Reasonable Inference from charge balance; CS-3].
