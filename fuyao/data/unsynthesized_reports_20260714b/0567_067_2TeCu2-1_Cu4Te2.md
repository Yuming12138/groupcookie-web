### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0430 eV/atom, which falls in the ≤0.05 eV/atom bracket and therefore scores high. This is similar to TS1–TS5 analogues with E_hull values around 0.030–0.057 eV/atom, several of which have reported solid-state syntheses. The remaining uncertainty is that the target is not exactly on the hull, so it may require kinetic trapping or careful low-temperature annealing rather than representing the equilibrium Cu₂Te phase.

#### F2/F3 — Route 1: direct solid-state reaction / melt–anneal

Route 1 is supported by CS1, CS3, SS4/TS3, and TS4, all of which use sealed-tube or evacuated solid-state conditions for chalcogenides/tellurides. The severity score is reduced mainly by vacuum ampoule use, Te volatility, and staged heating/annealing. Kinetically, Te melting should help reaction, but elemental Cu is dense and full phase reconstruction from elements is slower; long anneals and controlled cooling in CS1 and SS4/TS3 support this caution.

#### F2/F3 — Route 2: binary-precursor + mechanical activation

Route 2 is the best route because TS1 shows mechanical alloying can improve phase purity in Ag–Cu sulfides, while TS2/SS2 shows binary chalcogenides can react at relatively low temperature in evacuated ampoules. The route avoids very high temperatures and high pressure but still requires sealed handling and multiple steps. Kinetically, starting from Cu–Te bonded precursors reduces reconstruction compared with elemental reaction, although ordering into tetragonal Cu₂Te may still be slow.

#### F2/F3 — Route 3: high-pressure/high-temperature quench

High-pressure synthesis is supported by CS2, CS4, and CS5 for chalcogenide/telluride polymorphs, especially phases stabilized by pressure and quenching. The route is penalized heavily because pressures above 5 GPa require specialized equipment. It could be valuable if ambient-pressure routes yield only competing Cu₂Te polymorphs, but transferability is uncertain because the cited HP analogues are mostly pyrite-like CuTe₂/NiTe₂ or ZnTe rather than Cu₂Te.

#### F2/F3 — Route 4: thin-film co-deposition

No CS/SS/TS source directly supports thin-film synthesis, so this route is a reasonable inference rather than evidence-based. It may help stabilize a 2D metastable tetragonal phase by nonequilibrium growth and substrate/template effects. Its main kinetic and practical uncertainties are Te flux control, narrow Cu:Te stoichiometry, and whether the phase crystallizes directly or requires annealing.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has only one cation type, Cu, so the cation-radius mismatch check is not triggered. The described Cu₂Te structure is simple and layered, with no evidence for >40 atoms/cell or highly mixed polyhedral connectivity. Therefore the amorphization tendency is low, although thin-film growth at too low a substrate temperature could still produce amorphous or nanocrystalline Cu–Te as a processing artifact.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Cu₂Te with E_hull = 0.043 eV/atom is likely a low-energy metastable phase rather than a clearly stable ground-state phase. The CS/SS/TS analogues strongly support sealed-tube solid-state synthesis of Cu/telluride and related chalcogenide phases, especially through evacuated ampoules and long anneals. The most critical enabling factor is the simple binary Cu–Te chemistry combined with Te’s ability to provide a reactive, low-melting transport medium. The most critical limiting factor is phase competition among Cu–Te polymorphs and stoichiometries, especially if Te is lost or if high-temperature cooling selects the equilibrium polymorph rather than the tetragonal target. Overall, synthesis is feasible, with the binary-precursor/mechanochemical route giving the best balance of reactivity and metastable-phase retention.
