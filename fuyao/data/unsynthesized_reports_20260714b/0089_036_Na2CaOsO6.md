### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0500 eV/atom, so it is at the boundary of the highest scoring thermodynamic bin. CS gives strong support because Ca2ScOsO6 is on-hull and synthesized by controlled oxidation (CS-1), while Na-containing oxide CS-4 is experimentally accessible even at E_hull ≈ 0.089 eV/atom. TS also supports accessibility because several thermodynamic analogues with E_hull ≈ 0.055–0.095 eV/atom are reported as synthesized (TS-1, TS-3, TS-4, TS-5). Remaining uncertainty is whether the target is a true low-temperature metastable phase requiring peroxide/O–O stabilization; insufficient evidence from CS/TS directly confirms this exact Na–Ca–Os–O peroxide-like motif.

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 avoids >1000 °C and high pressure, consistent with Na3OsO5 formation at 400 °C under O2 flow (SS-2). Severity comes from Na2O2 reactivity and Os toxicity/possible OsO4 volatility, both consistent with SS-2’s air-sensitive Na–Os chemistry. The route likely needs multiple grinding/annealing steps to incorporate Ca [Reasonable Inference], but CS/SS/TS provide insufficient direct evidence for Ca incorporation at 400 °C.

#### F3 — Route 1

Kinetics are helped by Na2O2, which is a reactive oxidizing sodium source used in SS-2 and SS-1. The major kinetic penalty is refractory Os and the need to form a ternary/quaternary Na–Ca–Os oxide rather than the simpler Na3OsO5 phase. Structural reconstruction is moderate because SS-2 already has trigonal-bipyramidal OsO5 similar to the target, but SS-2 also reports persistent impurity, indicating phase competition.

#### F2 — Route 2

Route 2 is more severe because CS-1 requires vacuum-sealed tubes and a two-step oxidation protocol using PbO2. It does not exceed 1000 °C, but Na loss risk and Os/Pb toxicity add significant practical penalties. CS strongly supports Ca–Os oxide formation, but SS gives insufficient evidence that the target’s OsO5/peroxide-like structure survives 900–950 °C.

#### F3 — Route 2

Ceramic kinetics are comparatively poor because CaO and OsO2 are dense oxide precursors, as in CS-1. CS-1 shows that Ca–Os oxides can form by solid-state reaction, but the target structure is not the same as the octahedral double perovskite in CS-1. Therefore, the route likely requires complete structural reconstruction and careful oxidation-state control.

#### F2 — Route 3

Route 3 uses moderate temperature around 600 °C, directly supported by hydroxide flux growth of Eu3OsO7 in TS-3. It avoids external high pressure and vacuum, but the sealed NaOH/H2O system, Os toxicity, and multi-step flux removal add penalties. CS/SS provide insufficient direct evidence for Na2CaOsO6 in hydroxide flux, but TS-3 is a strong practical analogue for Os oxide crystal growth.

#### F3 — Route 3

Flux kinetics are the best among the proposed routes because molten NaOH supplies high ionic mobility, as shown by Os oxide single-crystal growth in TS-3. This partially offsets the high refractory character of Os. The uncertainty is whether Ca dissolves and incorporates in the correct Na:Ca:Os ratio and whether the O–O motif survives the flux chemistry.

#### F2 — Route 4

Route 4 is operationally challenging because SS-1 uses 175 MPa O2 for 8 days in autoclaves, although this pressure is below the >5 GPa scoring penalty. Alkali peroxide/superoxide handling and hygroscopic products are major risks according to SS-1 and SS-2. It avoids very high temperature, which is favorable for retaining alkali-rich oxidized phases.

#### F3 — Route 4

Reactive peroxides and high oxygen pressure improve kinetics, supported by SS-1 and SS-2. However, SS-1 also indicates long reaction times and impurity/hygroscopicity problems, which reduce the kinetic and phase-control score. The route is plausible if high oxygen chemical potential is essential, but insufficient evidence from CS/TS shows that Ca-containing osmates benefit from high-pO2 autoclaves.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has large cation size/coordination mismatch among Na, Ca, and Os, and it contains mixed edge/corner polyhedral connectivity; both raise disorder or amorphization risk [Reasonable Inference] based on the target structure and mixed-connectivity analogues in CS-1 and TS-5. The unit cell appears small from the provided P1 description, so the atom-count trigger is not applied. Remaining uncertainty is whether the O–O bond indicates a peroxide unit that might actually stabilize a specific ordered framework rather than amorphize.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Na2CaOsO6 is likely a near-ground-state or weakly metastable phase because its E_hull is 0.0500 eV/atom, exactly at the common experimental-accessibility threshold. The CS data support Ca–Os oxide formation and controlled Os oxidation (CS-1), while SS strongly supports Na–Os trigonal-bipyramidal OsO5 chemistry (SS-2). The TS data support flux-based Os oxide growth at moderate temperature, especially in NaOH-rich melts (TS-3). The most critical enabling factor is maintaining a high enough oxygen/alkali chemical potential while avoiding Os over-oxidation to volatile OsO4. The most critical limiting factor is phase competition among Na–Os oxides, Ca–Os oxides, carbonates/hydroxides, and possible impurity phases, as impurity and hygroscopicity problems are repeatedly reported in SS-1 and SS-2.
