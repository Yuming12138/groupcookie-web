### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0500 eV/atom, exactly at the ≤0.05 eV/atom high-synthesizability threshold, so it receives 90/100. TS1 is particularly supportive because FeNbO4, a hydrophilite-derived niobate analogue with E_hull = 0.0629 eV/atom, is experimentally synthesized by solid-state firing. SS1/TS2 also show that a hydrophilite-derived Cr oxide at E_hull ≈0.047 eV/atom is experimentally accessible, although by HPHT. Remaining uncertainty is whether Pbcn CrNbO4 is the ambient-pressure polymorph or a metastable hydrophilite derivative relative to competing Cr–Nb oxides.

#### F2/F3 — Route 1

Route 1 has moderate condition severity because it likely requires >1000 °C and repeated firing, as shown by TS1 and SS5, but it avoids high pressure, vacuum, and volatile precursors. Kinetics are the limiting factor: Cr2O3 and Nb2O5 are refractory oxides, and SS5 explicitly required repeated grinding/firing to obtain single-phase Cr2WO6. The route is nevertheless credible because TS1 demonstrates direct niobate hydrophilite formation at ambient pressure in air. Uncertainty remains in the exact CrNbO4 phase field and whether Cr2O3/Nb2O5 react fully below 1400 °C.

#### F2/F3 — Route 2

Route 2 is penalized strongly for high-pressure equipment because SS1/TS2 and TS5 use 5–9 GPa-class apparatus for related dense octahedral oxides. Its kinetic score is better than Route 1 because HPHT can accelerate densification and stabilize rutile/hydrophilite-type networks in short dwell times, as in SS1/TS2. However, the same SS1 evidence reports composition- and pressure-dependent phase mixtures, so phase control is not trivial. This route is best viewed as a fallback if ambient-pressure synthesis gives the wrong polymorph.

#### F2/F3 — Route 3

Flux synthesis reduces diffusion barriers, and TS3/TS4 show that flux growth can produce thermodynamically similar complex oxide crystals. The route is penalized for uncertain solubility and possible flux incorporation because no direct CrNbO4 flux analogue is provided; insufficient evidence from CS and SS for flux transferability is treated as neutral. It could be useful for single crystals or lower-temperature crystallization, but it is less reliable for first synthesis than Route 1. The main uncertainty is selecting a flux that dissolves both Cr and Nb without reducing/oxidizing Cr.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target consists of compatible CrO6 and NbO6 octahedra with similar bond lengths, and the reported structure contains corner and edge sharing but no face sharing. The likely unit-cell size is not excessive for a Pbcn ABO4 hydrophilite-derived phase, so large-cell amorphization risk is low. This gives a high F4 score of 95/100. Remaining uncertainty is minor and relates mostly to possible polymorphism rather than amorphization.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

CrNbO4 is likely a near-threshold metastable or marginally stable phase because its energy above hull is 0.0500 eV/atom, but analogous oxides with comparable or higher hull energies have been synthesized (TS1, SS1/TS2). The strongest support comes from TS1 FeNbO4, which is a hydrophilite-derived niobate made by conventional high-temperature solid-state synthesis, and from SS5 Cr2WO6, which confirms ceramic accessibility of Cr-containing rutile-derived octahedral oxides. The key enabling factor is the structural compatibility of Cr3+O6 and Nb5+O6 octahedra in a dense corner/edge-sharing framework. The main limiting factor is sluggish solid-state diffusion between refractory Cr2O3 and Nb2O5, likely requiring high temperature, repeated grinding, and careful phase-purity monitoring. HPHT is available as a fallback if the Pbcn hydrophilite-derived polymorph proves metastable relative to other Cr–Nb oxides.
