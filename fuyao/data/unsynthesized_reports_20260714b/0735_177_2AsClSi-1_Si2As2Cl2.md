### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

**F1 — Thermodynamic stability.**  
The target E_hull is 0.0450 eV/atom, which places it in the most favorable scoring bin and suggests it is near the convex hull. Experimentally accessible analogues exist at similar or higher hull energies: CoSbS at ~0.044 eV/atom was synthesized under high pressure (CS5), Na(GeAs)6 at ~0.043 eV/atom was made in a sealed tube (TS2), and SiCl2 analogue at ~0.061 eV/atom was crystallized by sublimation (SS4, TS1). The uncertainty is that thermodynamic similarity does not specify the same decomposition products for Si–As–Cl; insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS for the exact Si–As–Cl phase diagram.

**F2 — Route 1, halogen-assisted CVT.**  
The condition severity is moderate because CVT analogues use sealed evacuated tubes and volatile halogens, e.g., GeAsSe iodine transport at 450 °C (CS2, SS2) and SiAs2 halogen transport under evacuated/autogenous pressure (TS3). No high pressure or >1000 °C is required in the recommended adaptation, although TS3 uses a 1200 °C hot end for SiAs2. The main penalties come from vacuum/sealing, volatile As/halide precursors, and toxic/moisture-sensitive reagents; insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS for a precise safe Cl loading window.

**F3 — Route 1, halogen-assisted CVT.**  
Kinetics are favorable because vapor transport bypasses slow bulk diffusion, and close analogues crystallize as 2D platelets within a week (CS2, SS2). Structural transferability is strong from GeAsSe’s 2D corner-sharing tetrahedral sheets (CS2, SS2), and silicon chloride transport is demonstrated for CdSiAs2 (SS5). The remaining uncertainty is whether terminal Cl can be incorporated stoichiometrically into a P3m1 SiAsCl sheet rather than forming volatile SiClx or separate Si–As phases.

**F2 — Route 2, direct sealed-tube solid-state.**  
The route uses standard evacuated sealed-tube conditions, supported by GeAs2 at 800 °C (SS3), NaGeAs at 650 °C for 10 days (TS2), and arsenide/chalcogenide sealed-tube syntheses (CS3, CS4). It is penalized by vacuum/sealing, volatile components, toxic As/chloride sources, and likely multi-step grinding/annealing as in CoAsS (CS3). It avoids high pressure and ultrahigh temperature.

**F3 — Route 2, direct sealed-tube solid-state.**  
Kinetics are only moderate because elemental Si is relatively refractory [Reasonable Inference], and long anneals are common for related solid-state products, e.g., 10 days for NaGeAs (TS2) and repeated cycles for CoAsS (CS3). Volatile As/Cl transport inside the ampoule may help, consistent with halogen transport in SiAs2 (TS3). The key uncertainty is whether Cl remains in the target phase during long heating instead of forming volatile SiCl4 or condensing elsewhere.

**F2 — Route 3, molecular halide copyrolysis.**  
Severity is moderate-to-high despite low temperature because volatile, toxic, moisture-sensitive halides are central. B–As–Cl copyrolysis uses B2Cl4/AsCl3 at 330 °C with high-vacuum/inert techniques and fractional sublimation (CS1), and SiCl2-type material is moisture-sensitive and sublimed from Si4Cl8 (SS4, TS1). There is no high-pressure or high-temperature penalty, but the handling and separation burden is significant.

**F3 — Route 3, molecular halide copyrolysis.**  
Molecular halides give excellent precursor activity and avoid diffusion limitations, as shown by B–As–Cl copyrolysis at 330 °C (CS1). However, structural kinetics are uncertain because the closest chemical analogue forms zero-dimensional clusters rather than 2D sheets (CS1). Thus, the route may rapidly produce Si–As–Cl species but not necessarily the ordered layered SiAsCl target.

**F2 — Route 4, high pressure.**  
High-pressure synthesis is penalized heavily because analogues use ~60 kbar or 15–89 kbar conditions with specialized presses (CS5, TS4). The route also requires encapsulation and likely prereaction, as used for CoSbS-type high-pressure phases (CS5). It is plausible only as a rescue route for metastability, not as the first choice.

**F3 — Route 4, high pressure.**  
Kinetics are mixed: pressure and heat can drive transformations, but dwell times are short and phase control in high-pressure analogues is difficult, with partial transformations and impurities noted (CS5, TS4). Because the target is a layered halide, insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS indicates that pressure would stabilize the desired P3m1 sheet. The route is therefore kinetically and mechanistically less reliable than CVT.

**F4 — Amorphization tendency.**  
The target is simple and low-dimensional, with one Si, one As, and one Cl site and corner-sharing tetrahedra only, so none of the three amorphization triggers apply from the target description. In contrast, some CS/TS analogues with complex mixed connectivity require careful phase control (CS3–CS5, TS5), but that complexity is not present in the target. The uncertainty is that layered halides can disorder or delaminate under moisture/thermal stress, suggested indirectly by SiCl2 moisture sensitivity and poor crystallinity (SS4, TS1).

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

SiAsCl is most likely a near-hull metastable phase rather than a robust ground-state compound, because its E_hull is 0.0450 eV/atom. The analogues support synthesis reasonably well: layered GeAsSe forms by iodine CVT (CS2, SS2), Si/As compounds form in sealed tubes and halogen transport (SS3, TS3), and near-hull metastable pnictide/halide-related materials have been experimentally accessed (CS5, TS1, TS2). The most critical enabling factor is halogen-mediated vapor transport, which can simultaneously mobilize Si/As species and provide the terminal Cl required for SiAs3Cl tetrahedra (SS5, TS3). The most critical limiting factor is chlorine chemical potential: too little Cl may yield SiAs/SiAs2-type binaries, while too much Cl may produce volatile silicon chlorides or molecular chlorides [Reasonable Inference, supported by SS4/TS1 and CS1]. Overall, the best prospect is a dry, sealed, low-halogen CVT experiment with tight stoichiometry control.
