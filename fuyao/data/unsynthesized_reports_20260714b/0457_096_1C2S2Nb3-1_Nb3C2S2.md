### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

**F1 — Thermodynamic stability.**  
The target E_hull is 0.045 eV/atom, placing it inside the ≤0.05 eV/atom high-synthesizability bin. Close Nb₂S₂C analogues have E_hull values of ~0.017–0.022 eV/atom and are experimentally accessible by topochemical routes [CS-1, CS-2, TS-1, TS-2]. The target is probably metastable rather than a strict ground state, but its energy is low enough for kinetic trapping [Reasonable Inference]. Remaining uncertainty is whether the P-6m2 Nb₃C₂S₂ polymorph competes with other Nb–C–S compositions such as Nb₂S₂C, NbC, or NbSₓ [CS-1, CS-2, SS-3].

**F2 — Route 1.**  
The 1200 °C vacuum synthesis, long dwell time, and multi-step Cu extraction are directly supported by Nb₂S₂C analogues [CS-1, CS-2, TS-1, TS-2]. Sulfur volatility requires sealed/vacuum containment [Reasonable Inference from sulfur use in CS-1, CS-2]. The absence of high pressure makes this route less severe than the NbPS route [CS-3, CS-4], but the route remains operationally demanding.

**F3 — Route 1.**  
Kinetics are helped by a topochemical mechanism that preserves the Nb–C–S sheet during Cu extraction [CS-1, CS-2]. However, refractory Nb–C bonding and repeated annealing/regrinding indicate slow equilibration [CS-1, CS-2, SS-3]. Structural similarity is excellent because the analogues are 2D Nb–C–S sheets with nearly identical Nb–C and Nb–S distances [SS-1, SS-2].

**F2 — Route 2.**  
Direct solid-state synthesis uses familiar vacuum/sintering conditions, but the combination of >1000 °C, sulfur volatility, and repeated grinding imposes significant severity [CS-1, CS-2, SS-3]. Nb carbide synthesis by direct sintering can require very high temperature, 2300 K, in high vacuum [SS-3], showing that the dry solid-state route may be harsher if NbC-like diffusion dominates. Insufficient evidence from TS for direct synthesis of the target was treated as neutral 50 for route support.

**F3 — Route 2.**  
Dense Nb, NbC, and graphite are low-activity precursors, and Nb–C diffusion is intrinsically slow [SS-3]. Unlike the topochemical route, direct reaction must build the layered Nb₃C₂S₂ framework from scratch, so structural reconstruction is difficult [Reasonable Inference]. Competing NbC or sulfide phases are likely because NbC and residual phases are already noted as phase-control issues in related systems [CS-1, CS-2, SS-3].

**F2 — Route 3.**  
Flux routes can operate at lower effective temperatures, as NaCl flux forms Zr₂N₂Se platelets at 900 °C [CS-5], while Cu/Al flux Nb boridecarbides require 1600 °C and slow cooling [SS-4, SS-5]. For the target, sulfur volatility still requires sealed or controlled atmosphere handling [Reasonable Inference]. Because no exact Nb–C–S flux synthesis is reported, direct support from CS/SS/TS is incomplete and partially neutral.

**F3 — Route 3.**  
Flux improves mass transport and can grow plate-like layered crystals [CS-5] or complex Nb-containing boridecarbides [SS-4, SS-5]. However, the target’s exact Nb–C–S framework has no direct flux analogue in the provided data, so nucleation and stoichiometry control remain uncertain. Cu flux may also intercalate or alter the structure, which could be helpful for Route 1 but problematic for direct flux growth [CS-1, CS-2; Reasonable Inference].

**F2 — Route 4.**  
High-pressure NbPS/NbPSe syntheses require 1200 °C and 65 kbar, which imposes a large severity penalty [CS-3, CS-4]. This route avoids an open sulfur vapor problem by encapsulation, but the equipment burden is high [CS-3, CS-4]. Insufficient structural evidence from SS for high-pressure stabilization of layered Nb₃C₂S₂ is treated as neutral 50 before physicochemical adjustment.

**F3 — Route 4.**  
High pressure can accelerate formation of dense ternary phases and suppress volatile loss [CS-3, CS-4; Reasonable Inference]. Yet the target is explicitly two-dimensional, and high pressure may not favor layered van der Waals-like separation [Reasonable Inference]. Because the high-pressure analogues are Nb–P–S/Se rather than Nb–C–S layered carbosulfides, kinetic transferability is limited [CS-3, CS-4].

**F4 — Amorphization tendency.**  
Only one cation, Nb, is present, so cation radius mismatch is absent [Target structure]. The target formula is small, Nb₃C₂S₂, so a large unit-cell amorphization penalty is not indicated [Target structure]. The main amorphization/defect risk comes from mixed face-, edge-, and corner-sharing octahedral connectivity, which can create competing local arrangements [Target structure; Reasonable Inference].

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Nb₃C₂S₂ is likely a low-energy metastable phase rather than a guaranteed ground-state phase, because its E_hull is 0.045 eV/atom rather than zero [Target data]. The synthesis outlook is favorable because the closest chemical, structural, and thermodynamic analogues are experimentally synthesized Nb₂S₂C layered phases with nearly identical Nb–C/Nb–S coordination and topochemical Cu extraction chemistry [CS-1, CS-2, SS-1, SS-2, TS-1, TS-2]. The most critical enabling factor is preservation of a preorganized Nb–C–S sheet during topochemical deintercalation, which avoids complete reconstruction from refractory Nb/C solids [CS-1, CS-2]. The most critical limiting factor is phase control: the target stoichiometry Nb₃C₂S₂ differs from Nb₂S₂C, and competing NbC/NbSₓ or residual Cu-containing intermediates are plausible [CS-1, CS-2, SS-3]. Overall, the material appears synthesizable but likely requires careful topochemical precursor design rather than simple direct reaction.
