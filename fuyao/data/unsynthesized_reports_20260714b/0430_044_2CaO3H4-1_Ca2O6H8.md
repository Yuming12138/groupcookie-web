### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0410 eV/atom, placing it in the high-synthesizability ≤0.05 eV/atom range [Target]. A chemically and thermodynamically close Ca(OH)₂ analogue has E_hull = 0.0354 eV/atom and is experimentally synthesized hydrothermally [CS2, TS1]. Thermodynamically similar hydrates/hydroxides with E_hull around 0.048–0.067 eV/atom are also known experimentally [TS3, TS4, TS5], so a 92/100 score is justified. The main uncertainty is that nonzero hull energy implies metastability and possible dehydration or decomposition to Ca(OH)₂ + H₂O [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 1 hydrothermal severity

Hydrothermal Ca(OD)₂ synthesis uses 235 °C for one week in a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave [CS2/TS1], and Mg(OD)₂ uses 548 K for ~20 h in an autoclave [CS5]. These are moderate, accessible conditions, but sealed water/D₂O and multiple handling steps justify deductions [CS2/TS1]. No high-temperature ceramic processing, >5 GPa pressure, or reactive gas is required [CS2/TS1, CS5]. The uncertainty is whether the target monohydrate persists at 180–250 °C or dehydrates to Ca(OH)₂ [Reasonable Inference].

#### F3 — Route 1 hydrothermal kinetics

CaO is a reactive precursor for hydroxide formation, as shown by direct CaO + D₂O hydrothermal synthesis [CS2/TS1]. Hydrothermal media provide solution-mediated transport, so diffusion barriers are much lower than in solid-state ceramic synthesis [CS2/TS1, CS5]. Structural reconstruction is moderate because CaH₄O₃ is related to Ca hydroxide sheets but includes additional water/hydrogen bonding not present in ordinary Ca(OH)₂ [Target; CS3; Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 2 cooling crystallization severity

Cooling crystallization from aqueous alkaline solution is comparatively mild: related hydrate and hydroxo phases crystallize from boiling or 40–65 °C solutions [SS3/TS4, SS5, TS3]. Protective gas or CO₂ exclusion is needed for alkaline hydroxide hydrates, as explicitly noted for Ba hydroxyhydrate crystallization [SS5]. The only significant severity deduction is the multi-step sequence of saturation, filtration, cooling, and aging [SS3/TS4, SS5]. Insufficient evidence from CS for direct CaH₄O₃ cooling crystallization, so CS is neutral for this route-specific factor, source score 50.

#### F3 — Route 2 cooling crystallization kinetics

Solution crystallization gives high mobility and avoids solid-state diffusion, as supported by Na₂Sn(OH)₆ and hydrate salt crystallizations [SS3/TS4, TS3]. The method can yield X-ray-quality crystals in related hydroxide/hydrate systems [SS3/TS4, SS5]. The limiting kinetic issue is Ca(OH)₂’s low solubility and the need to nucleate the specific CaH₄O₃ hydrate rather than ordinary portlandite or carbonate [Reasonable Inference]. TS gives useful support for solution crystallization of thermodynamically similar hydrates [TS3, TS4], while CS gives insufficient direct evidence for low-temperature CaH₄O₃ crystallization and is neutral, source score 50.

#### F2 — Route 3 supercritical ammonia severity

Supercritical NH₃ recrystallization requires 1.8–5 kbar NH₃, controlled temperature gradients, and high-pressure autoclaves [CS1, SS4]. SS4 also reports glovebox handling under Ar for hydroxide hydrate precursors/products, increasing operational difficulty [SS4]. Although pressure is below the >5 GPa scoring threshold, toxic/corrosive NH₃, sealed volatile solvent, and multi-step high-pressure handling justify a low F2 score [CS1, SS4]. TS provides insufficient evidence for supercritical NH₃ synthesis of thermodynamically similar Ca phases, so TS is neutral, source score 50.

#### F3 — Route 3 supercritical ammonia kinetics

The recrystallization mechanism in supercritical NH₃ is favorable for mass transport and crystal growth, as shown for K/Rb hydroxide monohydrates [CS1, SS4]. These analogues have closely related O–H bonding motifs and hydrated hydroxide chemistry, supporting good kinetic plausibility [CS1, SS4]. However, transfer to Ca²⁺ hydroxide hydrate is not demonstrated and may be less favorable because Ca²⁺ has stronger lattice binding than alkali cations [Reasonable Inference]. TS gives insufficient evidence for this specific route and is neutral, source score 50.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target is a single-cation Ca hydroxide hydrate, so there is no multi-cation ionic-radius mismatch trigger [Target]. The structure is described as a 2D sheet with Ca–O and O–H bonding but not as a complex network with simultaneous vertex/edge/face sharing, so the mixed-connectivity trigger is not activated [Target]. CS/SS/TS analogues include both simple sheet hydroxides and more complex hydrates, but they do not provide direct evidence that the target should amorphize; insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS is therefore treated as neutral source score 50 for F4. The remaining uncertainty is the exact unit-cell atom count, which is not specified [Target].

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

CaH₄O₃ is likely a low-energy metastable hydrate rather than a strict ground-state phase because its E_hull is 0.0410 eV/atom [Target]. The synthesis outlook is favorable because chemically and thermodynamically close Ca(OH)₂ is experimentally accessible by hydrothermal hydration of CaO, and multiple structurally/thermodynamically similar hydroxide hydrates crystallize from solution [CS2/TS1, SS3/TS4, SS5, TS3]. The most important enabling factor is solution- or hydrothermal-mediated transport, which avoids the slow diffusion and dehydration risks of high-temperature ceramic synthesis [CS2/TS1, CS5, SS3/TS4]. The most important limiting factor is control of hydration state and CO₂ exclusion, because Ca hydroxides readily form Ca(OH)₂ or carbonate under inappropriate water activity or atmospheric exposure [SS5; Reasonable Inference]. Overall, the target should be synthetically accessible, but probably through low-temperature hydrated crystallization rather than high-temperature solid-state methods.
