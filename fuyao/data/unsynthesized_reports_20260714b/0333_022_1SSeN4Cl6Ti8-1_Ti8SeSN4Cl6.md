### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0020 eV/atom, so by the required rubric it scores in the 90–100 range. TS strongly supports this score because the closest thermodynamic analogues are stable or near-stable: Ti₃Se₄ at 0.012 eV/atom (TS-1), Ti₆Se₈ at 0.0 eV/atom (TS-2), UNCl at 0.0 eV/atom (TS-3), and Ti₂GaN at 0.0 eV/atom (TS-4). CS is somewhat contradictory: sulfide analogues are stable/near-stable (CS-1 to CS-4), but the closest N/S/Cl analogue V₂S₄N₆Cl₄ is much higher in energy at 0.275 eV/atom (CS-5), so CS is supportive but less decisive than TS. Remaining uncertainty is that DFT stability does not guarantee kinetic accessibility or mixed-anion ordering.

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 has moderate-to-severe conditions because it uses NH₃ and sealed/dry handling, directly supported by UNCl ammonolysis in sealed tubes or NH₃ stream (SS-2, TS-3). Volatile and air-sensitive chemistry is also expected from Ti chloride and S/Se handling [Reasonable Inference], consistent with sealed ampoule Ti–Se routes (SS-1, TS-2) and drybox/vacuum TiCl₄/S₄N₄ chemistry (CS-5). However, it avoids >1000 °C and avoids GPa pressure, unlike TS-5 high-pressure Nb–Se synthesis. The main uncertainty is whether Cl retention and N insertion can be balanced without forming TiN, TiClₓ residues, or TiSe₂/TiS₂.

#### F3 — Route 1

The kinetics are favorable relative to conventional ceramic synthesis because NH₃/metal chloride chemistry is reactive at 525–650 °C for UNCl (SS-2, TS-3), and TiCl₄/S₄N₄ chemistry reacts readily in solution (CS-5). Ti–Se octahedral frameworks are known but often require high-temperature/topochemical processing (SS-1, TS-2), so diffusion and ordering are not trivial. The structural relationship to a 2D nitride chloride analogue is helpful (SS-2, TS-3), but the target’s mixed Se/S/Cl/N anion ordering is not directly demonstrated. Thus F3 is good but not excellent.

#### F2 — Route 2

Route 2 uses standard sealed-ampoule solid-state methods like those used for Ti–Se and sulfide analogues (SS-1, TS-2, CS-3, CS-4), so it avoids extreme pressure. It still requires vacuum/Ar sealing, volatile S/Se/Cl control, likely regrinding, and air-sensitive chloride handling, which are all severity penalties supported by CS-3, CS-4, SS-1/TS-2, and CS-5. CS sulfide sources provide useful sealed-ampoule/annealing signal, while CS-1 thermal decomposition is less relevant to mixed Ti–N–Cl chemistry and is treated as weak/near-neutral for Route 2 condition selection. The uncertainty is whether a bulk ceramic reaction can retain enough chloride and nitrogen at elevated temperature.

#### F3 — Route 2

Kinetics are less favorable because TiN and binary titanium chalcogenides are refractory [Reasonable Inference], and Ti₂GaN-type solid-state nitride synthesis can require 500–900 h (SS-3, TS-4). Ti–Se frameworks can form by direct elemental reaction (SS-5, TS-1), but ordered mixed-anion sheets likely require significant reconstruction. CS-3 and CS-4 show that sulfide solid-state products often need tempering/long annealing, supporting a lower kinetics score. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS exists for direct formation of Ti₈SeS(N₂Cl₃)₂ by one-pot solid-state reaction, so this route is kinetically risky.

#### F2 — Route 3

Route 3 benefits from low temperatures, supported by CS-5 solution/vacuum chemistry and CS-2 supercritical NH₃ at 160 °C. However, CS-5 explicitly involves vacuum line/drybox handling, S₄N₄, TiCl₄, dropwise addition, drying, sublimation, and recrystallization, so the operational severity remains high. CS-2 uses specialized high-pressure autoclave conditions with NH₃ and Ar counterpressure, which adds equipment and safety burden even though pressure is far below GPa. The main uncertainty is whether the low-temperature precursor can be converted to a crystalline extended 2D target without decomposition.

#### F3 — Route 3

Precursor activity is high because TiCl₄/S₄N₄ reactions occur readily in solution (CS-5), and supercritical NH₃ can crystallize chalcogenides at low temperature (CS-2). Molecular mixing should reduce diffusion limitations [Reasonable Inference], giving a better diffusion score than Route 2. The limiting kinetic factor is structural reconstruction: CS-5 reports mixtures and adducts, while CS-2 reports contamination/phase mixtures, so nucleating the exact Ti–Se–S–N–Cl sheet is uncertain. Therefore Route 3 has promising chemistry but high phase-selectivity risk.

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

By the required rubric, the target triggers no cation-radius mismatch because Ti is the only cation, and no face-sharing is reported in the target connectivity. The provided formula has 20 atoms per formula unit, and the structure description does not prove a >40 atom unit cell; therefore C2 is not triggered. SS-1/TS-2 Ti–Se analogues do show face-sharing, but that is not described for the target, so it should not trigger C3. Remaining uncertainty is that mixed-anion disorder could still broaden crystallinity, but this is not captured by the specified F4 checks.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Ti₈SeS(N₂Cl₃)₂ is likely very close to a ground-state phase because its E_hull is only 0.0020 eV/atom, and TS analogues are mostly stable or near-stable (TS-1 to TS-4). The synthesis evidence is moderately supportive: SS/TS provide strong precedent for Ti–Se frameworks and 2D nitride chloride chemistry, while CS-5 confirms relevant transition-metal N/S/Cl molecular reactivity but also warns of mixture formation. The most critical enabling factor is reactive ammonolysis/halide chemistry, because UNCl forms at 525–650 °C under NH₃ or sealed pressure (SS-2, TS-3), avoiding the slowest bulk diffusion regime. The most critical limiting factor is mixed-anion phase control: S, Se, N, and Cl must all be retained and ordered without forming TiN, TiSe₂, TiS₂, TiClₓ, or molecular N–S–Cl adducts. Overall, the target is synthesizable in a research solid-state/inorganic laboratory, but it should be approached as a moisture-sensitive, phase-selectivity-limited mixed-anion material.
