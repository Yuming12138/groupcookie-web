### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target’s E_hull is 0.0500 eV/atom, which falls at the upper boundary of the “90–100” category in the required rubric. Chemically similar Na–La–Te and Na–La–W/Fe oxides are reported at E_hull = 0 eV/atom [CS1–CS3], which supports the general stability of Na–rare-earth multication oxides. Thermodynamic analogues with E_hull values around 0.039–0.084 eV/atom were synthesized by solid-state or flux methods [TS2–TS5], indicating that the target’s metastability is not prohibitive. Structural sources alone provide insufficient evidence from SS for target thermodynamic stability beyond showing that related Te/Bi oxide frameworks can be made.

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 uses air and sub-1000 °C firing, matching the mild solid-state conditions used for NaMgLaTeO₆ at 973 K in air [CS1] and La₂LiSbO₆ up to 1073 K in air [CS4]. The main severity penalty is chemical handling of Bi/Te compounds, which are not air-sensitive but require toxicity precautions [Reasonable Inference]. There is insufficient evidence from TS that Route 1 specifically controls Na/Te volatility, so no sealed-system deduction is applied unless open firing fails.

#### F3 — Route 1

Nitrate/hydroxide precursors are reactive and supported by CS1 and CS4, giving good precursor activity. However, solid-state diffusion in a four-cation oxide and the need to form a low-symmetry P1 structure reduce the kinetics score relative to sol-gel or hydrothermal routes [Reasonable Inference]. CS1 shows a chemically close phase can form in only 6 h at 973 K, which is favorable, but insufficient evidence from SS/TS confirms the exact NaLaBiTeO₆ structure type.

#### F2 — Route 2

Route 2 has more processing steps than Route 1 because it includes dissolution, resin formation, drying, decomposition, and calcination, as in CS2/CS3. It avoids high pressure and can likely be run in air for this Bi/Te oxide target, whereas the reducing atmospheres in CS2/CS3 were tied to Fe/W chemistry and are not directly required here [Reasonable Inference]. The use of nitric acid and Bi/Te precursors gives a modest chemical-handling penalty.

#### F3 — Route 2

CS2/CS3 explicitly state that soft-chemistry was used to obtain reactive, homogeneous precursors for multication Na–rare-earth oxides. This strongly improves precursor activity and diffusion relative to ordinary oxide mixing. The remaining kinetic uncertainty is structural: NaLaBiTeO₆ has Bi/Te lone-pair distortions and triclinic P1 symmetry, which are not demonstrated in the Fe/W perovskite analogues [CS2, CS3, Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 3

Route 3 operates at only ~230 °C and does not require high pressure beyond normal autogenous hydrothermal pressure [SS3]. The severity is therefore low, with the main penalty being Bi/Te precursor toxicity and sealed-autoclave handling [SS3, Reasonable Inference]. There is insufficient evidence from CS/TS that hydrothermal conditions specifically yield NaLaBiTeO₆ rather than related tellurites.

#### F3 — Route 3

Hydrothermal synthesis benefits from solution-mediated transport, and SS3 directly shows Na–rare-earth–Te oxide crystals grown at 230 °C for 4 days. This gives excellent ion transport compared with solid-state diffusion. The uncertainty is phase selectivity: SS3 reports NaY(Te₂O₅)₂, not NaLaBiTeO₆, and Bi may introduce competing bismuth tellurite phases [SS3, Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 4

Route 4 is penalized because evacuated ampoules and sealed systems are required, as used for CaTeO₃ [SS2] and Bi oxyhalide/oxide analogues [TS2, TS5]. It also may involve flux preparation/removal, making it multistep [TS3]. The conditions are still below 1000 °C in the recommended adaptation, so no high-temperature penalty is applied.

#### F3 — Route 4

Flux or sealed-tube chemistry can enhance mass transport; Bi₂O₃ flux enabled crystal growth of a Bi oxide in TS3, and sealed ampoules controlled CaTeO₃ formation in SS2. However, SS2 also reports polymorph and impurity issues for tellurates/tellurites, lowering the phase-control component. This route is valuable as a backup when Na/Te volatility or poor crystallinity limits open-air synthesis.

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

The target has very different cation sizes and coordination preferences: Na is 6-coordinate, La is 7-coordinate, Bi is 3-coordinate, and Te is distorted 4-coordinate in the target description, triggering the ionic-radius/coordination mismatch check [Reasonable Inference from target structure]. There is insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS that the unit cell contains more than 40 atoms, and the target description does not demonstrate simultaneous vertex/edge/face-sharing connectivity. Therefore, only one amorphization-risk trigger is applied, giving a low–medium risk score.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

NaLaBiTeO₆ is likely a **near-hull metastable phase** rather than a clearly deep ground-state phase, because its E_hull is 0.0500 eV/atom. The chemical analogues provide strong support: Na–La–Te oxide chemistry can be accessed by simple air solid-state synthesis [CS1], and multication Na–rare-earth oxides can be made by citrate soft chemistry [CS2, CS3]. Structural support is moderate because hydrothermal Na–rare-earth–tellurium oxides exist [SS3], but the exact Bi-containing P1 structure is not demonstrated. The most critical enabling factor is the availability of reactive nitrate/hydroxide or citrate-derived precursors that can homogenize Na/La/Bi/Te before crystallization [CS1–CS3]. The main limiting factor is phase competition and stoichiometry control involving volatile Na/Te components and fluxing Bi₂O₃, as suggested by tellurite polymorph issues and Bi oxide flux behavior [SS2, TS3].
