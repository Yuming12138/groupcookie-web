### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target E_hull is 0.0500 eV/atom, which falls at the favorable boundary and justifies a high thermodynamic score of 90. Chemically similar Sr2CaMoO6 is calculated on the hull and is experimentally synthesized by a ceramic route, supporting the Sr2CaBO6 double-perovskite topology (CS-1). Thermodynamic analogues near 0.034–0.084 eV/atom are accessible but often metastable Cr oxides, so TS supports possible synthesis but with caution (TS-1 to TS-4). SS gives mixed evidence because some structurally similar Cr perovskites are metastable or high-pressure phases, especially BaCrO3 and CaCrO3 variants (SS-1, SS-3, SS-4).

#### F2 — Route 1 condition severity

Route 1 receives 70 because it uses standard ceramic conditions like the Sr2CaMoO6 route, requiring only a furnace, air/O2, and repeated grinding/pelletizing (CS-1). The temperature likely exceeds 1000 °C and the process is multistep, both directly supported by CS-1 and CS-2. The added penalty is Cr(VI) precursor toxicity, which is a [Reasonable Inference] from the formal charge balance of Sr2CaCrO6. SS/TS do not require high pressure for this exact composition, but they warn that Cr oxide perovskite phase control can be difficult (SS-4, TS-1).

#### F3 — Route 1 reaction kinetics

Route 1 has moderate kinetics because carbonate/oxide/chromate solid-state reactions typically require long diffusion distances [Reasonable Inference], and chemically similar double perovskites required repeated firings and regrinding (CS-1 to CS-3). The structural reconstruction is favorable because Sr2CaMoO6 has the same Sr2CaBO6 double-perovskite motif with corner-sharing CaO6 and MoO6 octahedra (CS-1). The uncertainty is that replacing Mo6+ by Cr6+ may alter decomposition and redox behavior, which is not directly resolved by CS.

#### F2 — Route 2 condition severity

Route 2 receives 80 because it avoids very high temperatures and high pressure while improving cation mixing [Reasonable Inference]. It is still multistep because gelation, burnout, calcination, and final annealing are required [Reasonable Inference]. The main severity penalty is Cr(VI) toxicity if chromate or CrO3 is used [Reasonable Inference]. There is insufficient direct evidence from CS/SS/TS for sol-gel Sr2CaCrO6, so the sol-gel-specific source signal is neutral where direct support is absent.

#### F3 — Route 2 reaction kinetics

Route 2 scores best kinetically because soluble nitrate/acetate/citrate precursors should provide molecular-scale mixing and higher precursor activity [Reasonable Inference]. This directly addresses the repeated regrinding and long sintering reported for ceramic double perovskites (CS-1, CS-2). The structural reconstruction from amorphous precursor to ordered double perovskite remains significant [Reasonable Inference], so the score is not higher. Remaining uncertainty is whether Cr can be oxidized and retained as Cr(VI) during calcination without forming separate SrCrO4/CaCrO4 phases.

#### F2 — Route 3 condition severity

Route 3 receives a low severity score of 30 because high pressure above 5 GPa is directly supported by several Cr perovskite analogues (SS-1, SS-3, SS-4, TS-1, TS-3). Specialized belt, Walker-type, or anvil apparatus and capsules are reported for those analogues (SS-1, SS-4, TS-1). Dry-box handling and capsule loading add complexity, as reported for Sr1−xCaxCrO3 (SS-4, TS-1). The route is plausible only as a fallback because the closest chemical analogue, Sr2CaMoO6, did not require pressure (CS-1).

#### F3 — Route 3 reaction kinetics

High-pressure synthesis can rapidly stabilize dense Cr–O perovskite frameworks, as shown for BaCrO3 and Ca/Sr–Cr perovskites (SS-1, SS-3, SS-4, TS-1, TS-3). However, short high-pressure dwell times and oxide precursors may not allow full Sr/Ca/Cr cation ordering [Reasonable Inference]. The route also risks stabilizing Cr(IV)-type perovskites instead of formal Cr(VI) Sr2CaCrO6, because the SS/TS high-pressure analogues mainly concern Cr(IV) compounds (SS-4, TS-1). Therefore F3 is moderate-low.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target triggers the cation-size mismatch criterion because Sr, Ca, and Cr occupy substantially different coordination environments and have very different bond lengths in the target structure (target description). It does not trigger the large-cell criterion because the described primitive structure contains 10 atoms, not more than 40 (target description). It also does not trigger the mixed edge/face/vertex connectivity criterion because CaO6 and CrO6 octahedra are reported as corner-sharing only (target description). The amorphization risk is therefore low-to-medium rather than high.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Sr2CaCrO6 is likely a borderline stable or mildly metastable phase because its E_hull is exactly 0.0500 eV/atom, while thermodynamically similar Cr oxides around this range are experimentally accessible but often condition-sensitive (TS-1 to TS-4). The strongest positive analogue is Sr2CaMoO6, a chemically very close Sr2CaB6+O6 double perovskite made by conventional ceramic synthesis in air (CS-1). The most critical enabling factor is the strong structural and charge analogy between Mo6+ and Cr6+ double perovskites, which supports an ambient-pressure oxidizing route (CS-1 and [Reasonable Inference]). The most critical limiting factor is Cr oxidation-state control: SS/TS Cr perovskites show high-pressure requirements, impurity formation, and sensitivity to synthesis conditions for related Cr oxides (SS-2, SS-4, TS-1, TS-4). Overall, the material is reasonably synthesizable, especially by a homogeneous precursor route followed by oxidizing annealing, but phase-purity optimization is likely required.
