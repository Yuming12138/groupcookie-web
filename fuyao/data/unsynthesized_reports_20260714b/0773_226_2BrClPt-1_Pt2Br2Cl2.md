### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target E_hull of 0.0160 eV/atom is well inside the ≤0.05 eV/atom high-synthesizability range, so the thermodynamic score is high. TS analogues with E_hull values around 0.011–0.036 eV/atom are experimentally synthesized, indicating that small positive hull energies do not preclude isolation in halide systems [TS1–TS5]. SS provides additional support because PtCl₂ and PdBr₂ structural analogues are stable, known phases [SS1, SS2]. CS gives weaker but consistent support because several chemically related halides are stable or near-stable and synthesized [CS1–CS5].

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 has moderate severity because it uses 500–650 °C temperatures, which are not extreme, but requires sealed ampoules and reactive halogens [SS1, SS2]. Chlorine is explicitly used for PtCl₂ CVT [SS1], and bromine is explicitly used as transport agent for PdBr₂ [SS2]. The main uncertainty is safe control of mixed Br₂/Cl₂ fugacity and suppression of Pt(IV) or binary PtX₂ phases [Reasonable Inference]. CS gives insufficient direct Pt-specific signal for this route, so CS is treated as neutral for Pt CVT severity.

#### F3 — Route 1

Kinetics are favorable because vapor transport bypasses slow solid diffusion, as shown by PtCl₂ and PdBr₂ crystal growth through halogen transport [SS1, SS2]. The local Pt–Cl geometry in PtCl₂ is extremely similar to the target Pt–Cl environment, reducing structural reconstruction [SS1]. PdBr₂ supplies support for Br-bridged heavy-metal halide frameworks but is not chemically identical to Pt, so some uncertainty remains [SS2]. TS2 also supports halide sublimation/growth but warns that metastable PdCl₂ requires careful cooling, relevant to mixed-halide phase control [TS2].

#### F2 — Route 2

Route 2 avoids very high temperatures and high pressure, consistent with sealed solid-state halide syntheses at 350–600 °C [CS2, CS5, SS5]. It still requires vacuum sealing and may require volatile halogen buffers, producing deductions for controlled atmosphere and sealed volatile species [CS5, SS5; Reasonable Inference]. The process is less hazardous than direct Cl₂/Br₂ CVT if only binary Pt halides are used, but Pt halides and possible halogen release remain toxic/corrosive risks [Reasonable Inference]. SS provides strong structural relevance through PtCl₂, while CS/TS mainly support sealed-tube feasibility.

#### F3 — Route 2

Solid-state kinetics are moderate because PtCl₂/PtBr₂ are chemically active halide precursors, but anion ordering and intergrowth conversion may be slow [SS1; Reasonable Inference]. CS5 shows a related sealed solid-state halide reaction needs 10 days at 500 °C, and CS2 reports 4 weeks at 600 °C with phase-purity problems for a more complex halide/chalcogenide [CS5, CS2]. The target has only one cation, which helps relative to multi-cation CS analogues [Reasonable Inference]. Remaining uncertainty is whether binary PtCl₂ and PtBr₂ form a true ordered PtBrCl phase rather than a solid solution or physical mixture [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 3

The solution route has low temperature and ambient pressure, directly analogous in severity to the room-temperature SnICl precipitation method [CS1]. It involves corrosive/toxic Pt salts and strong acids, but no high-temperature halogen pressure if the optional anneal is omitted [CS1; Reasonable Inference]. SS and TS provide insufficient direct evidence for solution synthesis of PtBrCl and are treated as neutral for this route’s severity. The route is operationally easy but not necessarily phase-selective.

#### F3 — Route 3

Solution kinetics are fast because halide exchange and precipitation can occur at room temperature, as shown for SnICl [CS1]. However, CS1 also reports phase sensitivity with varying HI amount, implying that mixed-halide stoichiometry is a serious kinetic/thermodynamic control issue [CS1]. SS and TS provide insufficient evidence that PtBrCl forms by precipitation, and Pt(II) halide complexes can remain soluble or form competing complexes [Reasonable Inference]. Therefore, structural crystallization—not chemical mixing—is the limiting kinetic factor.

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

The target has a simple one-cation PtBrCl composition, so the cation-size-disorder criterion is not triggered [Target structure]. The described structure is a 2D sheet with Pt coordinated to two Br and two Cl, and no mixed vertex/edge/face polyhedral network is reported [Target structure]. CS/SS/TS do not provide the target’s exact atom count, so C2 has some uncertainty, but the formula and simple sheet description argue against a large >40 atom cell [Target structure; insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS]. Overall amorphization tendency is low.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

PtBrCl is likely a near-ground-state or mildly metastable phase because its E_hull is only 0.0160 eV/atom, and comparable low-positive-hull halides are experimentally accessible [TS1–TS5]. The strongest support comes from structurally similar PtCl₂, which has nearly identical Pt–Cl coordination and is grown by chlorine CVT [SS1], and from PdBr₂/PdCl₂ halide frameworks grown by vapor transport or sublimation [SS2, TS2]. The most critical enabling factor is the availability of volatile/reactive halide transport chemistry that can equilibrate Pt–Br and Pt–Cl bonding at moderate temperatures [SS1, SS2]. The most critical limiting factor is Br/Cl ordering and halogen chemical-potential control, because mixed-halide analogues can form competing phases when halide stoichiometry varies [CS1]. Overall, synthesis appears feasible, with CVT/sealed halogen transport the most credible route for crystalline phase-pure material.
