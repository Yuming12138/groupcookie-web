### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

**F1 — Thermodynamic stability.**  
The target’s E_hull of 0.0500 eV/atom lies at the boundary of the 90–100 scoring bin, so a score of 90 is justified by the provided target data. CS1 and CS3 show uranium-containing oxide frameworks with very low hull energies, while TS1/TS4/TS5 show experimentally referenced materials around 0.04–0.06 eV/atom, supporting accessibility of mildly metastable phases. SS1 and SS5 are on-hull structurally similar phosphates, but SS data do not specifically validate Cs–U–phosphate stability, so uncertainty remains.

**F2/F3 — Route 1 flux growth.**  
CS1 strongly supports uranium phosphate flux synthesis using H₃BO₃/B₂O₃, while SS1 and TS3 support phosphate flux crystallization; this lowers kinetic barriers and motivates a relatively strong F3. The severity is moderate because CS1 uses high temperatures, slow cooling/quenching, Pt crucibles, and uranium precursors; TS4 additionally supports concern about alkali volatility. The main uncertainty is U(V) stabilization because CS1 appears closer to uranyl U(VI) chemistry than the target.

**F2/F3 — Route 2 ceramic solid-state.**  
CS3 and TS4 support conventional high-temperature ceramic synthesis of related oxide frameworks, but CS3 required 1200 °C for 1 week with repeated grinding, indicating slow kinetics. TS4 shows excess alkali carbonate is needed for volatile alkali retention, directly relevant to Cs-containing high-T synthesis. Because UO₂/U₃O₈ and phosphate precursors require extensive reconstruction, F3 is lower than flux/hydrothermal routes.

**F2/F3 — Route 3 hydrothermal.**  
SS5 is the strongest evidence for this route because it uses actinide oxide, Cs₂CO₃, H₃PO₄, HF, and water at 180 °C to form actinide phosphate/fluoride products; SS3 independently supports acidic hydrothermal phosphate crystal growth at 170–250 °C. This gives low condition severity and favorable dissolution–reprecipitation kinetics. However, SS5 also shows time-dependent formation of multiple Np phases, so phase selectivity is the main uncertainty.

**F2/F3 — Route 4 sealed-tube redox.**  
CS2 supports evacuated sealed-tube synthesis with slow cooling and a metal/oxide redox pair, which is relevant by analogy to controlling U valence. TS1 supports phosphate solid-state reaction at lower temperature with degassing/regrinding. The route is penalized for vacuum sealing, volatility, toxicity, and indirect structural transferability because no SS analogue directly validates sealed-tube synthesis for this target.

**F4 — Amorphization tendency.**  
The main amorphization trigger is the large cation size/field-strength mismatch between Cs, U, and P, consistent with the target’s very different Cs–O, U–O, and P–O bond lengths. No evidence in the target description indicates a >40-atom unit cell or simultaneous vertex/edge/face polyhedral connectivity; CS/SS/TS provide insufficient direct evidence to trigger those checks for the target. Thus the amorphization risk is low–medium rather than high.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

CsU(PO₄)₂ is likely a mildly metastable phase rather than a deeply unstable compound, because its E_hull is 0.0500 eV/atom and TS analogues around 0.04–0.06 eV/atom are experimentally accessible. The CS data strongly support uranium phosphate crystallization by flux methods, while SS data most strongly support hydrothermal actinide phosphate formation. The most critical enabling factor is using a liquid-mediated pathway—hydrothermal or flux—to overcome slow U/phosphate solid-state diffusion. The most critical limiting factor is U oxidation-state control, because the charge-balanced target requires U(V), while many uranium phosphate analogues favor U(VI) uranyl or U(IV)/U(VI) redox products. Cs volatility is a secondary limitation for high-temperature routes, supported by TS4’s need for excess alkali carbonate.
