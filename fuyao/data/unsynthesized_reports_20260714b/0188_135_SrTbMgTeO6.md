### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0500 eV/atom, which lies at the high-synthesizability boundary of the provided scoring table. Thermodynamic analogues with comparable or slightly higher hull energies are reported as known/synthesized phases, including Sr₂YSbO₆ at 0.0519 eV/atom (TS5), NaEuGeO₄ at 0.0604 eV/atom (TS1), and Na₂TbO₃ at 0.0760 eV/atom (TS4). The remaining uncertainty is that the target sits exactly on the threshold, so small DFT or configurational errors could shift it from near-ground-state to moderately metastable.

#### F2 — Route 1: ceramic solid-state

Route 1 is supported by the closest chemical analogue Sr₂MgTeO₆, synthesized at 900/1000/1100 °C in air with regrinding (CS4), and by Sr₂CoTeO₆ requiring 950 °C for 120 h with intermediate grinding (CS3). These conditions are standard but moderately severe because they involve high temperature, multi-step processing, and possible Te volatility/toxicity. Insufficient evidence from SS for this exact Sr–Mg–Tb–Te chemistry, so SS contributes only a neutral structural signal for condition severity.

#### F3 — Route 1: ceramic solid-state

Kinetics are moderate because CS4 shows the exact Sr–Mg–Te framework can form by ceramic reaction, but MgO and rare-earth oxides are diffusion-limited [Reasonable Inference]. CS3 and TS5 show long dwell times and repeated grinding are often needed for Sr-based ordered perovskites, indicating slow cation ordering. The main uncertainty is whether Tb incorporation slows ordering or creates competing Tb/Te oxide phases.

#### F2 — Route 2: wet-chemical nitrate/citrate

CS1 provides direct evidence that Mg–lanthanide–Te oxides can be made from nitrate/hydroxide/telluric precursors at relatively low temperatures in air. The proposed citrate-gel implementation is a [Reasonable Inference] that improves homogeneity while adding solution-processing and burnout steps. Insufficient evidence from SS and TS for citrate-gel synthesis of this exact structure, so those sources are neutral for this route’s condition severity.

#### F3 — Route 2: wet-chemical nitrate/citrate

The high F3 score is based on reactive nitrates/hydrated precursors in CS1 and the expected shorter diffusion lengths of gel-derived powders [Reasonable Inference]. However, structural reconstruction remains nontrivial because the target has ordered MgO₆/TbO₆ octahedra plus TeO₅ square pyramids, while CS1 contains TeO₆ octahedra. The largest uncertainty is whether lower-temperature crystallization yields the desired Cm phase or a related tellurate/tellurite impurity.

#### F2 — Route 3: flux/sealed-crucible

Flux or sealed growth is supported by TS2 using sealed Au-tube slow cooling and by SS1 using sealed Pt crucibles for high-temperature crystal growth. The route is penalized for sealed handling, possible Te volatility, toxicity, and multi-step preparation. Insufficient direct evidence from CS for flux growth of SrMgTbTeO₆, so CS is neutral for this specific route.

#### F3 — Route 3: flux/sealed-crucible

Flux transport should improve ion mobility relative to ceramic sintering [Reasonable Inference], and TS2 demonstrates oxide single-crystal growth from a melt/flux. SS1 shows sealed-crucible high-temperature treatment can yield crystals, but the chemistry is Ge-based rather than Te/Tb-based. The uncertainty is high because flux chemistry may stabilize competing tellurite or Sr–Te phases rather than the target.

#### F2 — Route 4: hydrothermal

TS1 shows hydrothermal rare-earth germanate synthesis at 230 °C for two weeks in a Teflon-lined autoclave, which is mild in temperature and pressure. The route is penalized mainly for multi-step handling and caustic/toxic reagents. Insufficient evidence from CS and SS for hydrothermal synthesis of Sr–Mg–Te perovskite-like oxides, so those sources are neutral or weak.

#### F3 — Route 4: hydrothermal

Hydrothermal solution transport gives favorable precursor activity and diffusion, as demonstrated by single-crystal formation in TS1. However, the structural pathway score is low because no provided hydrothermal analogue forms a Sr–Mg–Te ordered perovskite or TeO₅-containing framework. The route is therefore exploratory despite good nominal kinetic conditions.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target contains a large Sr A-site cation and much smaller Mg/Tb/Te B-site cations, so the ionic-size contrast check is triggered; analogous large Sr–small B-site coordination appears in Sr₂MgTeO₆ (CS4). The target description does not demonstrate a >40 atom primitive cell, and it reports corner plus face sharing but not edge sharing, so those checks are not triggered. Remaining uncertainty is moderate because the low-symmetry Cm structure and mixed TeO₅/MO₆ units may still encourage disorder or glassy intermediates during low-temperature routes.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

SrMgTbTeO₆ is likely a near-ground-state or weakly metastable oxide because its E_hull is exactly 0.0500 eV/atom, and thermodynamic analogues with similar hull energies are known phases (TS1, TS4, TS5). The chemical support is strong for Sr–Mg–Te oxide formation by ceramic processing because Sr₂MgTeO₆ is directly synthesized from SrCO₃, MgO, and TeO₂ (CS4), while Mg–lanthanide–Te oxide chemistry is supported by NaMgLaTeO₆ from nitrate/telluric precursors (CS1). The most critical enabling factor is cation homogeneity, best addressed by a nitrate/citrate precursor route inferred from CS1 and standard wet-chemical practice. The most critical limiting factor is redox/phase control involving Tb and Te, because the target contains TeO₅ square pyramids rather than the more common TeO₆ octahedra seen in several analogues (CS1, CS3, CS4). Overall, synthesis is plausible but will likely require careful annealing and phase monitoring.
