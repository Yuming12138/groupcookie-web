### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target has E above hull = 0.023 eV/atom, which is within the commonly synthesizable near-ground-state range and therefore receives **95/100**. CS strongly supports this because the closest Y–N–Cl analogue Y₂NCl₃ is calculated at 0 eV/atom and experimentally synthesized (CS-1), while layered Y/O/Cl and Zr/N/Cl analogues are also stable or nearly stable (CS-2 to CS-5). TS also supports this score because several thermodynamically similar phases with E_hull ≈0.026–0.042 eV/atom were synthesized by conventional sealed-ampoule or solid-state routes (TS-1, TS-2, TS-3). SS is supportive but less chemically specific; insufficient evidence from SS alone for exact Y–N–Cl stability, so SS is treated as neutral-to-supportive rather than decisive.

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 avoids high pressure and temperatures above 1000 °C, matching CS-1 where Y₂NCl₃ formed at 800–900 °C. The severity penalties come from sealed volatile/air-sensitive chloride chemistry, glovebox handling, ampoule welding, and long annealing, all explicitly indicated by CS-1. SS-5 and TS-3 confirm that sealed Ta/Nb-style ampoule chemistry at similar temperatures is normal for rare-earth halide/interstitial compounds, but they do not remove the moisture-sensitivity risk. Remaining uncertainty is whether the more Y-rich target has a narrower phase field than CS-1.

#### F3 — Route 1

The same YCl₃–YN–Y precursor chemistry in CS-1 gives strong kinetic relevance, but CS-1 required up to 16 days, implying slow diffusion and ordering. YCl₃ may provide halide-assisted transport, but YN and Y metal are refractory enough that complete homogenization may be sluggish [Reasonable Inference supported by CS-1 long anneal]. The target layered P-6m2 structure differs from monoclinic Y₂NCl₃, so the score is moderate rather than high. Insufficient evidence from SS/TS for the exact P-6m2 Y₃N₂Cl₂ reconstruction pathway.

#### F2 — Route 2

Route 2 uses moderate 850–900 °C temperatures based on SS-1, SS-3, TS-1, and TS-3, but its severity is increased by NaN₃ hazard, gas evolution, sealed-ampoule pressure, and flux removal. SS-3 explicitly used slow heating and a 300 °C hold for azide decomposition, which adds process complexity. CS-1 warns that Y–N–Cl products are moisture sensitive, so ordinary aqueous flux washing may be unsafe. Insufficient evidence from CS that azide flux is compatible with Y–N–Cl specifically.

#### F3 — Route 2

Flux and azide routes have better mass transport than direct solid-state reaction, as SS-1/SS-3/TS-1 show single crystals after 7 days at 850–900 °C. TS-3 specifically shows NaCl flux promotes lamellar crystals in a Y-containing layered anion compound, supporting possible growth of the 2D target. However, no provided source demonstrates Y₃N₂Cl₂ or any Y–N–Cl phase from NaN₃ flux, so the structural and chemical selectivity remain uncertain. This gives a good kinetics score but not the best overall route.

#### F2 — Route 3

High-pressure synthesis is operationally challenging even though the rubric only penalizes pressure above 5 GPa; CS-4 and CS-5 used 3–5 GPa, specialized multianvil apparatus, noble-metal capsules, BN cells, graphite heaters, and controlled quenching. The route also uses volatile NH₄Cl/NH₄X fluxes and multiple handling steps, as in CS-4/CS-5. It may require temperatures around or above 1000 °C, especially by analogy with CS-5. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS that Y₃N₂Cl₂ itself requires high pressure.

#### F3 — Route 3

CS-4 and CS-5 show that high pressure plus NH₄X flux can rapidly crystallize layered nitride halides in 1–1.5 h, so diffusion and crystallization kinetics are favorable. This route is attractive if the P-6m2 phase is metastable relative to other Y–N–Cl phases. However, the high-pressure analogues are Zr/Hf MNX compounds rather than reduced Y-rich nitridochlorides, so chemical transferability is incomplete. The kinetic score is therefore high-moderate, but the route is not preferred unless ambient-pressure synthesis fails.

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

The target has only one cation, Y, so cation-radius mismatch does not apply; this is a favorable factor based on the target composition. The likely primitive atom count is small because the formula unit is Y₃N₂Cl₂ and the structure is described as a single 2D sheet in P-6m2, so no large-cell amorphization penalty is applied. The mixed edge/corner/face-sharing connectivity in the target triggers one amorphization-risk point. SS-1/SS-3/TS-1 show that related mixed-connectivity rare-earth nitrides can still crystallize, so the risk is low–medium rather than severe.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Y₃(NCl)₂ is likely a **near-ground-state or mildly metastable** phase because its E_hull is only 0.023 eV/atom, which is well within the experimentally accessible range. The strongest support comes from CS-1, where a chemically very similar Y–N–Cl phase is synthesized from YCl₃, YN, and Y metal in sealed Nb ampoules under conditions directly transferable to the target. SS and TS analogues support the broader feasibility of rare-earth nitride/halide/interstitial layered phases in sealed ampoules and fluxes, but they are less chemically exact. The most critical enabling factor is **strictly controlled reducing, anhydrous sealed-ampoule chemistry** using Y metal to access the Y-rich nitridochloride composition. The main limiting factor is **phase competition and stoichiometry control**, especially between YN, YCl₃-rich phases such as Y₂NCl₃, metallic Y-rich products, and possible oxychloride impurities.
