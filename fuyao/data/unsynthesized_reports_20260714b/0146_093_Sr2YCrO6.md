### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0500 eV/atom, which falls at the high-synthesizability boundary in the specified rubric. CS strongly supports feasibility because several chemically similar Sr double perovskites are on or very near the hull: Sr₂CaMoO₆ and Sr₂YNbO₆ are E_hull = 0 (CS-1, CS-2), and Sr₂ZrTiO₆/Sr₂ZrMnO₆/Sr₂ZnMoO₆ are within ~0.005–0.017 eV/atom (CS-3 to CS-5). TS gives a cautionary but not prohibitive signal because similar Cr oxides span ~0.034–0.084 eV/atom and are experimentally accessible, sometimes only under high pressure (TS-1, TS-2, TS-5). Remaining uncertainty is that the target requires Cr⁵⁺, while many CS examples involve more conventional d⁰/d¹⁰ or mixed transition-metal oxidation states.

#### F2 — Route 1 severity

Route 1 is moderate severity because CS analogues routinely use high-temperature ceramic firing above 1000 °C with repeated regrinding and pelletizing (CS-1, CS-3, CS-4, CS-5). It avoids high pressure, and many related phases form in air (CS-1, CS-4, TS-4). The main severity penalty is the need for multi-step firing and toxic Cr-containing/chromate-like reagents, where toxicity is a [Reasonable Inference] and Cr–Sr chemistry is supported by SS-1/TS-5. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS exists for an exact Sr₂CrYO₆ ceramic protocol, so this route retains uncertainty.

#### F3 — Route 1 kinetics

Kinetics are only moderate because oxide/carbonate precursors are less active than nitrate/sol-gel precursors, and CS examples often require repeated firing: CS-3 reports six 24 h steps and CS-4 requires up to 1400 °C for 48 h. Structural reconstruction is favorable because CS-1, CS-2, CS-4, and CS-5 are perovskite-derived double perovskites, closely matching the target topology. However, B-site ordering between large Y³⁺ and high-valent Cr is likely slow by physicochemical reasoning, and TS-4 shows incomplete B-site ordering can remain even in related double perovskites.

#### F2 — Route 2 severity

Route 2 is severe because high-pressure Cr perovskite synthesis requires specialized equipment: TS-1 uses 9 GPa in a Walker-type press and TS-2 uses 65–70 kbar in a belt-type apparatus with gold capsules. The temperature itself is not extreme compared with ceramic routes, but high pressure and capsule preparation dominate the penalty. Dry-box handling of SrO-type reagents is supported by TS-2. Insufficient evidence from CS exists that high pressure is required for Sr₂CrYO₆, so this route is a backup rather than the primary recommendation.

#### F3 — Route 2 kinetics

High pressure can help stabilize compact Cr perovskite frameworks, as shown by TS-1 and TS-2, but it does not guarantee long-range Y/Cr rock-salt ordering. Precursor activity remains moderate because TS-1 and TS-2 use oxide-type reagents or pre-synthesized chromate precursors rather than highly mixed molecular precursors. The shorter high-pressure holds in TS-1 suggest rapid crystallization can occur, but phase windows are narrow and impurities are reported in TS-2, lowering the kinetics/phase-control score.

#### F2 — Route 3 severity

Route 3 has similar thermal severity to Route 1 but avoids high pressure and should reduce the maximum required temperature. Direct sol-gel evidence is insufficient from CS, SS, and TS, so the route is scored from a [Reasonable Inference] rather than direct precedent. The multi-step nature of gelation, burnout, calcination, and annealing triggers a penalty, as does use of toxic Cr salts by [Reasonable Inference]. The route remains experimentally accessible with standard furnaces and wet-chemistry equipment.

#### F3 — Route 3 kinetics

The nitrate/citrate route receives the best kinetics score because the scoring rubric assigns high precursor activity to nitrate/sol-gel chemistry, and molecular mixing should reduce the long diffusion distances implied by CS-1, CS-3, CS-4, and CS-5. The main remaining kinetic barrier is ordering/crystallization into a triclinic/orthorhombic double perovskite rather than forming amorphous or chromate impurities. TS-4’s incomplete B-site ordering in Sm₂CoMnO₆ supports concern that ordering may remain imperfect after ceramic sintering. Direct sol-gel evidence from CS/SS/TS is insufficient, so the improvement is inferred rather than demonstrated.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has one significant amorphization/ordering risk: a large B-site size mismatch between Y and high-valent Cr, reflected directly in target bond lengths of Y–O ≈2.21–2.23 Å and Cr–O ≈1.88–1.90 Å. However, the structure is a corner-sharing perovskite-derived network, which is commonly crystallizable in CS double perovskites such as Sr₂CaMoO₆ and Sr₂YNbO₆ (CS-1, CS-2). There is insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS that the target has a >40 atom unit cell or mixed edge/face/vertex connectivity. Thus the amorphization risk is low–medium rather than high.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Sr₂CrYO₆ is likely a marginally metastable or near-threshold phase rather than a deep ground-state compound, because its E_hull is exactly 0.0500 eV/atom. The CS dataset strongly supports the general feasibility of Sr-based double perovskite synthesis by ceramic routes, especially Sr₂YNbO₆ and Sr₂ZrMnO₆ analogues (CS-2, CS-4). The TS dataset introduces the main caution: Cr perovskite chemistry can require high oxygen potential or high pressure and can be phase-sensitive, as shown for BaCrO₃, CaCrO₃, and Sr₂CrO₄-related chemistry (TS-1, TS-2, TS-5). The most critical enabling factor is the strong structural compatibility of Sr/Y/Cr/O with a corner-sharing double-perovskite framework, supported by CS-1 to CS-5. The most critical limiting factor is controlling Cr oxidation state near +5 without forming chromate, Cr₂O₃, SrCrO₄, or other competing Sr–Cr–O phases.
