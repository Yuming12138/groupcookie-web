### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target E_hull is 0.0500 eV/atom, which falls at the favorable boundary of the ≤0.05 eV/atom scoring bin, so a high score of 90 is assigned. TS strongly supports that nearby thermodynamic analogues with E_hull values around 0.04–0.076 eV/atom have reported syntheses (TS-1–TS-5). CS also shows Sr double perovskites with very low E_hull values, including near-ground-state Sr₂CoWO₆ and Sr₂CdWO₆ variants (CS-1–CS-4). Remaining uncertainty is that the target may be a metastable low-symmetry polymorph rather than the unique ground-state phase.

#### F2/F3 — Route 1

Route 1 benefits from the strongest chemical precedent: Sr₂CoWO₆ and Sr₂CdWO₆ are synthesized from SrCO₃ and oxide precursors by ceramic reaction in air with regrinding at roughly 900–1100 °C (CS-1–CS-4). The severity score is reduced because the target likely needs >1000 °C, multiple firing cycles, and volatile/toxic Sb oxide handling; Sb-specific evidence is insufficient from CS/SS/TS and this penalty is based on [Reasonable Inference]. Kinetics are moderate because Ta₂O₅ is refractory, but the structure type is highly transferable from the CS double-perovskite analogues.

#### F2/F3 — Route 2

Route 2 is more severe because TS and SS controlled-atmosphere analogues require Ar, vacuum, or sealed ampoules for redox-sensitive oxides (TS-2, TS-3, TS-5, SS-4). This route may be necessary if Sb³⁺ must be preserved or Sb₂O₃ volatility prevents stoichiometric ceramic reaction; that requirement is [Reasonable Inference] because no Sb analogue is provided. Kinetics remain similar to Route 1 because the same refractory oxide diffusion problem remains, although stoichiometry control may be better. The main uncertainty is the correct oxygen fugacity window for Ta⁵⁺/Sb³⁺ coexistence.

#### F2/F3 — Route 3

Route 3 is inferred rather than directly supported by CS/SS/TS, but it addresses the main kinetic bottleneck: diffusion through refractory Ta₂O₅ particles. The high precursor-activity score comes from [Reasonable Inference] that citrate/Pechini mixing gives nanoscale cation homogeneity, while CS repeated-regrinding requirements indicate that conventional powder mixing is kinetically limiting (CS-1–CS-4). Severity is moderate because organics require burnout and Sb/Ta solution chemistry is nontrivial. The largest uncertainty is whether the amorphous precursor crystallizes into the desired ordered Sr₂TaSbO₆ phase rather than competing tantalates or antimonates.

#### F2/F3 — Route 4

Route 4 is supported mainly by TS high-temperature sealed-tube tantalate chemistry, especially the 1450 °C sealed-tube route in TS-3 and slow-cooling tantalate crystallization in TS-4. It is penalized heavily for high temperature, sealed/vacuum conditions, flux handling, and volatile Sb control. Kinetically, flux transport may help, but the lack of a direct Sr₂TaSbO₆ flux precedent from CS/SS/TS makes phase selectivity uncertain. This route is better suited for exploratory crystal growth than scalable powder synthesis.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

Only one of the three amorphization checks is clearly triggered: the large A-site/B-site cation size difference, consistent with perovskite chemistry and the large Sr–O versus shorter Ta–O/Sb–O coordination environments described for the target and CS analogues. The target description does not indicate a unit cell exceeding 40 atoms, and its B-site octahedra are described as corner-sharing rather than mixed edge/face/vertex-sharing. CS analogues do contain Sr polyhedral face sharing, but the target’s key TaO₆/SbO₆ network is corner-sharing, so C3 is not counted. The amorphization risk is therefore low to medium.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Sr₂TaSbO₆ is likely **near-metastable or marginally stable**, because its E_hull is exactly 0.0500 eV/atom, near the conventional synthesizability threshold. CS analogues strongly support the feasibility of Sr₂BB′O₆ double-perovskite synthesis by ceramic routes in air, while TS supports that thermodynamically similar Ta-containing oxides can be synthesized even at comparable hull energies. The most important enabling factor is the robust Sr-based double-perovskite framework demonstrated in CS-1–CS-4. The most important limiting factor is control of Sb stoichiometry and oxidation state, for which CS/SS/TS provide insufficient direct evidence and which must be managed by precursor choice, atmosphere, and possible Sb excess. Overall, synthesis is plausible but will likely require optimization rather than a single direct firing.
