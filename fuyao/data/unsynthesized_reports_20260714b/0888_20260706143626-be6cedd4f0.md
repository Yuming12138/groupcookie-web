### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target is reported at **0.0000 eV/atom above hull**, so the assigned F1 score is high. Thermodynamic analogues in TS include several stable tellurides at E_hull = 0.0, including CsUZrTe5, Rb2ZrTe3, K2ZrTe3, CsUCuTe3, and Ti3Te4 (TS-1 to TS-5), reinforcing that complex alkali/transition-metal tellurides can be ground-state phases. CS also supports stability because CsMo6Te7-type and CsUZrTe5-type analogues are at E_hull = 0.0 (CS-1, CS-2), while SS is mixed because VTe has E_hull ≈0.150 eV/atom but V3Te4 is near-stable at ≈0.014 eV/atom (SS-1, SS-3). Remaining uncertainty is synthetic, not thermodynamic: the hull value does not guarantee a wide phase field.

#### F2/F3 — Route 1: direct solid-state reaction

Condition severity is high because direct reaction likely requires evacuated sealed tubes, volatile Te/Cs containment, and repeated annealing; these features are explicitly present in CS-1, SS-1, SS-3, and TS-5. Kinetics are only moderate-to-poor because V metal is refractory and SS-3 requires pressing, reheating, two-week annealing, and quenching to make V3Te4. Structural reconstruction from elemental or binary precursors into Cs3V9Te13 is substantial [Reasonable Inference], and competing V–Te binaries are plausible because SS-1 and SS-3 show stable V–Te phases. Insufficient evidence from CS alone to identify a direct Cs–V–Te solid-state product, so this route is feasible but not preferred.

#### F2/F3 — Route 2: reactive Cs–Te flux

This route has moderate condition severity because Cs2Te3 flux analogues use sealed tubes, Ar/vacuum handling, high but sub-1000 °C temperatures, and slow cooling (CS-2, CS-4, TS-1). Kinetics are better than direct sintering because flux/liquid transport can dissolve and redistribute Cs, Te, and metal species; this is supported by CsUZrTe5 and Cs2Mn3Te4 flux growth (CS-2, CS-4). The main uncertainty is phase selectivity: CS-2 notes crystal-growth limitations, and TS-4 mentions mixed products in some Cs–metal–Te flux reactions. Nevertheless, this is the best-supported route because both chemical similarity and thermodynamic similarity include Cs–metal–Te phases made under closely related flux conditions.

#### F2/F3 — Route 3: stoichiometric melt growth

Severity is comparable to flux growth because sealed vacuum ampoules and alkali telluride handling are required, as in CS-5. Kinetics may be acceptable because a melt improves transport, but composition control is more difficult than in a flux because Cs and Te volatility can shift stoichiometry [Reasonable Inference]. CS-5 supports slow-cooled stoichiometric melts for several alkali transition-metal tellurides, but it also reports that some Cs analogues did not yield suitable crystals. Insufficient evidence from SS and TS for melt growth of the exact Cs–V–Te composition makes this a secondary route.

#### F2/F3 — Route 4: ion exchange

Ion exchange has lower temperature severity because CS-3 performs alkali exchange near the melting temperature of alkali halides rather than at >1000 °C. However, kinetic scoring is limited by lack of evidence for a suitable V–Te parent framework; insufficient evidence from SS and TS supports a direct precursor for Cs3V9Te13. CS-3 demonstrates the method for CsCr5Te8-type chalcogenides, so the concept is chemically plausible, but transferability to a Cs3V9Te13 framework is uncertain. Toxicity or handling risks may be significant if a Tl-containing parent is required [Reasonable Inference based on CS-3].

#### F4 — Amorphization / structural complexity

The target contains very different cation environments, with large 10-coordinate Cs sites and smaller 5/6-coordinate V–Te sites; this triggers cation-size mismatch risk [Reasonable Inference from target structure]. The target also has mixed corner- and face-sharing octahedral connectivity, and similar mixed connectivity is common in SS-1, SS-3, CS-3, and TS-5. The unit formula has 25 atoms, and no Z value is provided, so there is insufficient evidence to trigger the >40 atom unit-cell criterion. The resulting F4 score reflects medium risk: crystallization should be possible, but long annealing or flux-mediated ordering is likely needed.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Cs3V9Te13 is likely a **ground-state or very near-ground-state phase** because its reported energy above hull is 0.0000 eV/atom, and many related Cs/Rb/K/U/Zr/Ti tellurides are also hull-stable in CS/TS analogues (CS-1, CS-2, TS-1 to TS-5). The strongest synthetic support comes from Cs–metal–Te reactive flux chemistry using Cs2Te3 and sometimes CsCl, which has produced complex crystalline tellurides under sealed-tube slow-cooling conditions (CS-2, CS-4, TS-1, TS-4). Structurally, V–Te octahedral networks are well precedented in VTe and V3Te4, although those binaries require long annealing and careful thermal treatment (SS-1, SS-3). The most critical enabling factor is maintaining high Cs/Te chemical activity in a sealed flux, which should help overcome refractory V diffusion [Reasonable Inference supported by CS-2 and CS-4]. The most critical limiting factor is phase competition between Cs–Te flux phases and V–Te binaries, especially because V–Te phases are readily formed under similar conditions (SS-1, SS-3).
