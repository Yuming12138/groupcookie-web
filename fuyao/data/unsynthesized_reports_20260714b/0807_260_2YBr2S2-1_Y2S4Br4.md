### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0440 eV/atom, which is below the requested 0.05 eV/atom high-score threshold and suggests a realistic metastable synthesis window. CS supports this because the isostructural Nb(SBr)₂ analogue is nearly on the hull, E_hull = 0.0008 eV/atom (CS1), and another rare-earth sulfobromide analogue has E_hull = 0.0189 eV/atom (CS4). TS supports the idea that materials in the 0.03–0.06 eV/atom range are experimentally accessible through sealed-tube or hydrothermal routes (TS1–TS3, TS5). SS provides insufficient direct thermodynamic evidence for Y–S–Br chemistry, so SS is treated as neutral for F1.

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 is mild in temperature because the closest analogue Nb(SBr)₂ forms at 500 °C in 2 days (CS1). The main severity penalties are volatile Br₂/S in a sealed system and toxic/corrosive Br₂, with optional Cd adding toxicity (CS1). No high pressure, >1000 °C temperature, or inherently multi-step protocol is required by the direct analogue (CS1). The remaining uncertainty is whether Y requires more stringent atmosphere control than Nb, which is a **[Reasonable Inference]** based on rare-earth halide moisture sensitivity and air alteration in CS4.

#### F3 — Route 1

Kinetically, Route 1 benefits from reactive sulfur and bromine, and the exact layered M(SBr)₂ framework is already demonstrated for Nb(SBr)₂ (CS1). The limiting kinetic factor is conversion of Y metal, which may be slower than volatile/reactive anion transport **[Reasonable Inference]**. CS1’s 2-day synthesis suggests the framework can form without extremely long annealing, but CS1 also reports simultaneous polymorphs, implying some phase-selection uncertainty. SS and TS broadly support sealed-tube solid-state chalcogenide synthesis but do not directly resolve Y(SBr)₂ kinetics (SS1–SS3, TS1–TS4).

#### F2 — Route 2

Route 2 requires evacuated sealed tubes and a controlled temperature gradient, both directly supported by NbX₂Y₂ CVT and CrSBr CVT syntheses (CS2, CS5). The temperature remains moderate, around 400–600 °C for Nb chalcogenide-halide transport (CS2), so no high-temperature penalty applies. The penalties come from vacuum/controlled atmosphere, volatile halogen transport, multi-step gradient growth, and toxic/air-sensitive halide handling (CS2, CS5). SS2 also supports vapor transport for layered sulfides but does not provide direct Y–Br transferability.

#### F3 — Route 2

CVT should have favorable mass transport because volatile halide species can carry material across the ampoule, as demonstrated for NbX₂Y₂ compounds (CS2) and CrSBr (CS5). This route is kinetically stronger than purely binary solid-state annealing because it reduces long-range diffusion limitations. However, CS2 explicitly notes polymorph/stoichiometry sensitivity and phase-control difficulty in NbX₂Y₂ systems, so the kinetics are good but the crystallization window may be narrow. Transfer to Y remains uncertain because rare-earth bromides may transport differently from Nb halides **[Reasonable Inference]**.

#### F2 — Route 3

Flux growth occurs at modest temperature because Nd sulfobromide crystallization in NdBr₃ flux occurs near 700 K / 427 °C (CS4). Severity arises from anhydrous rare-earth bromide handling, sealed or inert conditions, flux separation, and possible use of Br₂/S vapor **[Reasonable Inference]**. SS4 confirms that molten halide salts can grow halide-containing crystals, but it also shows multiple handling steps and moisture-sensitive/corrosive melt issues. Thus, the route is not thermally severe but operationally demanding.

#### F3 — Route 3

The molten bromide flux should enhance ion mobility and reduce the diffusion barrier compared with solid Y metal or Y₂S₃ powders **[Reasonable Inference]**. CS4 provides direct chemical support that a rare-earth sulfobromide can crystallize from a rare-earth bromide flux. The weakness is structural specificity: the Nd phase in CS4 is not the same Y(SBr)₂ topology or stoichiometry, so the flux may stabilize alternative rare-earth sulfobromides. This uncertainty lowers the structural-reconstruction component.

#### F2 — Route 4

Binary-precursor annealing avoids direct liquid Br₂ if YBr₃ is used, but it still requires sealed ampoules, inert/vacuum handling, and likely repeated annealing. CS3, TS3, and TS4 show that binary halide/chalcogenide routes often use sealed tubes and controlled thermal profiles. The route is moderate in temperature but multi-step and air/moisture sensitive. No direct source proves binary Y₂S₃/YBr₃ conversion to Y(SBr)₂, so this route carries the largest extrapolation.

#### F3 — Route 4

The kinetic score is lowest because Y₂S₃ and YBr₃ are expected to be comparatively stable ionic binaries, and reconstructing them into a layered mixed-anion S–S/Br phase may be slow **[Reasonable Inference]**. Long annealing and careful thermal cycling are common in binary solid-state syntheses such as CS3, TS3, and TS4. The route may improve stoichiometric control but risks trapping stable binaries. SS and TS analogues support feasibility of sealed anneals but provide insufficient direct evidence for this target topology.

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

The target has only one cation, Y, so the cation-radius mismatch check is not triggered by the target composition. The described Y(SBr)₂ sheet is simple and does not indicate a >40-atom unit cell or mixed vertex/edge/face-sharing polyhedral network; the isostructural Nb(SBr)₂ analogue likewise has a simple layered C2/m arrangement (CS1). SS analogues include more complex layered sulfides, but they are not direct evidence of amorphization risk for Y(SBr)₂ (SS1–SS3). The main uncertainty is not amorphization but phase competition and stoichiometry control.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Y(SBr)₂ is likely a low-energy metastable phase rather than a strict ground-state phase because its E_hull is 0.0440 eV/atom, below the common metastability threshold but not zero. The strongest support comes from CS1, where the isostructural Nb(SBr)₂ phase is synthesized directly at 500 °C in a sealed tube; CS2 and CS5 further support halogen-assisted vapor/transport synthesis of layered chalcogenide halides. The most important enabling factor is the very close structural and chemical precedent of M(SBr)₂ layered sheets in CS1. The most important limiting factor is that Y may prefer more ionic Y³⁺ sulfide/bromide products, making the electron count and phase selectivity less straightforward than in Nb(SBr)₂ **[Reasonable Inference]**. Overall, synthesis is plausible, especially by sealed-tube elemental reaction, but phase-pure material may require careful Br/S chemical-potential tuning and moisture-free handling.
