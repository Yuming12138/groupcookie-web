### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target Eₕull is 0.047 eV/atom, placing it at the top of the requested 90–100 scoring range. CS includes experimentally synthesized chemically similar sulfides up to 0.151 eV/atom (CS2) and near-hull/high-pressure sulfides around 0.019–0.053 eV/atom (CS1, CS5), indicating that comparable metastabilities can be experimentally accessible. SS analogues are mostly stable or near-stable P–S frameworks (SS1–SS5), and TS analogues include synthesized materials in a similar thermodynamic range, e.g., GeS₂ at 0.055 eV/atom (TS1) and K₂Fe(PS₃)₂ at 0.070 eV/atom (TS3). Remaining uncertainty is that the target is a specific binary P₂S₅ polymorph, so thermodynamic accessibility does not guarantee phase selectivity.

#### F2/F3 — Route 1: Direct sealed-tube solid-state

Route 1 receives moderate F2 because it avoids high pressure and very high temperature, but requires vacuum sealing and volatile P/S handling; this is consistent with SS1–SS3 and TS3, where P–S chalcogenides are synthesized in evacuated or sealed ampoules. Its F3 is favorable because only P and S must diffuse/react, and SS1–SS3 show P–S frameworks form under sealed-tube conditions, but structural ordering may require days to weeks as in SS2–SS3. CS3 warns that P–S chemistry can produce competing molecular/polymorphic phases, so slow ramping and careful stoichiometry are needed. Insufficient evidence from TS for the exact binary P₂S₅ phase, so TS contributes general sealed-tube support rather than exact phase guidance.

#### F2/F3 — Route 2: CVT/temperature-gradient growth

Route 2 has lower F2 than Route 1 because it adds a gradient, possible transport agent, and multi-step prereaction/loading; SS1 and TS2 show that such CVT setups are experimentally standard but require control. Its F3 is moderate-to-good because vapor transport can bypass slow bulk diffusion, as shown for TiP₂S₆ in SS1 and MnIn₂Se₄ in TS2. The main kinetic/phase uncertainty is composition control: TS2 explicitly notes optimization to avoid multiphase condensation, and P/S volatility makes this worse by inference. Insufficient evidence from CS for CVT of binary P₂S₅ specifically; CS mainly supports P–S chemical similarity and phase sensitivity.

#### F2/F3 — Route 3: High-pressure quench

Route 3 is penalized heavily in F2 because CS4–CS5 and TS1 require specialized high-pressure apparatus, BN containers, graphite heaters, and precise pressure/temperature programs. Its F3 remains reasonable because high pressure can rapidly crystallize alternative tetrahedral sulfide polymorphs, as shown by SiS₂ phases forming in 15 min in CS4–CS5. However, TS1 emphasizes narrow high-pressure phase windows and difficulty growing certain single crystals, which lowers confidence in phase selectivity. This route is best treated as a rescue route if ambient-pressure sealed-tube synthesis gives only competing P–S phases.

#### F2/F3 — Route 4: Solution crystallization

Route 4 has moderate F2 because temperatures are low, but it requires inert, dry solution handling and potentially hazardous P–S solvents/precursors; CS3 and TS5 support solution crystallization/diffusion under controlled conditions. Its F3 is mixed: solution diffusion is kinetically favorable, but the structural-reconstruction score is low because CS3 demonstrates molecular P₄S₃ crystallization rather than a 2D P₂S₅ network. Insufficient evidence from SS for solution formation of analogous P₂S₅ sheets, and insufficient evidence from TS for binary P–S solution crystallization; therefore this route is exploratory despite a moderate numerical score.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has only one cation element, P, so the cation-radius mismatch check does not trigger. The structure description indicates corner-sharing PS₄ tetrahedra in a 2D sheet, with no mixed edge/face-sharing connectivity, so the polyhedral-complexity check does not trigger. CS4–CS5 and TS1 show that tetrahedral chalcogenide networks can have multiple crystalline polymorphs rather than necessarily amorphizing, but TS1 also shows glass precursors can be used for GeS₂ high-pressure synthesis. Remaining uncertainty is that P₂S₅ may form glassy or molecular P–S byproducts if quenched too fast or off-stoichiometric.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

The target P₂S₅ phase is likely a near-hull metastable phase rather than a robust ground state, because its Eₕull is 0.047 eV/atom. CS, SS, and TS analogues collectively support synthesis of P–S/chalcogenide frameworks in sealed ampoules, with SS1–SS3 giving the strongest transferability because they directly involve P/S framework formation from elements. The most important enabling factor is the high chemical reactivity and volatility of P and S, which can promote reaction at moderate temperature in sealed systems, consistent with SS1 and SS3. The main limiting factor is phase selectivity: CS3 shows P–S polymorph/molecular phase competition, and SS4–SS5 show impurity/non-homogeneity issues in related P–S sulfide syntheses. Overall, the material is synthesizable with moderate difficulty, with direct sealed-tube synthesis as the best first attempt and CVT as the best crystal-growth refinement.
