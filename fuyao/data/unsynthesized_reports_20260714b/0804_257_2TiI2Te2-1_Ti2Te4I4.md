### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target’s energy above hull is **0.0470 eV/atom**, which places it just inside the highest stability bin specified in the prompt. This suggests it may be either a near-ground-state compound or a low-energy metastable phase; the latter is plausible because several structurally similar Te–I phases include metastable or narrow-window phases in SS-1 to SS-3. CS analogues include multiple E_hull = 0 compounds, but those are not Ti-containing, so CS provides general support for stable layered telluride halides but insufficient evidence from CS for the exact Ti–Te–I thermodynamic landscape. TS analogues include materials near comparable metastability, such as UTe₅ at E_hull ≈0.046 eV/atom (TS-2), supporting that phases in this energy range can be experimentally accessed.

#### F2 — Route 1: Sealed-tube direct solid-state reaction

Route 1 is penalized for vacuum/sealed-tube conditions because CS-1, SS-4, and TS-5 all use evacuated or sealed ampoules. It is also penalized for volatile iodine/tellurium-containing components because SS-4 and TS-5 rely on sealed iodine-containing environments, and iodine volatility is a standard physicochemical concern [Reasonable Inference]. It avoids a >1000 °C penalty if operated at 650–900 °C, but the higher 1073 K condition in SS-4 shows that demanding thermal treatment may be required for refractory transition-metal chalcogenide iodides. Remaining uncertainty is whether Ti reacts sufficiently without forming TiI₄ or binary Ti–Te impurities.

#### F3 — Route 1: Sealed-tube direct solid-state reaction

Kinetics are the main weakness for Route 1 because elemental Ti is refractory and surface-passivated [Reasonable Inference]. SS-4 demonstrates that an elemental Nb/Se/I reaction can make a related metal chalcogenide iodide, but it requires two weeks plus quenching/reheating/slow cooling and explicitly notes difficulty preparing phase-pure samples. TS-5 shows iodine can accelerate ampoule reactions, which partially offsets the solid-state diffusion barrier. Overall, the route is feasible but likely slow and phase-sensitive.

#### F2 — Route 2: Acidic hydrothermal synthesis

Route 2 has low thermal severity because SS-1 to SS-3 use 195–280 °C hydrothermal conditions and SS-5 uses 220 °C. It is penalized for sealed volatile/corrosive HI/I₂ conditions and toxic reagents, supported by the concentrated HI hydrothermal setups in SS-1 to SS-5. There is no high-pressure penalty under the prompt’s >5 GPa criterion because hydrothermal autogenous pressure is far below that threshold [Reasonable Inference]. The main uncertainty is not the Te–I chemistry, which is well supported, but Ti compatibility in strongly acidic iodide media.

#### F3 — Route 2: Acidic hydrothermal synthesis

Hydrothermal HI conditions are kinetically favorable for Te–I substructure formation, as SS-1 to SS-3 state Te subiodides are best obtained in acidic hydrothermal media. SS-5 further shows concentrated HI can produce halogenated chalcogenide crystals in 5 days with controlled cooling. However, insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS directly demonstrates Ti incorporation under HI hydrothermal conditions, and Ti may passivate or hydrolyze [Reasonable Inference]. The score is therefore high but not maximal.

#### F2 — Route 3: Chemical vapor transport

Route 3 requires evacuated sealed quartz/silica tubes, directly supported by TS-3 and TS-4, so it receives the controlled-atmosphere penalty. It also uses volatile iodine or Te–I species, consistent with TS-3 using iodine transport agent and TS-4 using a chalcogen halide precursor. A multistep penalty is applied because pre-synthesis of TiTe₂ or related binary precursor is likely needed [Reasonable Inference]. Thermal severity is moderate because TS-3/TS-4 operate in the 350–500 °C range, far below 1000 °C.

#### F3 — Route 3: Chemical vapor transport

CVT offers favorable kinetics because vapor transport bypasses long-range solid-state diffusion, as demonstrated for TS-3 and TS-4. TS-4 is especially relevant because MoTe₄Br forms from MoTe₂ and Te₂Br, suggesting a binary telluride plus chalcogen-halide strategy can build layered metal telluride halides. For Ti(TeI)₂, vapor speciation and phase selectivity are uncertain; insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS directly establishes stable Ti–Te–I transport chemistry. The route is best viewed as a crystal-growth route after precursor/phase chemistry is mapped.

#### F2 — Route 4: Halide flux growth

Route 4 is penalized for vacuum/Ar sealed ampoules, long multistep flux handling, and volatile/toxic halogen components. These requirements are supported by CS-2 and CS-5, where fluxes are dried, loaded under controlled atmospheres, sealed in quartz ampoules, heated for ~1 week, slowly cooled, and washed. The route does not require extreme temperature or high pressure, since CS-2/CS-5 use ~700 °C / 970 K. Uncertainty remains because no Ti–Te–I flux analogue is directly provided.

#### F3 — Route 4: Halide flux growth

Flux growth improves kinetics by providing a liquid medium and enabling slow crystallization, as shown for layered LnSeTe₂ and PrSeTe₂ in CS-2 and CS-5. Very slow cooling in CS-2/CS-5 supports good crystal quality but also indicates sensitivity to nucleation/growth conditions. Because Ti(TeI)₂ contains a volatile iodide component and refractory Ti, flux may help but composition control could be difficult [Reasonable Inference]. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS directly confirms Ti solubility and target phase stability in chloride/iodide flux.

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

The target has only one cation, Ti, so the cation-size mismatch criterion is not triggered [Reasonable Inference]. The reported structure is a 2D C2/m sheet with Ti, Te, and I sites, but the prompt does not indicate >40 atoms per unit cell or mixed vertex/edge/face-sharing polyhedral networks. Several analogues in CS and SS are low-dimensional crystalline telluride halides, supporting low amorphization tendency, though insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS gives a direct amorphization assessment for Ti(TeI)₂. The main structural risk is not glass formation but competing crystalline iodotelluride or titanium iodide phases.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Ti(TeI)₂ is likely a **near-ground-state or mildly metastable phase**, because its E_hull is 0.0470 eV/atom, just below the prompt’s 0.05 eV/atom high-stability threshold. CS and SS analogues strongly support the general feasibility of crystalline telluride iodide or chalcogenide halide phases, especially through hydrothermal HI chemistry (SS-1 to SS-5) and sealed-tube halogen chemistry (CS-1, SS-4, TS-5). TS analogues show that compounds with comparable metastability can be synthesized, including UTe₅ with E_hull ≈0.046 eV/atom by CVT (TS-2). The most critical enabling factor is the use of iodide-rich transport or mineralizing conditions, which promote mobility and crystallization at moderate temperatures. The most critical limiting factor is Ti chemistry: Ti is refractory in the elemental state and can form stable titanium iodides or hydrolysis/oxide impurities, and insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS directly demonstrates Ti–Te–I phase-selective synthesis.
