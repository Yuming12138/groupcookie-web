### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target has E above hull = 0.0190 eV/atom, placing it well within the ≤0.05 eV/atom high-synthesizability window. TS-1 and TS-2 show experimentally synthesized chalcohalide analogues with similar metastabilities near 0.028 eV/atom, supporting that this energy range is accessible. CS gives mixed thermodynamic signals: CS-1 is on the hull and easy, while CS-3/CS-4 CoSbS polymorphs near 0.01–0.04 eV/atom required high pressure; however, those are chemically less transferable because they involve Co frameworks and high-pressure polymorphism. Remaining uncertainty is whether SbSCl is a true low-temperature phase or a metastable polymorph requiring kinetic trapping.

#### F2/F3 — Route 1: Sealed-Ampoule Solid-State

F2 is moderate because TS-4 demonstrates Sb-containing chalcohalides can form at 400–430 °C in vacuum-sealed quartz tubes, avoiding high pressure and >1000 °C conditions. The deductions come from vacuum sealing, volatile halide/chalcogen components, and toxic/moisture-sensitive SbCl₃, consistent with dry sealed halide chemistry in SS-1/TS-1 and TS-4. F3 is favorable relative to oxide ceramics because SbCl₃ is reactive and Sb has relatively low melting point [Reasonable Inference], while TS-4 shows intermediate-temperature reaction is enough for related frameworks. The uncertainty is phase competition with Sb₂S₃ or SbCl₃-rich molecular phases.

#### F2/F3 — Route 2: CVT / Vapor Growth

F2 is lower than Route 1 because CVT requires dry evacuated ampoules, volatile transport agents, temperature gradients, and multiple handling steps, all directly supported by SS-1/TS-1/TS-3 and SS-4/TS-2. F3 is high because vapor transport reduces diffusion limitations and has produced heavy-pnictogen chalcohalide ribbons such as BiSeI (TS-5). The route is particularly attractive for single crystals rather than bulk powder. The main uncertainty is whether SbSCl has sufficient vapor-transport stability without decomposing to SbCl₃/Sb₂S₃ or forming chloride-rich phases.

#### F2/F3 — Route 3: Acidic Solution Precipitation

F2 is the best because CS-1 demonstrates chemically similar main-group mixed halide formation at room temperature by simple dropwise addition, and SS-3/SS-5 demonstrate antimony halides crystallize from solution under mild conditions. F3 is strong because solution chemistry avoids solid-state diffusion, but structural reconstruction/nucleation is the limiting kinetic factor. CS-1 explicitly reports stoichiometry-sensitive mixed phases, and SS-3/SS-5 show polymorph contamination in antimony halide crystallization, so phase control is not trivial. The route remains the most promising if high chloride activity and slow sulfide release are maintained.

#### F2/F3 — Route 4: Solvothermal / Hydrothermal

F2 is moderate because the route uses sealed autogenous pressure, toxic Sb and sulfide reagents, and multiple steps, though not high pressure in the CS-3/CS-4 sense. F3 is strong because solvothermal dissolution–reprecipitation should bypass diffusion barriers [Reasonable Inference], and mild solution analogues in CS-1/CS-2 support low-temperature reactivity. However, CS/SS/TS provide insufficient direct hydrothermal evidence for SbSCl, so this route is less certain than Route 3. It is best viewed as a crystallinity-improving variant if ambient precipitation gives amorphous or poorly crystalline product.

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

The target is compositionally simple with only one cation, so there is no cation-size mismatch trigger; CS/SS/TS do not provide contrary evidence. The described structure is a simple 1D ribbon with Sb coordinated to S and Cl, not a large mixed-polyhedral network, so the amorphization risk is low. SS-2 and TS-5 demonstrate related 1D ribbon compounds can be obtained as crystals, supporting a low amorphization tendency. The residual uncertainty is that rapid sulfide precipitation from solution could produce amorphous Sb₂S₃-like material, but that is a route-specific kinetic issue rather than an intrinsic structural amorphization tendency.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

SbSCl is likely a **near-ground-state or weakly metastable** compound because its E above hull is only 0.0190 eV/atom, comparable to experimentally synthesized thermodynamic analogues in TS-1 and TS-2. The analogues support synthesis well: CS-1 supports mild solution formation of main-group mixed halides, SS-3/SS-5 support antimony halide crystallization from solution, and TS-4/TS-5 support sealed chalcohalide routes for related low-dimensional materials. The most critical enabling factor is the soft, polarizable Sb(III)–S/Cl chemistry, which should allow low-temperature crystallization rather than refractory solid-state diffusion [Reasonable Inference supported by CS-1 and TS-4]. The most critical limiting factor is phase selectivity: excess sulfide may form Sb₂S₃, excess chloride may leave SbCl₃-rich or molecular halide phases, and water may hydrolyze SbCl₃ [Reasonable Inference; phase sensitivity supported by CS-1 and SS-3/SS-5]. Overall, the target appears highly synthesizable, with solution precipitation or chloride-rich solvothermal crystallization as the most practical first attempts.
