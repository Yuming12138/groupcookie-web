### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0500 eV/atom, which falls at the ≤0.05 eV/atom threshold and therefore receives 90/100 by the provided rubric. CS and SS analogues are mostly ground-state or nearly ground-state phases, including CS1, CS2, CS3, SS1, SS2, SS3, and SS4 at 0.0 eV/atom. TS analogues show that phases with comparable or higher metastability, such as TS1 at 0.0588 eV/atom, TS2 at 0.0698 eV/atom, and TS5 at 0.0956 eV/atom, have reported syntheses. Remaining uncertainty is that none of CS/SS/TS directly reports NaLaSbTeO6.

#### F2 — Route 1 solid-state in air

The severity is moderate because CS1 made a close Na–La–Te oxide in air at only 973 K for 6 h, and CS4/SS4 show related oxides can be made without high pressure or exotic atmospheres. Deductions are assigned for multistep firing/regrinding, supported by CS4 and SS4, and for toxic Sb oxide precursors by **[Reasonable Inference]**. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS exists for exact Sb volatility under these target conditions, so no sealed-system deduction is applied to the base open-air route.

#### F3 — Route 1 solid-state in air

Kinetics are favorable compared with many refractory oxides because CS1 used nitrate/hydroxide precursors and achieved reaction at 700 °C. However, the target’s four-cation P1 arrangement requires more cation ordering than CS1’s perovskite-derived analogue, and SS4 shows triclinic oxide synthesis may require week-long heating with intermediate grindings. The main uncertainty is whether Sb and Te enter the desired mixed framework rather than forming binary antimonates/tellurites.

#### F2 — Route 2 hydrothermal

Hydrothermal severity is low to moderate because CS2/CS3/SS2/SS3/SS5 all use 230 °C, 4-day autoclave conditions rather than high-temperature or high-pressure solid-state conditions. Only the toxic Sb source deduction is applied by **[Reasonable Inference]**. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS exists for needing H2, NH3, vacuum, or >5 GPa pressure, so those deductions are not applied.

#### F3 — Route 2 hydrothermal

Hydrothermal kinetics are strong because solution transport bypasses solid-state diffusion, and CS2/CS3 demonstrate alkali rare-earth tellurites crystallizing directly at 230 °C. SS1 shows alkaline hydrothermal conditions crystallize SrTeO3, while SS5 shows related tellurium oxides can form hydrothermally with controlled cooling. The uncertainty is Sb speciation and solubility, because CS/SS/TS do not include a direct Sb-bearing hydrothermal analogue.

#### F2 — Route 3 sealed ampoule

Sealed-ampoule synthesis is more severe because TS5 and TS2 require evacuated silica ampoules/tubes. The route also uses a sealed system specifically to manage volatile Na/Te/Sb components, which is a **[Reasonable Inference]** from the chemistry and from TS5’s use of sealed ampoules for tellurium oxyhalides. Its advantage is volatility control, but the equipment and safety burden are higher than Route 1 or Route 2.

#### F3 — Route 3 sealed ampoule

Kinetics are weaker because low-temperature sealed annealing reduces mass transport, and TS5 required 96 h even for a related tellurium oxyhalide. TS5 also reports phase-control issues such as stoichiometry deviations and extra diffraction lines for related compounds. Therefore, this route is best viewed as a volatility-control backup, not the first-choice route.

#### F2 — Route 4 flux/nitrate-flux

The flux route avoids very high temperature and high pressure, and CS1 provides direct support for nitrate-containing low-temperature reaction mixtures. It receives deductions for multistep processing and Sb toxicity by **[Reasonable Inference]**. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS exists that a sealed tube is mandatory for the nitrate-flux variant, so sealed-system volatility is not deducted for the base version.

#### F3 — Route 4 flux/nitrate-flux

Kinetics should be good because molten nitrate or salt flux improves ion mobility, consistent with CS1’s nitrate-assisted synthesis and TS1’s successful flux growth. Slow cooling can assist crystallization, supported by TS1 and by controlled cooling in CS2/CS3. The main risk is composition control, because Na-containing flux could over-stabilize Na-rich byproducts; this is a **[Reasonable Inference]** rather than directly shown in CS/SS/TS.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has large cation size and bonding differences, visible in the target’s Na–O, La–O, Sb–O, and Te–O bond-length ranges, so C1 is triggered. C2 is not triggered because the listed P1 model contains one of each cation and six O sites, and there is insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS for a >40-atom cell. C3 is also not triggered because the target description does not explicitly identify simultaneous vertex-, edge-, and face-sharing connectivity; insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS is treated neutrally.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

NaLaSbTeO6 is likely a near-threshold metastable phase rather than an unambiguous ground-state phase, because its E_hull is exactly 0.0500 eV/atom. The CS and SS analogues support synthesis well for Na/La/Te oxides and alkali rare-earth tellurites, especially CS1 and CS2/CS3, while TS analogues show phases with similar metastability can be experimentally made. The most important enabling factor is the availability of low-temperature solution or nitrate/flux transport routes, supported by CS2/CS3/SS5 and CS1. The most important limiting factor is phase and oxidation-state control for the combined Sb–Te oxide network, since no CS/SS/TS source directly demonstrates the full Na–La–Sb–Te–O chemistry. Overall, the target appears reasonably synthesizable, with hydrothermal screening followed by solid-state/flux optimization as the best strategy.
