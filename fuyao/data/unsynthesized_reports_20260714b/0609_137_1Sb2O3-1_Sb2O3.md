### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic Stability

The target E_hull is 0.0290 eV/atom, which falls in the ≤0.05 eV/atom scoring bin and therefore supports a high thermodynamic synthesizability score. TS2 and TS3 show that oxide polymorphs with comparable low positive hull energies, such as metastable Bi₂O₃ phases at 0.0386 and 0.0165 eV/atom, can be synthesized, though sometimes with narrow stability windows. CS1/SS3 show that Sb₂O₃ itself has a stable experimentally accessible polymorph, but they do not confirm the target Cm sheet phase. Remaining uncertainty is whether the monoclinic Cm phase is a quenchable metastable polymorph or an artifact of finite-temperature/DFT energetics.

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 has moderate severity because CS1/SS3 require only heating above 570 °C under N₂ in a sublimation setup, not high pressure or extreme temperature. The main penalties are Sb₂O₃ volatility, directly evidenced by the sublimation method in CS1/SS3, and Sb₂O₃ toxicity by standard chemical safety knowledge. Insufficient evidence from TS for this exact vapor route is treated as neutral, because TS does not provide a directly comparable Sb₂O₃ sublimation example.

#### F3 — Route 1

Kinetics are favorable for mass transport because CS1/SS3 directly demonstrate Sb₂O₃ sublimation and redeposition. However, crystallization into the target Cm polymorph is uncertain because the reported product is orthorhombic valentinite rather than monoclinic Cm Sb₂O₃. Composition control should be simpler than multication oxides because the vapor and target share Sb₂O₃ stoichiometry, but volatility makes gradients likely.

#### F2 — Route 2

Route 2 has the mildest condition severity because SS1, SS2, and CS2 demonstrate relevant hydrothermal chemistry at 190–300 °C without GPa pressure or reactive gas. The only scoring deduction is the toxicity of Sb₂O₃, which is the needed Sb source in SS1/SS2. Insufficient evidence from TS for this exact pure Sb₂O₃ hydrothermal polymorph is neutral, although TS3 supports hydrothermal access to metastable Bi₂O₃.

#### F3 — Route 2

Hydrothermal kinetics are strong because SS1/SS2 show Sb₂O₃ can dissolve/recrystallize into crystalline Sb–O frameworks, and CS2 shows water-assisted pnictogen sesquioxide polymorph conversion. Solution-mediated transport reduces diffusion barriers relative to solid-state annealing. The main uncertainty is phase selectivity: SS1/SS2 products incorporate Sr or Na, so pure Sb₂O₃ requires suppressing foreign-cation incorporation.

#### F2 — Route 3

Route 3 uses moderate temperatures, 500–700 °C, consistent with SS5/TS1 and close to the Sb₂O₃ sublimation/inversion range in CS1/SS3. It avoids GPa pressure and can use N₂ rather than vacuum, but Sb₂O₃ volatility at these temperatures and Sb toxicity cause deductions. SS4 shows vacuum ampoules can be used for related antimonites, but vacuum is not the preferred condition here because it would add severity and volatility risk.

#### F3 — Route 3

Solid-state/flux kinetics are moderate: oxide-powder precursors in SS4/SS5/TS1 are accessible, and related Sb(III) oxides form at 500–700 °C. However, SS4 and SS5/TS1 report multi-day heating, indicating ordering or phase-purity kinetics can be slow. The phase-selection problem is substantial because bulk annealing of Sb₂O₃ may relax to known stable polymorphs reported in CS1/SS3 rather than the slightly metastable Cm sheet phase.

#### F2 — Route 4

Route 4 is severe because TS2 required 6.3 GPa and 900 °C for metastable HP-Bi₂O₃, triggering the high-pressure penalty. It also requires multiple operations including capsule loading, high-pressure heating, quenching, and decompression, as described in TS2. CS and SS provide insufficient direct evidence for high-pressure Sb₂O₃ Cm synthesis, so their route-specific contribution is neutral.

#### F3 — Route 4

High-pressure kinetics can be adequate because TS2 converted Bi₂O₃ to a metastable polymorph in only 0.5 h at HP/HT conditions. For Sb₂O₃, a single-cation oxide should not suffer multication diffusion mismatch, which improves the inferred kinetics. The uncertainty is high because TS2 is Bi₂O₃, not Sb₂O₃, and pressure may stabilize a different polymorph from the target Cm sheet.

#### F4 — Amorphization Tendency

The target has only one cation, Sb, so the cation-size-mismatch amorphization criterion is not triggered. The described target structure has two inequivalent Sb and three inequivalent O sites and no explicit vertex+edge+face-sharing coexistence, so the unit-cell-size and mixed-connectivity checks are not triggered based on the provided target description. CS/SS/TS do not provide direct amorphization evidence for Cm-Sb₂O₃, so that uncertainty is treated as neutral rather than penalizing.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

The target is likely a low-energy metastable polymorph rather than the ground-state Sb₂O₃ phase, because its E_hull is 0.0290 eV/atom while CS1/SS3 report a stable orthorhombic Sb₂O₃ polymorph at E_hull = 0. The analogue support is strong chemically because CS1/SS3 use the exact Sb₂O₃ composition, and strong structurally because SS1/SS2 show Sb(III)–O frameworks with similar low-coordinate Sb environments. The most critical enabling factor is the high mobility/reconstructability of pnictogen sesquioxides under hydrothermal or sublimation conditions, supported by CS1, CS2, SS1, and SS2. The most critical limiting factor is polymorph selectivity against known stable Sb₂O₃ forms, especially valentinite/senarmontite-like products implied by CS1/SS3. Overall, the target should be experimentally approachable, with hydrothermal recrystallization offering the best balance of mild conditions and kinetic polymorph control.

| 1 | All | Competing stable Sb₂O₃ polymorph formation | High | Use kinetic control: hydrothermal low-temperature recrystallization as in SS1/SS2/CS2, or vapor temperature-gradient control as in CS1/SS3. |
| 2 | Route 1, Route 3 | Volatile Sb₂O₃ loss at elevated temperature | High | Use covered/sealed or controlled sublimation geometry; CS1/SS3 show Sb₂O₃ sublimation above 570 °C, so containment and temperature gradients should be controlled. |
| 3 | Route 2 | Incorporation of mineralizer or alkali/alkaline-earth cations | Medium | Keep KF/KOH/NaOH low; SS1 and SS2 show mineralized hydrothermal conditions can form Sr–Sb–O or Na–Sb–O phases rather than pure Sb₂O₃. |
| 4 | Route 2 | Low Sb₂O₃ solubility and slow recrystallization | Medium | Use mild basic pH and slow cooling; SS1 used pH ~10, KF, 4 days, and 8 °C/h cooling to obtain crystals. |
| 5 | Route 3 | Slow ordering kinetics in bulk/flux annealing | Medium | Use long holds and possible regrinding; SS4 reports multi-day solid-state heating, and SS5/TS1 report 2–10 day heating for related antimonites. |
| 6 | Route 4 | Specialized high-pressure equipment and uncertain pressure phase field | High | Use HP/quench only as a screening/fallback route; TS2 demonstrates metastable oxide trapping at 6.3 GPa but does not establish Cm-Sb₂O₃. |
| 7 | All | Oxidation-state drift toward Sb(V) oxides under oxidizing conditions | Medium | Avoid high oxygen pressure; CS3 shows Sb₂O₅ requires high O₂ pressure, indicating strongly oxidizing conditions can change Sb oxidation state. |
| 8 | Route 1, Route 2 | Crystal growth difficulty and polymorph nucleation control | Medium | Apply slow cooling: SS1 used 8 °C/h cooling hydrothermally, while CS1/SS3 indicate sublimation growth can produce crystalline Sb₂O₃. |
