### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target E_hull of 0.035 eV/atom falls in the high-synthesizability band, and TS analogues with comparable E_hull values are experimentally synthesized, including Cu₂FeGeSe₄ at 0.038 eV/atom and MnIn₂Se₄ at 0.0316 eV/atom [TS1, TS2]. CS analogues include several stable or nearly stable chalcogenide halides, supporting that layered M–Q–X compounds are accessible [CS1–CS4]. SS analogues are mostly tetrahedral selenides rather than bromoselenide sheets, so SS gives only weak thermodynamic corroboration and is treated near-neutral for chemical transferability [SS1–SS5]. Remaining uncertainty comes from Y’s different preferred oxidation chemistry compared with Nb/Cr analogues [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 1 condition severity

Route 1 avoids extreme temperature and high pressure because the closest C2/m analogue Nb(SBr)₂ forms at 500 °C in a sealed glass tube [CS2], and Nb–Se–Br chemistry occurs around 500 °C in vacuum tubes [CS3]. Deductions arise from vacuum sealing, volatile Br₂/Se, and hazardous/reactive precursors [CS2, CS3]. SS/TS do not provide route-specific bromination evidence, so their condition signal for Route 1 is secondary or insufficient. The main uncertainty is ampoule pressure management with Br₂ and Se [Reasonable Inference].

#### F3 — Route 1 kinetics

The same layered M(QBr)₂ topology in Nb(SBr)₂ strongly supports structural transferability [CS2]. However, using Y metal likely gives slower reaction kinetics than Nb powder because Y is refractory and forms stable surface oxides/halides [Reasonable Inference]. CS1 and CS4 report long times or phase-control difficulty in related chalcogenide halides, justifying a moderate penalty [CS1, CS4]. SS/TS provide general evidence that sealed chalcogenide syntheses work but no direct kinetic evidence for Y–Se–Br [SS1, TS3].

#### F2 — Route 2 condition severity

Route 2 uses moderate temperatures, comparable to 400–515 °C for NbSe₂Cl₂ CVT and 500–460 °C for Nb–Se–Br CVT [CS1, CS3]. It still requires an evacuated sealed tube, volatile transport species, and toxic bromine chemistry [CS1, CS3]. SS and TS support halide-assisted transport in broader selenides but are less chemically specific [SS1, SS2, TS1, TS2]. No high-pressure or >1000 °C penalty is needed.

#### F3 — Route 2 kinetics

CVT is kinetically attractive because it bypasses bulk diffusion and has produced single crystals of chemically related chalcogenide halides [CS1, CS3]. The weak point is Y vapor transport, because the data do not directly prove transport of a Y–Se–Br phase; this is inferred through likely Y bromide transport complexes [Reasonable Inference]. Composition control is penalized because CS analogues show polymorph coexistence, contamination, and narrow phase windows [CS1, CS2, CS4]. TS provides no direct vapor-transport kinetics for Y bromoselenides, so it is neutral for this subfactor.

#### F2 — Route 3 condition severity

Flux growth is based mainly on SS3, where a rare-earth selenide compound is synthesized at 750 °C under vacuum with controlled cooling [SS3]. The route has more procedural complexity than Routes 1–2 because it requires dry loading, flux reaction, slow cooling, and flux removal [SS3]. CS gives no direct flux synthesis of M(SeBr)₂ phases, and TS gives no halide-flux evidence, so both are neutral for route-specific feasibility. Deductions come from vacuum sealing, volatile Se/optional Br₂, multi-step processing, and air-sensitive halides [SS3; Reasonable Inference].

#### F3 — Route 3 kinetics

Flux growth should improve kinetics by dissolving Y/Se/Br species and reducing solid-state diffusion barriers; this is consistent with molten-mixture rare-earth selenide growth in SS3 [SS3]. It receives the best F3 score because flux can promote crystal growth and chemical equilibration, but this is partly speculative for Y(SeBr)₂ [Reasonable Inference]. The structural pathway is uncertain because none of the CS analogues report flux growth for the same layered chalcogenide bromide motif. TS provides insufficient route-specific kinetic evidence and is treated as neutral.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has only one cation, so the ionic-radius-mismatch criterion is not triggered [target structure]. The structure is described as a simple layered C2/m sheet with Y coordinated to Se and Br and no explicit coexistence of vertex-, edge-, and face-sharing networks [target structure]. CS/SS/TS do not report amorphization issues for directly analogous M(QX)₂ sheets; the main reported issues are phase purity and polymorph control rather than glass formation [CS1, CS2, CS4]. Uncertainty remains because the full crystallographic cell size is not provided.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Y(SeBr)₂ is likely a low-energy metastable or near-ground-state phase rather than a deeply unstable compound, because its E_hull is only 0.035 eV/atom and similar-hull TS compounds have been synthesized [TS1, TS2]. The strongest synthesis support comes from CS: Nb(SBr)₂ has the same C2/m layered M(QBr)₂ architecture and forms at 500 °C in a sealed tube [CS2], while Nb–Se–Br phases grow by CVT near 500 °C [CS3]. The most critical enabling factor is halogen-assisted transport/reactivity, which can form layered chalcogenide halides at moderate temperature despite refractory metals [CS1–CS3]. The most critical limiting factor is phase competition from stable binary/ternary yttrium bromides and selenides, plus tight Br/Se chemical-potential control [Reasonable Inference, with phase-control difficulties supported by CS1/CS2/CS4]. Overall, synthesis is plausible but will likely require careful ampoule chemistry and iterative optimization rather than a simple stoichiometric firing.
