### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target’s E_hull is **0.0500 eV/atom**, which lies at the top of the most favorable bracket in the requested rubric, so F1 is scored at 90 rather than higher because it is exactly at the metastability boundary. CS analogues NaLaFeWO6, NaNdFeWO6, NaMgLaTeO6, and NaNdMnWO6 are reported with E_hull = 0.0, supporting the feasibility of nearby Na–Ln ordered oxides (CS1, CS2, CS3, CS5). TS analogues at 0.061–0.088 eV/atom are experimentally synthesized, showing that phases slightly above the hull can be accessible (TS1–TS5). Remaining uncertainty is that no source directly reports the Na–La–Ir–Te–O phase or its decomposition products.

#### F2/F3 — Route 1

Route 1 has moderate condition severity because CS1/CS2 use multiple solution/decomposition/annealing steps and controlled reducing atmospheres, but only 850–900 °C and no high pressure. Kinetically, the citrate method is favorable because CS1/CS2 explicitly use it to create homogeneous reactive precursors, which should help distribute Na, La, Ir, and Te before crystallization. The main kinetic penalty is Ir incorporation and cation ordering in an ilmenite-derived mixed corner/edge/face-sharing framework, which is more complex than the CS perovskite examples. SS and TS provide only indirect support for controlled-atmosphere 5d oxide synthesis, so their route-specific signal is limited.

#### F2/F3 — Route 2

Route 2 is more severe because ceramic synthesis may require up to 1000–1050 °C, repeated grinding, and sealed or controlled atmosphere to manage Na/Te volatility; CS5 reports 800–1200 °C ceramic conditions and TS3 reports staged Ar firings. Kinetically, binary oxides and carbonates are less reactive than citrate gels, and IrO2/Ir is expected to react slowly. CS5 and TS3 show that ordered oxide phases can be produced by ceramic methods, but repeated annealing and atmosphere control are likely. The uncertainty is whether a ceramic route will produce the R3 ilmenite-derived ordering or instead competing perovskite/pyrochlore/tellurate phases.

#### F2/F3 — Route 3

Route 3 has the mildest nominal conditions because CS3 forms NaMgLaTeO6 in air at 973 K for 6 h from nitrate/hydroxide precursors. Its kinetic score is limited because CS3 contains Mg rather than Ir, and there is no direct evidence that IrO2 will dissolve/react sufficiently at those temperatures. Air may stabilize Te6+ but may not stabilize the target’s apparent unusual Ir/Te charge balance, so this route is chemically uncertain [Reasonable Inference]. Insufficient evidence from SS and TS for this nitrate/hydroxide route makes those sources neutral.

#### F2/F3 — Route 4

Route 4 has moderate-to-high condition severity because SS2, TS1, and TS5 flux/sealed routes require sealed tubes, long holds, slow cooling, and post-synthesis washing. Kinetically, flux can improve mass transport and crystal growth, which is why it scores higher than the ceramic route. However, no source directly demonstrates Ir–Te flux chemistry, and alkali fluxes can introduce K/Na off-stoichiometry or dissolve volatile Te species. This route is attractive for crystal growth or phase screening but less direct than the citrate route.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has strongly mismatched cation sizes and bonding preferences, including large Na/La sites and compact Ir/Te octahedral sites, which triggers the ionic-radius mismatch criterion [Reasonable Inference from target coordination]. The target also explicitly contains corner-, edge-, and face-sharing octahedra, triggering the mixed-connectivity criterion. The unit-cell atom count is not provided, and CS/SS/TS do not give enough direct evidence for the target’s Z value, so that criterion is not triggered. The resulting medium risk mainly reflects structural complexity and possible cation disorder rather than a high likelihood of glass formation.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

NaLaIrTeO6 is likely a **borderline metastable but accessible** phase because its E_hull is 0.0500 eV/atom, exactly at the favorable metastability threshold. The CS analogues provide the strongest support because Na–La/Nd ordered oxides and Na–La–Te oxides have been synthesized by soft-chemistry, ceramic, or nitrate routes (CS1–CS5). SS and TS provide useful but indirect support that complex 5d oxides near the hull can be made under controlled or sealed atmospheres (SS2–SS4, TS1–TS5). The most critical enabling factor is **high cation homogeneity before crystallization**, best provided by citrate/soft chemistry. The most critical limiting factor is **Ir/Te redox and volatility control**, because no source directly demonstrates Na–La–Ir–Te–O synthesis and the target’s charge balance may require an unusual Ir/Te oxidation-state combination.
