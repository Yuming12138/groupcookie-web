### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

**F1 — Thermodynamic Stability**  
YSbTe is reported exactly on the convex hull, which strongly supports thermodynamic synthesizability [Target data]. CS and TS independently reinforce this because many chemically or thermodynamically similar compounds have E_hull = 0.0 or near-zero values [CS2–CS5][TS1–TS5]. SS is somewhat less favorable because closest P4/nmm LnTe2 structures have small positive E_hull values, but those values remain within or near the high-score stability window [SS1–SS5]. Remaining uncertainty is that WBM status is theory-only and does not prove experimental isolation [Target data].

**F2 — Route 1 Solid-State Severity**  
The solid-state route avoids high pressure and >1000°C temperatures because CeSbTe and YAsSe analogues use 500–900°C sealed-tube conditions [CS2][TS4]. Deductions come from controlled atmosphere/sealing, volatile Sb/Te risk, multi-step grinding/annealing, and air-sensitive/toxic elemental handling [CS2][TS4][SS1][Reasonable Inference]. CS/TS support moderate severity, while SS provides insufficient evidence for ambient solid-state target synthesis and is treated as neutral 50 for this route.

**F3 — Route 1 Solid-State Kinetics**  
Kinetics are moderate because elemental Y is not highly reactive, and the route likely depends on diffusion through solid or partially molten Sb/Te-rich phases [CS2][TS4][Reasonable Inference]. Same-prototype CeSbTe strongly helps structural reconstruction, but the long 1-week plus 3-day anneal indicates slow ordering [CS2]. The main uncertainty is whether Y forms YTe2/Y–Sb binaries faster than the ternary YSbTe target [SS1][Reasonable Inference].

**F2 — Route 2 Flux Severity**  
Flux growth uses moderate temperatures around 850°C and ambient pressure, avoiding the largest severity penalties [CS4][TS3]. It still requires Ar/vacuum sealing, slow cooling, flux removal, and careful handling of Y/Sb/Te, so the severity remains moderate rather than easy [CS1][CS4][TS3][Reasonable Inference]. SS gives insufficient evidence for flux growth and is neutral 50 for this factor.

**F3 — Route 2 Flux Kinetics**  
Flux improves precursor mobility and crystallization relative to dry sintering, which raises the kinetics score [CS1][CS4][TS3][Reasonable Inference]. Same-prototype LaSbTe and closely related LnAsTe flux products support low structural reconstruction difficulty [CS1][CS4][TS3]. The main uncertainty is composition control because LaSbTe flux crystals were reported as chalcogen deficient [CS1].

**F2 — Route 3 High-Pressure Severity**  
High-pressure synthesis is severe because SS and TS analogues use up to 100 kbar and up to 1200°C [SS1–SS5][TS2]. The pressure penalty dominates even though the temperature is below 1400°C [SS1–SS5]. CS gives insufficient evidence that LnSbTe requires high pressure and is neutral 50 for this route.

**F3 — Route 3 High-Pressure Kinetics**  
High temperature and pressure can help densify and react elemental powders, but the route remains kinetically constrained by solid-state diffusion and short high-pressure dwell times [SS1–SS5][Reasonable Inference]. Structurally similar P4/nmm Y/Ln tellurides support the motif, but SS entries also report narrow P–T windows, impurities, and instability [SS1–SS5]. This makes high pressure a fallback rather than the preferred first attempt.

**F2 — Route 4 CVT Severity**  
CVT avoids applied high pressure and can operate below 1000°C, as shown by DyTe3 transport at 1150→1070 K [TS1]. The method still requires sealed evacuated tubes, volatile transport chemistry, and toxic/corrosive transport agents such as TeCl4 or I2 [TS1][CS5][Reasonable Inference]. SS gives insufficient evidence for CVT and is neutral 50 for this route.

**F3 — Route 4 CVT Kinetics**  
Transport growth can yield crystals efficiently if volatile species form, and TS1 proves this for DyTe3 [TS1]. However, no provided source proves that Y, Sb, and Te transport congruently into YSbTe, so vapor availability and composition control are scored only moderate [TS1][CS5][Reasonable Inference]. The main uncertainty is whether transport enriches Te/Sb or produces binary tellurides instead of the ternary phase [CS1][Reasonable Inference].

**F4 — Amorphization Tendency**  
The target has a simple Matlockite P4/nmm structure with no evidence for >40 atoms per unit cell and no target-level face-sharing coexistence [Target data][CS1][CS2]. Cation-radius mismatch is not triggered because the target has only one main electropositive cation, Y, although CS/SS/TS do not provide quantitative ionic-radius data and are neutral 50 for that specific check [Reasonable Inference]. Overall amorphization risk is low because closest CS/SS analogues are ordered crystalline compounds rather than glass-forming complex networks [CS1][CS2][SS1].

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

YSbTe is likely a ground-state or very near-ground-state phase because the target E_hull is 0.0000 eV/atom and multiple CS/TS analogues are also on or near the hull [Target data][CS2–CS5][TS1–TS5]. The strongest synthesis support comes from CS because LaSbTe and CeSbTe are same-family LnSbTe Matlockite/P4/nmm analogues made by flux or sealed-tube routes [CS1][CS2]. SS provides a cautionary signal: structurally similar Y/Ln tellurides sometimes require high pressure and show instability or narrow phase fields [SS1–SS5]. The most critical enabling factor is the pre-existing Matlockite LnSbTe structural family, especially LaSbTe/CeSbTe [CS1][CS2]. The most critical limiting factor is stoichiometry control of volatile Sb/Te and possible chalcogen deficiency or competing Y–Te phases [CS1][SS1][Reasonable Inference].
