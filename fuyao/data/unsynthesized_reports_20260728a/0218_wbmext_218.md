**F2 — Route 2**  
Direct sintering inherits controlled-atmosphere and multi-step penalties from YS and reduced rare-earth sealed-tube analogues [SS1, TS1, CS4]. The route avoids a separate flux but remains penalized for >1000 °C annealing and air-sensitive/reducing chemistry [SS1, TS1, CS4; Reasonable Inference]. CS flux sources are insufficient for direct sintering and are neutral=50 for this route [CS1, CS3, CS5].

**F3 — Route 2**  
Solid Y/Y₂S₃/Ge feed is expected to react slowly because YS itself requires very high-temperature treatment, and related reduced rare-earth phases require weeks in sealed Ta tubes [SS1, TS1, CS4]. Structural similarity to YS helps, but ternary ordering is not directly demonstrated [Target, SS1, TS1]. This makes Route 2 credible for bulk feed preparation but less kinetically attractive than flux growth.

**F2 — Route 3**  
CVT operates near 950 °C based on TS5, so it avoids the high-temperature penalties [TS5]. It still requires evacuated sealed ampoules, volatile I₂, multiple handling steps, and hazardous/air-sensitive chemistry [TS5, CS3, CS5; Reasonable Inference]. SS gives insufficient CVT-specific evidence for Y₄GeS₃ and is neutral=50 for route transfer [SS1–SS5].

**F3 — Route 3**  
I₂ transport can improve precursor activity and crystal growth, as shown for Sc sulfide CVT [TS5]. The limiting kinetic issue is not diffusion alone but maintaining ternary Y:Ge:S transport without halide side phases, which is inferred from reduced rare-earth halide analogues [CS2, CS4; Reasonable Inference]. This gives Route 3 a strong but riskier score than Route 1.

**F2 — Route 4**  
The melt route is heavily penalized because structural/thermodynamic sulfide analogues use ~1700 °C, vacuum containment, and specialized Ta/Mo hardware [SS1, SS3, SS5, TS1, TS3, TS4]. Sulfur loss risk becomes more severe at high temperature, as TS5 reports sulfur evolution/nonstoichiometry in Sc sulfide chemistry [TS5]. CS gives insufficient melt-specific evidence for Y₄GeS₃ and is neutral=50 [CS1–CS5].

**F3 — Route 4**  
High temperature improves diffusion, but the same high temperature can destabilize sulfur stoichiometry and promote competing binary phases [SS1, TS1, TS5; Reasonable Inference]. Melt/anneal analogues also report phase-control complications such as miscibility gaps [SS3, TS3]. Route 4 is therefore plausible but not preferred.

**F4 — Amorphization Tendency**  
The target framework is ordered, 3D, and octahedral with corner/edge sharing but no reported face-sharing, so the rubric’s three amorphization triggers are not activated [Target]. CS/SS/TS provide insufficient direct ionic-radius and unit-cell atom-count evidence for the target, so those sub-checks are neutral=50 where they do not directly apply [CS1–CS5, SS1–SS5, TS1–TS5]. The low amorphization risk is tempered by the need for Ge/S ordering on a reduced framework [Target; Reasonable Inference].

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Y₄GeS₃ is likely a ground-state or very near-ground-state candidate because the supplied E_hull is 0.0000 eV/atom, but it remains theory-only with no reported experimental synthesis in the source dataset [Target]. The analogue support is moderate-to-strong: CS shows rare-earth chalcogenides and reduced Y-containing compounds can be made by sealed flux or sealed-tube routes, while SS/TS show stable octahedral Y–S/RE–S rock-salt-like frameworks [CS1–CS5, SS1, TS1–TS5]. The most important enabling factor is the simple 3D octahedral connectivity, which closely resembles stable YS-type motifs [Target, SS1, TS1]. The most important limiting factor is controlling a reduced, metal-rich Y–Ge–S stoichiometry without sulfur loss or competing YS/Y₂S₃/Ge-containing phases [Target, SS1, TS1, TS5; Reasonable Inference]. Flux-assisted sealed growth is therefore the best first experimental route, with CVT as a secondary crystal-growth screen [CS1, CS2, CS3, CS5, TS5].
