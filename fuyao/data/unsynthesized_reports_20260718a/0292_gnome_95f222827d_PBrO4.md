### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

**F1 — Thermodynamic stability.**  
The target has E_hull = 0.0000 eV/atom, so it receives the maximum thermodynamic score under the provided rubric. Thermodynamic analogues include several experimentally synthesized E_hull = 0.0 oxyhalogen/oxoanion phases, including BaPd(IO₃)₄, K₂Ge(IO₃)₆, LiIn(IO₃)₄, and Cu₃Tl₂(SeO₃)₆ (TS1, TS3, TS4, TS5). CS gives mixed evidence because some chemical analogues are ground-state phases while I(NO₃)₂ is substantially above hull (CS1, CS2, CS3). Remaining uncertainty is that DFT stability does not guarantee resistance to hydrolysis or bromine redox disproportionation [Reasonable Inference].

**F2 — Route 1 hydrothermal severity.**  
Hydrothermal iodate analogues are synthesized at moderate temperature, typically 200 °C for 4 days with slow cooling in Teflon-lined autoclaves (SS1, SS2, SS5, TS1, TS4). No extreme temperature, GPa pressure, or external reactive atmosphere is required in those analogues (SS1, SS2, TS1). Deductions are assigned for sealed volatile oxybromine chemistry and oxidizing/toxic bromate-type precursors [Reasonable Inference]. The main uncertainty is whether bromine oxyacid speciation remains stable under hydrothermal conditions [Reasonable Inference].

**F3 — Route 1 hydrothermal kinetics.**  
Solution/hydrothermal synthesis provides high precursor activity and avoids long-range solid-state diffusion, as shown by iodate phases crystallizing from aqueous autoclaves at 200 °C (SS1, SS2, SS5, TS1, TS4). Structural reconstruction is moderate because PO₄ tetrahedra and BrO₄ square-planar units must assemble into an anhydrous framework [Reasonable Inference]. The score is reduced relative to a direct analogue because no P–Br–O hydrothermal synthesis is provided in CS/SS/TS. Insufficient direct evidence from CS/SS/TS exists for bromine-specific kinetics, so that part is extrapolated [Reasonable Inference].

**F2 — Route 2 solution precipitation severity.**  
The closest low-severity precedent is K₂Ge(IO₃)₆ precipitation from nitric acid solution after one day, with only decantation reported (SS4, TS3). CS1 also supports mild solution crystallization for an oxyhalogen-related material. The main deduction is for oxidizing/toxic bromate or bromic acid precursors [Reasonable Inference]. Insufficient evidence from SS/TS confirms that a bromine analogue can remain stable during slow evaporation, so the route is scored high but not perfect.

**F3 — Route 2 solution precipitation kinetics.**  
Precipitation from solution is kinetically favorable because reagents are molecular/ionic and diffusion is through solution, as in K₂Ge(IO₃)₆ formation from HIO₃/KNO₃/GeO₂·aq (SS4, TS3). CS1 shows solution crystallization can produce oxyhalogen-rich crystals under mild conditions. The structural-reconstruction score is moderate because an anhydrous P–O–Br network may require dehydration and ordering beyond simple salt precipitation [Reasonable Inference]. The key uncertainty is competition with hydrated phosphate/bromate phases [Reasonable Inference].

**F2 — Route 3 sealed-ampoule severity.**  
The sealed-ampoule route avoids very high temperature but requires dry/vacuum sealing, volatile bromine oxide retention, and multiple preparation steps [Reasonable Inference]. CS3 demonstrates that anhydrous phosphate oxide crystal growth by transport can require sealed quartz ampoules, high temperatures, long times, and phase-control effort. CS4 shows P–O–chalcogen chemistry can involve inert handling and sublimation/crystallization purification. Because the proposed bromine route would use unstable/oxidizing Br–O species, it is more severe than solution routes [Reasonable Inference].

**F3 — Route 3 sealed-ampoule kinetics.**  
Dry oxide/vapor-assisted synthesis can be less homogeneous than solution routes, so precursor activity and structural reconstruction are scored lower [Reasonable Inference]. CS3 shows GeP₂O₇ required multiple steps and 240 h chemical transport, indicating that anhydrous phosphate oxide crystallization can be kinetically slow and phase-sensitive. The route may benefit from volatile transport of bromine oxide species [Reasonable Inference], but no direct CS/SS/TS example validates P–Br–O vapor transport. Insufficient direct evidence from SS/TS supports this route, so it is kept as a challenging backup.

**F4 — Amorphization tendency.**  
The target has two very different cation environments: tetrahedral P and square co-planar Br, which suggests cation-size/geometry mismatch and triggers one amorphization-risk check [Reasonable Inference from target structure]. The provided target description does not report edge- or face-sharing polyhedra, only O-bridged P–O–Br connectivity, so the mixed-connectivity trigger is not applied. The unit-cell atom count is not supplied; insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS is available for that check, and it is treated as neutral/low risk. The resulting amorphization risk is low–medium.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

The target is most likely a **thermodynamic ground-state candidate** because its reported E_hull is 0.0000 eV/atom, and several thermodynamic analogues are also experimentally known ground-state oxyhalogen/oxoanion phases (TS1, TS3, TS4, TS5). The CS/SS/TS evidence supports synthesis mainly through **solution or hydrothermal oxyhalogen chemistry**, especially iodate precipitation and hydrothermal iodate crystallization (SS1, SS2, SS4, TS1, TS3). The most critical enabling factor is the availability of low-temperature solution pathways that can assemble hypervalent halogen–oxygen frameworks without long-range solid-state diffusion (SS1, SS4, TS1, TS3). The most critical limiting factor is bromine redox/volatility/hydrolysis control, because the strongest analogues are iodates and periodates rather than bromine phosphate oxides [Reasonable Inference]. Overall, the target appears synthetically plausible, with the best first attempt being acidic solution precipitation or slow evaporation followed by hydrothermal optimization if crystallinity is poor.
