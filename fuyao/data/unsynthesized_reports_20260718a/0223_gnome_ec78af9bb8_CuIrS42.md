### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0000 eV/atom, so it is predicted to be a ground-state phase by the supplied thermodynamic data. Close thermodynamic analogues IrS₂, RuS₂, Ir₂S₃, and Rh₂S₃ are also on or near the hull and experimentally synthesized (SS-1, SS-3, SS-5, TS-1, TS-2, TS-3, TS-5). The main uncertainty is that calculated ground-state stability does not guarantee a wide synthesis window or resistance to competing Cu sulfides/Ir sulfides [Reasonable Inference]. Insufficient evidence from CS for direct Cu–Ir–S thermodynamic competition; CS is therefore neutral for this specific Cu–Ir phase-selection issue.

#### F2 — Route 1 direct sealed ampoule

The severity is moderate because close Ir sulfide analogues require 900–960 °C, evacuated silica tubes, long holds, and sometimes grinding/refiring (SS-1, TS-1). The route avoids high pressure and ultra-high temperature, but sulfur volatility requires sealed ampoules, as in IrS₂/RuS₂/Rh₂S₃ syntheses (SS-1, SS-3, SS-4, TS-1, TS-2, TS-5). Insufficient evidence from CS for direct Cu–Ir sulfide synthesis, but CS-1 supports sealed-ampoule chalcogenide synthesis from elements.

#### F3 — Route 1 direct sealed ampoule

Kinetics are the main weakness because elemental Ir is refractory and IrS₂ synthesis required 2 weeks and possible refiring for homogeneity (SS-1, TS-1). Structural reconstruction is partly favorable because target Ir–S distances and IrS₆ octahedra resemble IrS₂/RuS₂ motifs (SS-1, SS-3, TS-1, TS-2). The uncertainty is Cu ordering into CuS₆ sites, for which insufficient evidence from SS/TS is available.

#### F2 — Route 2 flux growth

Flux growth remains moderately severe because RuS₂ flux synthesis used sealed quartz tubes, high temperatures up to 1000 °C, slow cooling, and Te flux handling (SS-3, TS-2). It avoids high pressure and may reduce required reaction time compared with direct diffusion, but Te toxicity and possible flux removal add penalties [Reasonable Inference from Te flux use in SS-3/TS-2]. CS gives insufficient specific evidence for Te-flux Cu–Ir sulfide growth and is neutral for this route.

#### F3 — Route 2 flux growth

The flux route receives the best kinetic score because liquid/flux transport can improve mass transfer, as shown by RuS₂ single-crystal growth in Te flux (SS-3, TS-2). However, RuS₂ flux synthesis had a narrow sulfur-content window and risk of RuTe₂ formation, implying that Cu(IrS₄)₂ may also require tight sulfur/flux activity control (SS-3, TS-2). The target’s S–S dimers make sulfur chemical potential especially important [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 3 CVT / halide transport

CVT is harsher because Rh₃S₄ synthesis used approximately 1047 °C, evacuated ampoules, chloride chemistry, and a controlled gradient (TS-4). Halide precursors or transport agents add toxicity/corrosion and possible side reactions [Reasonable Inference from TS-4]. CS-2/CS-4/CS-5 support sealed halide reactions generally, but insufficient evidence from CS for Cu–Ir sulfide CVT specifically.

#### F3 — Route 3 CVT / halide transport

The kinetic advantage is strong because vapor transport can bypass slow Ir bulk diffusion [Reasonable Inference from CVT success for Rh₃S₄ in TS-4]. The penalty is phase control: TS-4 reports that pure Rh₃S₄ was difficult due to co-formation of Rh₁₇S₁₅ and a narrow stability window. This risk is likely relevant to Cu–Ir–S because multiple sulfide competitors are expected [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 4 HPHT

The high-pressure route is severe because the relevant IrS₂ analogue required 60 kbar and 1400–1500 °C in a belt superpressure apparatus (SS-2). It avoids ampoule sulfur-pressure concerns but requires specialized equipment and high temperature (SS-2). Since ambient-pressure IrS₂/Ir₂S₃ analogues exist (SS-1, SS-5, TS-1, TS-3), HPHT is not the first choice.

#### F3 — Route 4 HPHT

HPHT improves reaction rates by using 1400–1500 °C, but elemental Ir remains refractory and diffusion-limited [Reasonable Inference from SS-2]. The structural analogy is only partial because pressure-stabilized IrS₂ is pyrite-like, whereas the target is triclinic Cu–Ir sulfide with ordered CuS₆ and multiple S–S sites (SS-2). Insufficient evidence from TS for HPHT Cu-containing analogues, so Cu incorporation remains uncertain.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The amorphization risk is low–medium because only one check is triggered: Cu and Ir have substantially different cation size/chemistry in sulfide octahedral coordination [Reasonable Inference]. The target framework is mainly corner-sharing, whereas more complex edge/corner/face-sharing networks occur in corundum-like Rh₂S₃/Ir₂S₃ analogues (SS-4, SS-5, TS-3, TS-5), reducing the mixed-connectivity amorphization penalty for the target. The unit-cell atom-count criterion is not clearly triggered; insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS for a >40-atom target cell.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Cu(IrS₄)₂ is likely a ground-state or near-ground-state phase because the supplied target E_hull is exactly 0.0000 eV/atom, and several close Ir/Ru/Rh sulfide analogues are also experimentally known ground-state or near-ground-state phases (SS-1, SS-3, SS-5, TS-1, TS-2, TS-3). The analogues strongly support sulfide framework formation with IrS₆/RuS₆ octahedra and S–S bonding, but they do not directly prove Cu–Ir ordering, so Cu incorporation is the largest extrapolation [Reasonable Inference]. The most enabling factor is the strong thermodynamic signal plus close Ir–S bond-length/coordination match to known IrS₂ phases (SS-1, TS-1). The most limiting factor is kinetic and compositional control: refractory Ir, volatile sulfur, and possible competing Cu sulfides/Ir sulfides require sealed systems, slow cooling, and careful sulfur activity [SS-1, SS-3, TS-1, TS-2; Reasonable Inference]. Overall, synthesis appears feasible but not trivial, with flux growth or long sealed-tube annealing most credible.
