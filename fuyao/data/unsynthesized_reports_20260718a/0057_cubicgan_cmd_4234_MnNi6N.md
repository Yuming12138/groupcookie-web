### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target lies at E_hull = 0.0500 eV/atom, exactly at the favorable threshold in the provided rubric, so it receives a high but not perfect score. Synthesized analogues include Mn₃Ni₂₀P₆ at E_hull = 0.0568 eV/atom by direct solid-state reaction (CS1/TS1), indicating that slightly metastable Ni-rich Mn compounds can be experimentally accessible. Several structurally or thermodynamically related Ni-rich intermetallics are on or near the hull, including Mg₃Ni₂₀P₆, ZrNi₅, ScNi₅, and ZrNi₄Sn (CS3, CS5, SS2, TS5). Remaining uncertainty is that the target is a nitride, while most close CS/TS examples are phosphides or intermetallics, so nitrogen chemical potential may dominate actual stability.

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 is severe because SS1 required ~10 GPa and ~1800 K for 3d transition-metal nitrides, which triggers both high-pressure and high-temperature penalties. The use of a supercritical/high-pressure nitrogen environment also imposes controlled-atmosphere complexity (SS1). CS and TS provide insufficient evidence that high pressure is unnecessary for this exact Mn–Ni nitride, so the penalty is retained. The benefit is that SS1 directly supports nitride formation and NNi₆ octahedra, but the equipment burden is high.

#### F3 — Route 1

The kinetics score is moderate because metals plus N₂ are simple but sluggish precursors, and SS1’s use of high pressure/high temperature implies that ordinary N₂ reactivity is insufficient. The target shares NNi₆ octahedra and nearly identical Ni–N distances with Ni₃N, which supports local structural transferability (SS1). However, conversion from hexagonal Ni₃N-type motifs to cubic F-43m MnNi₆N requires cation ordering and structural rearrangement [Reasonable Inference]. Thus, high pressure helps nitrogen uptake but does not eliminate ordering uncertainty.

#### F2 — Route 2

Route 2 avoids multi-GPa pressure and uses standard alloy synthesis operations supported by CS5 and SS2–SS5, so its condition severity is lower than Route 1. It still requires controlled Ar/vacuum handling and a reactive nitridation atmosphere, because analogous intermetallics are arc-melted under Ar and annealed under vacuum/Ar, while nitride formation requires nitrogen activity (CS5, SS2–SS5, SS1). The multi-step sequence of arc melting, annealing, and nitridation adds a penalty. CS/TS give insufficient direct evidence for ambient-pressure MnNi₆N nitridation, so the route remains only moderately favorable.

#### F3 — Route 2

The metallic framework should form reasonably well because many Ni-rich intermetallic analogues are synthesized by arc melting followed by annealing, including cubic F-43m ZrNi₅ and related Ni-rich phases (CS5, SS2–SS5, TS5). Diffusion is aided by metallic bonding and high-temperature annealing, but dense metals are still less kinetically active than molecular or salt precursors. The main kinetic uncertainty is nitrogen insertion into a preformed Mn–Ni alloy, which no CS/TS analogue directly demonstrates. Therefore the route scores best overall but not exceptionally high in F3.

#### F2 — Route 3

Route 3 avoids DAC-level pressure but introduces sealed-tube, reactive-donor, and safety penalties. CS1–CS3 and TS5 support sealed/inert high-temperature solid-state methods, but Li₃N/NaN₃ donor use is extrapolated [Reasonable Inference]. Toxicity, air sensitivity, and gas-pressure buildup are practical risks for NaN₃/Li₃N [Reasonable Inference]. Since no CS/SS/TS source directly reports this route, the severity score is conservative.

#### F3 — Route 3

Solid nitrogen donors should provide higher local nitrogen activity than N₂ gas at ambient pressure [Reasonable Inference], which improves precursor activity relative to metal + N₂. However, the reaction must form both the ordered Mn–Ni sublattice and the nitride framework from a mixture, which likely requires extensive reconstruction. SS1 supports the desired NNi₆ local bonding, while CS1–CS3 support sealed solid-state synthesis of Ni-rich ternaries, but neither directly proves this exact route. The resulting kinetics score is moderate but uncertain.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has a simple ordered cubic description with only Mn, Ni, and N sites, and the reported connectivity does not include simultaneous corner-, edge-, and face-sharing polyhedra. Mn and Ni are chemically compatible neighboring transition metals, supported by Mn–Ni bonding in Mn₃Ni₂₀P₆ (CS1/TS1), and their size mismatch is unlikely to be large [Reasonable Inference]. Structurally similar Ni-rich frameworks often crystallize as ordered intermetallics rather than amorphous products (CS5, SS2–SS5, TS5). The main uncertainty is not amorphization but phase competition and nitrogen uptake.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

MnNi₆N is likely a marginally metastable or borderline ground-state-accessible phase because its E_hull is exactly 0.0500 eV/atom, which lies at the favorable cutoff but not clearly on the hull. The analogue support is good for Ni-rich Mn/intermetallic framework formation, especially from CS5 and SS2–SS5, and good for local Ni–N octahedral bonding from SS1, but no provided analogue directly combines Mn–Ni alloying with cubic nitride formation. The most critical enabling factor is the strong structural plausibility of NNi₆ octahedra with Ni–N ≈ 1.85–1.87 Å, supported by SS1 and the target geometry. The most critical limiting factor is achieving sufficient nitrogen chemical potential without decomposing or forming competing Ni₃N/Mn nitrides or Mn–Ni intermetallic phases. Overall, the material is synthesizable with moderate-to-high risk, with ambient-pressure alloy-nitridation as the best practical first attempt and high-pressure nitridation as the strongest fallback.
