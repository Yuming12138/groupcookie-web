### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0500 eV/atom, exactly at the rubric threshold for the highest thermodynamic bin, so F1 = 90. TS gives useful support because KNi2S2 at 0.0536 eV/atom and Mn3Ni20P6 at 0.0568 eV/atom are reported synthesized by solid-state methods (TS-1, TS-2). CS similarly shows Ni-rich ternaries synthesized near this energy range, including Mn3Ni20P6 at 0.0568 eV/atom and very stable Ni21Sn2P6 at ~0.00046 eV/atom (CS-1, CS-4). SS supports stable structural motifs, with Ni3N at 0.0147 eV/atom and CdNNi3 at 0 eV/atom (SS-1, SS-3), but SS does not directly establish Ni–Te–N stability.

#### F2 — Route 1 condition severity

Route 1 is penalized for NH3, because CdNNi3 synthesis uses a static NH3 atmosphere with repeated gas refilling (SS-3). It is also penalized for Te volatility and toxic/reactive handling, inferred from the common use of sealed silica tubes for Te/Se chalcogenide reactions (SS-2, TS-5) and from general Te/NH3 handling considerations [Reasonable Inference]. It avoids high pressure and >1000 °C temperatures, unlike the DAC Ni3N synthesis at ~10 GPa/1800 K (SS-1), so the severity is moderate rather than severe. CS gives insufficient direct evidence for NH3 nitridation and is neutral for that specific severity element.

#### F3 — Route 1 kinetics

The kinetic advantage of Route 1 is that SS-3 demonstrates nitridation of a Ni-containing cubic perovskite-like nitride at only 743 K under NH3, suggesting NH3 can overcome some N incorporation barriers. The target’s Ni–N bond length, 1.89 Å, is close to 1.87 Å in Ni3N (SS-1) and 1.91 Å in CdNNi3 (SS-3), supporting structural compatibility. The kinetic limitation is that Ni and Te are dense elemental solids, and the target requires ordering of TeNi12 cuboctahedra, which is not directly shown in SS-3 [Reasonable Inference]. Regrinding and repeated sintering are required in SS-3, so a moderate reconstruction penalty is included.

#### F2 — Route 2 condition severity

Route 2 uses standard sealed-tube or Ar/vacuum methods, which are well represented for Ni-rich phosphides and chalcogenides (CS-1, CS-4, SS-2, TS-5). It is penalized for controlled atmosphere, sealed Te handling, and multi-step annealing, because SS-2 and TS-5 use silica tubes under purified Ar plus homogenization/annealing. It avoids GPa pressure and can likely stay below 1000 °C [Reasonable Inference], making it less severe than Route 3. SS provides useful Ni3N precursor logic, but insufficient evidence that Ni3N survives tellurization without decomposition.

#### F3 — Route 2 kinetics

Ni3N provides a structurally relevant nitrogen source because it already contains NNi6 octahedra with Ni–N = 1.87 Å (SS-1). However, solid-state reaction among Ni/Ni3N/Te likely requires substantial diffusion and ternary reconstruction, and analogous chalcogenide syntheses report annealing, homogenization, and sometimes impurities (SS-2, TS-5). CS-1 and CS-4 show that Ni-rich ternaries can be made by long high-temperature annealing, but they also imply slow ordering kinetics. Therefore, the kinetics are moderate, not easy.

#### F2 — Route 3 condition severity

Route 3 is strongly penalized because the closest structural nitride precedent, Ni3N, was made at about 10 GPa and 1800 K using a DAC and YAG laser heating system (SS-1). TS-3 also shows high-pressure Ni-containing synthesis requiring several GPa and specialized high-pressure cells. The high-pressure N2 route is therefore experimentally challenging despite being chemically powerful. CS gives insufficient high-pressure nitride signal and is neutral for this route.

#### F3 — Route 3 kinetics

High pressure and high temperature should accelerate nitrogen insertion and Ni diffusion, and SS-1 demonstrates rapid nitride formation in 30 min under supercritical nitrogen. The NNi6 octahedral motif is strongly supported by SS-1, making the structural reconstruction score relatively high. The remaining uncertainty is Te incorporation: none of SS-1, CS, or TS directly shows a high-pressure Ni–Te–N ternary, so Te segregation or binary Ni–Te formation remains a serious risk [Reasonable Inference]. Thus Route 3 has good intrinsic reaction kinetics but poor practical severity.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has a relatively simple cubic ordered framework, and the reported connectivity includes NNi6 octahedra and TeNi12 cuboctahedra rather than a highly complex large-cell network. SS-3 shows that related cubic nitride frameworks with NNi6 octahedra can crystallize rather than amorphize. The main amorphization/disorder risk comes from the large size and bonding contrast between Ni and Te [Reasonable Inference], plus possible off-stoichiometry from Te volatility. CS/TS provide insufficient direct amorphization data and are neutral for this factor.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Ni6TeN is most likely a near-hull metastable or marginally stable phase rather than a deeply stable ground-state compound, because its E_hull is exactly 0.0500 eV/atom. The structural evidence is encouraging: Ni–N distances and NNi6 octahedra closely match Ni3N and CdNNi3 analogues (SS-1, SS-3), while Te-containing solid-state routes are well established for Ni/Pd chalcogenides (SS-2, TS-5). The most important enabling factor is the strong local compatibility of N in octahedral Ni6 coordination, supported by SS-1 and SS-3. The most important limiting factor is simultaneous control of nitrogen activity and Te volatility/stoichiometry, because the provided data do not directly demonstrate a ternary Ni–Te–N synthesis. Overall, ammonolysis of a Ni–Te precursor is the best balance between structural transferability and practical conditions.
