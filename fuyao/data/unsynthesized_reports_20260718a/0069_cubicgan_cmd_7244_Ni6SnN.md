### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.050 eV/atom, which falls exactly at the prompt’s highest stability bin boundary, so it receives 90/100. Thermodynamic analogues include TS1 with E_hull ≈ 0.0568 eV/atom and successful direct solid-state synthesis, indicating phases slightly above the hull can still be experimentally accessible (TS1). TS5 is a Sn-containing F-43m compound with E_hull ≈ 0.0328 eV/atom and established arc-melt synthesis, supporting the viability of near-hull Ni/Sn-rich intermetallic frameworks (TS5). Remaining uncertainty is that the target contains nitrogen, while most thermodynamic analogues are phosphides, hydrides, oxides, or intermetallics rather than Ni–Sn nitrides (TS1–TS5).

#### F2 — Route 1

Route 1 is moderately severe because it uses NH3, which is a reactive/toxic controlled atmosphere, and it requires repeated grinding/sintering similar to CdNi3N synthesis (SS2). It avoids >1000 °C temperatures and high pressure, making it much milder than arc melting or high-pressure routes (SS2 versus TS2/TS4). The main uncertainty is whether Sn remains in the desired local environment during NH3 nitridation rather than forming competing Ni–Sn phases [Reasonable Inference].

#### F3 — Route 1

Kinetics are mixed: elemental Ni/Sn powders are less reactive than soluble precursors, but NH3 successfully forms NNi6 octahedra in CdNi3N at only 743 K (SS2). The target Ni–N distance of 1.89 Å is very close to CdNi3N’s 1.91 Å, supporting structural transferability for the nitride sublattice (SS2; target structure). Uncertainty remains because Cd is replaced by Sn and the target stoichiometry Ni6SnN is different from CdNi3N (SS2; target formula).

#### F2 — Route 2

Route 2 is penalized heavily for arc melting, controlled Ar/vacuum atmosphere, possible Sn loss, and subsequent NH3 nitridation (SS1, TS5, SS2). Arc melting/remelting and long annealing are standard for Ni-rich intermetallics and Sn-containing phases, but they add operational complexity (SS1, CS4, TS5). The evidence supports cation homogenization well but does not directly show nitrogen insertion into Ni–Sn alloys.

#### F3 — Route 2

Pre-alloying should improve Ni/Sn homogeneity [Reasonable Inference], and analogous Ni-rich intermetallics are routinely homogenized by arc melting plus annealing (SS1, SS3, SS4, TS5). Nitrogen insertion into a dense alloy may be slower than nitridation of mixed powders [Reasonable Inference], although NH3 can form related NNi6 motifs in CdNi3N (SS2). This route is kinetically plausible but may form only surface nitride unless the alloy is powdered before ammonolysis [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 3

Route 3 avoids arc melting and high pressure but introduces sealed-tube pressure and potentially hazardous nitrogen donors [Reasonable Inference]. Sealed ampoules are well supported for Ni-rich pnictide/intermetallic synthesis (CS1, CS3, TS1), but no CS/SS/TS entry directly demonstrates azide or NH4Cl synthesis of Ni–Sn nitrides. Thus, the condition severity is moderate-to-high because safety and nitrogen chemical potential must be managed.

#### F3 — Route 3

Reactive nitrogen donors may improve nitrogen availability compared with N2 gas [Reasonable Inference]. The solid-state reconstruction remains substantial because elemental Ni/Sn plus an N source must form a specific ordered F-43m nitride (target structure). Evidence from CS1/CS3/TS1 supports sealed-tube solid-state formation of Ni-rich compounds, while SS2 supports the desired NNi6 motif, but the route is more extrapolative than Route 1.

#### F2 — Route 4

High-pressure synthesis is the most severe route because TS2 and TS4 use multi-GPa pressures, specialized cells, and quenching protocols. Such conditions are justified only if the target behaves as a metastable nitride inaccessible at ambient pressure [Reasonable Inference]. CS and SS provide insufficient evidence that high pressure is needed for Ni6SnN.

#### F3 — Route 4

High pressure can stabilize dense phases and metastable products, as shown for high-pressure thermodynamic analogues (TS2, TS4). However, neither TS2 nor TS4 is a Ni–Sn nitride, so the chemical transferability is weak. The route may overcome thermodynamic decomposition but does not guarantee cation ordering or nitrogen incorporation into the exact F-43m structure [Reasonable Inference].

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has a simple, high-symmetry ordered structure with NNi6 octahedra and SnNi12 cuboctahedra (target structure), which lowers amorphization risk. Only the large Ni/Sn size contrast is counted as a trigger [Reasonable Inference]; edge-sharing coexistence and >40 atom cell complexity are not supported by the target description. SS2 shows a related simple cubic nitride can crystallize under mild ammonolysis, further reducing amorphization concern, although SS2 is Pm-3m rather than F-43m.

Ni6SnN is likely a near-hull, mildly metastable or borderline-stable phase because its E_hull is exactly 0.050 eV/atom, at the upper edge of the high-stability scoring bin. The strongest synthesis support comes from the structurally similar CdNi3N nitride, which has NNi6 octahedra and very similar Ni–N bond lengths under NH3 synthesis conditions (SS2). Chemical support from Ni–Sn/Ni-rich intermetallics shows that Ni–Sn frameworks and F-43m Ni-rich structures are accessible by conventional metallurgy (CS1, CS4, TS5). The key enabling factor is the close match between the target’s Ni–N octahedral environment and the CdNi3N ammonolysis analogue (SS2; target structure). The key limiting factor is the absence of a direct Ni–Sn–N experimental analogue, creating uncertainty about competing Ni–Sn intermetallics, nitrogen uptake, and Sn stoichiometry control [Reasonable Inference].
