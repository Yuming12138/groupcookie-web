### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

**F1 — Thermodynamic stability.**  
The target Eₕull is 0.050 eV/atom, which falls at the favorable boundary of the 90–100 scoring bin. TS contains synthesized or reported compounds with similar hull energies, e.g. TS3 at 0.048 eV/atom, TS4 at 0.0506 eV/atom, TS1 at 0.0777 eV/atom, and TS2 at 0.0876 eV/atom, indicating that this metastability range can be experimentally accessible. CS and SS mostly provide stable K-rich oxide analogues with Eₕull = 0, which supports chemical plausibility but does not directly validate the S–O coordination. Remaining uncertainty is dominated by whether the reported computational structure is dynamically and chemically viable with octahedral S(VI).

**F2 — Route 1.**  
Route 1 scores high because CS1, CS5, SS1, and SS3 show that K-rich oxides can be synthesized at moderate temperatures in sealed Ag or metal containers without extreme pressure. The main penalty is the air/moisture sensitivity of K₂O, consistent with strict handling requirements in CS4 and SS2. Evidence from TS is thermodynamically useful but gives insufficient direct signal for this specific solid-state K–S–O route, so TS is neutral for route-specific condition transferability.

**F3 — Route 1.**  
K₂O is reactive and K-rich oxide formation at 500–700 °C is well represented by CS1, CS5, SS1, and SS3, supporting good diffusion kinetics. However, the target’s SO₆ octahedron is a major local-structure departure from ordinary sulfate precursors [Reasonable Inference], and no CS/SS/TS source demonstrates this reconstruction for sulfur. Long anneals in CS1 and SS1 suggest that even favorable K-rich oxide syntheses can be slow.

**F2 — Route 2.**  
The alkali oxide flux route is more severe because it may involve volatile sulfur oxide species and sealed sulfur-oxide chemical potential control [Reasonable Inference]. CS1/CS5/SS1 support the sealed flux concept, but no source gives direct SO₃-containing alkali oxide flux conditions for K₆SO₆, so evidence from CS/SS/TS is incomplete. Air-sensitive alkali oxides impose an additional handling penalty.

**F3 — Route 2.**  
Flux chemistry should improve mass transport, consistent with crystal growth in sealed alkali oxide systems in CS1 and SS1. The kinetic score is higher than Route 1 because a reactive melt/flux can help overcome diffusion barriers [Reasonable Inference]. The uncertainty remains high because sulfur oxide volatility and competing sulfate formation are not resolved by CS/SS/TS.

**F2 — Route 3.**  
High-pressure synthesis is penalized because pressures above 5 GPa trigger a major severity deduction. This route is not directly supported by CS, SS, or TS; the only rationale is that pressure may stabilize the target’s unusual S coordination [Reasonable Inference]. Therefore, CS/SS/TS provide insufficient direct evidence for high-pressure feasibility and are scored neutral for this route-specific condition factor.

**F3 — Route 3.**  
High pressure could improve the structural plausibility of octahedral sulfur [Reasonable Inference], raising the structural-reconstruction score relative to ambient solid-state synthesis. However, precursor activity remains only moderate, and the absence of high-pressure analogues in CS/SS/TS adds uncertainty. This is a plausible rescue route rather than the first experimental choice.

**F2 — Route 4.**  
Autoclave amide synthesis is supported by CS2 and TS3 for alkali-rich oxide/oxonitride crystals at 350–750 °C, but it requires reactive amide/NH₃ environments and specialized vessels. CS2 also notes ammonia-related high-pressure/autoclave conditions, while TS3 uses autoclaves to prevent amide decomposition. The method is conditionally transferable but chemically risky for a pure oxide sulfate target.

**F3 — Route 4.**  
Amide fluxes can be kinetically powerful, as shown by crystalline products in CS2 and TS3. The limiting factor is chemical selectivity: CS2 and TS3 involve nitrido/amide chemistry and phase competition, so a nitrogen-free K–S–O phase may be difficult to isolate [Reasonable Inference]. Evidence is therefore positive for kinetic activation but weak for phase purity.

**F4 — Amorphization tendency.**  
The target is compositionally simple with only K, S, and O, and the formula unit has 13 atoms, so it does not obviously meet the >40 atom complexity trigger from the target description. The K⁺/S⁶⁺ size and bonding contrast is large [Reasonable Inference], giving one amorphization-risk trigger. Mixed polyhedral sharing is not described for the target, and CS/SS/TS do not provide a direct target-specific signal, so that check remains untriggered.

Source neutral notes: TS gives strong hull-energy context but insufficient direct signal for sulfur oxide synthesis routes; CS gives strong K-rich oxide synthesis evidence but insufficient direct signal for octahedral S; SS gives strong structural evidence for K-rich octahedral TeO₆ oxides but insufficient direct chemical evidence for substituting S into that role.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

K₆SO₆ is likely a **near-hull metastable phase**, not clearly a robust ground-state phase, because its Eₕull is exactly 0.050 eV/atom. The CS and SS analogues strongly support that K-rich oxides can be synthesized in sealed Ag or metal containers at 500–750 °C, often over days to weeks, but they do not directly demonstrate octahedral sulfur. The most critical enabling factor is the availability of reactive K₂O/KOₓ sealed-flux chemistry, which repeatedly succeeds for K-rich oxide crystals in CS1, CS5, SS1, and SS2. The most critical limiting factor is the target’s unusual SO₆ octahedral unit, since common S(VI) oxides are tetrahedral sulfates [Reasonable Inference] and no CS/SS/TS source confirms this coordination in a K–S–O material. Overall, synthesis is plausible but should be treated as a controlled-atmosphere, phase-selective experiment rather than a routine solid-state preparation.
