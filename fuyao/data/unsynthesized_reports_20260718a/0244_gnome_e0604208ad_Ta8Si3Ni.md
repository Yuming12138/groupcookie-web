### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target is predicted at E_hull = 0.0000 eV/atom, giving a high thermodynamic score. Stable analogues with E_hull = 0.0 are common in the CS/TS sets, including CS1, CS2, CS4, CS5 and TS2–TS5, which supports that Ta-rich intermetallic/metalloid phases can be genuine ground-state compounds. A caveat is that SS1 is experimentally known despite E_hull = 0.114 eV/atom, showing that computed hull values are not perfectly aligned with synthesis outcomes. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS exists for the exact Ta–Ni–Si ternary phase diagram, so competing phases remain uncertain.

#### F2 — Route 1: Arc-melting + post-annealing

Route 1 is high temperature but uses standard inert-atmosphere arc-melting equipment, as in CS5 and SS2/TS1. The main severity deductions come from temperatures exceeding 1400 °C and the multi-step melt/remelt/anneal workflow. CS5 shows repeated remelting can yield single-phase homogeneous products, while TS1/SS2 show Ta–Ge arc melting under gettered Ar is feasible. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS indicates whether Ta8Si3Ni requires vacuum rather than Ar, so Ar is treated as adequate.

#### F3 — Route 1: Arc-melting + post-annealing

Kinetics are moderate because the melt bypasses some solid-state diffusion barriers, supported by successful arc-melting of refractory analogues in CS5 and SS2/TS1. However, Ta is refractory, and SS1/CS1 show Ta-rich silicide/metalloid systems often need high-temperature homogenization. Structural ordering into a Khatyrkite-derived P422 ternary is not directly demonstrated by CS/SS/TS, so post-annealing is likely required [Reasonable Inference]. The uncertainty is mainly whether Ni enters the ordered site cleanly or forms Ni–Si/Ta–Ni secondary phases [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 2: Solid-state reaction / sintering

Route 2 has higher condition severity because close Ta–Si and Ta–Ge–B analogues require long, high-temperature treatments. CS1 required 1200 °C for 100 h plus 1800 °C for 24 h to reach single phase, and SS1 involved 1800–2300 °C homogenization under H₂. Vacuum or controlled atmosphere is likely needed to avoid oxidation and contamination, consistent with SS4/TS4 high-T sealed-vacuum routes. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS supports use of low-temperature soft-chemistry precursors for this metallic intermetallic, so no mild route is assumed.

#### F3 — Route 2: Solid-state reaction / sintering

Solid-state kinetics are poor because Ta diffusion is slow and elemental Ta powders are refractory. SS1 and CS1 both show long/hot treatments for Ta-rich metalloid phases, supporting a low diffusion score. The target also requires ternary ordering, while CS/SS/TS do not report a direct precursor-to-target topotactic route, so structural reconstruction is likely substantial [Reasonable Inference]. The uncertainty is whether a transient Ni–Si liquid at high temperature could improve kinetics; this is plausible but not directly established in CS/SS/TS [Reasonable Inference].

#### F2 — Route 3: Melt growth / controlled cooling

Route 3 is severe in temperature but avoids high pressure and can be done under Ar, as shown for Ta3Al2CoC melt growth in TS3. It receives a multi-step penalty because controlled heating, holding, slow cooling, and annealing are required. TS3 demonstrates that 1500 °C, long holds, and controlled cooling are realistic for Ta-containing intermetallic/carbidic systems. Insufficient evidence from CS/SS/TS indicates whether a flux excess is required, so the base route is scored as stoichiometric melt growth.

#### F3 — Route 3: Melt growth / controlled cooling

Melt growth gives the best kinetic score because it reduces the need for long-range solid-state diffusion compared with Route 2 [Reasonable Inference], and TS3 shows a successful Ta-containing melt-growth process. Controlled cooling can favor ordered intermetallic crystallization, while post-annealing can improve site ordering, following the logic of TS3 and TS1. The limiting kinetic factor remains dissolution/homogenization of refractory Ta and avoidance of competing binaries [Reasonable Inference]. No CS/SS/TS source directly confirms Khatyrkite-derived Ta8Si3Ni crystallization from melt, so structural selectivity remains uncertain.

#### F4 — Amorphization tendency

The target has one size-mismatch trigger because Ta and Ni differ significantly in metallic radius [Reasonable Inference]; CS/SS/TS do not provide radius values directly. The structure description does not indicate a very large unit cell or explicit mixed vertex/edge/face polyhedral sharing, so C2 and C3 are not triggered. Some analogues show complex mixed sharing or large coordination environments, for example CS1 and SS4, but this is not sufficient to assign the same amorphization behavior to the target. Overall amorphization risk is low–medium rather than high.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Ta8Si3Ni is likely a ground-state phase because the target E_hull is 0.0000 eV/atom, and many chemically or thermodynamically similar Ta-rich compounds are also on the hull (CS1, CS2, CS4, CS5, TS2–TS5). The synthesis evidence is moderately strong: CS/SS/TS show that Ta-rich silicides, germanides, chalcogenides, and transition-metal intermetallics can be made by arc melting, high-temperature sintering, or melt growth, but no exact Ta–Ni–Si Khatyrkite-derived analogue is provided. The most critical enabling factor is the metallic/intermetallic character, which allows arc or melt processing rather than requiring complex redox chemistry [Reasonable Inference; supported by CS5, SS2/TS1, TS3]. The most critical limiting factor is slow Ta homogenization and possible competition from binary Ta–Si or Ni–Si phases, because Ta-rich analogues often require very high temperatures, repeated melting, or long anneals (CS1, SS1, CS5). Overall, synthesis is feasible but likely requires careful high-temperature processing and phase-purity optimization.
