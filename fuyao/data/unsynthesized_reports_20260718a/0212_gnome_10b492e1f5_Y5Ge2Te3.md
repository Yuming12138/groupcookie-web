### SECTION 5 — Scoring Rationale

#### F1 — Thermodynamic stability

The target has E_hull = 0.0000 eV/atom, which places it in the highest thermodynamic-stability bin. CS and TS include multiple ground-state analogues, especially BaY₂Te₄ at E_hull = 0 and ErSb/SmAs/rare-earth bismuthides at E_hull = 0 (CS; TS). This supports that rare-earth chalcogenide/pnictide frameworks can be true ground-state phases, although none directly verifies Y₅Ge₂Te₃. Remaining uncertainty is kinetic/phase competition rather than thermodynamic instability.

#### F2 — Route 1: sealed-tube solid-state

The 950–1000 °C sealed-tube route is directly supported by BaY₂Te₄ synthesized at 1000 °C for 6 days in evacuated fused-silica tubes (CS; TS). Severity is moderate because vacuum sealing, volatile Te, and air-sensitive/toxic precursors impose deductions; no high pressure or >1400 °C step is needed. SS and TS rare-earth pnictide data also support sealed ampoule synthesis but add that repeated annealing may be needed for phase purity (SS; TS). The main uncertainty is whether Ge enters the target framework without forming GeTe or Y–Te binaries.

#### F3 — Route 1: sealed-tube solid-state

Kinetics are moderate-to-slow because elemental Y and Ge are not highly reactive molecular precursors [Reasonable Inference], and analogous solid-state syntheses require long holds of 5–7 days or 6 days (CS; SS; TS). The Y–Te octahedral chemistry in BaY₂Te₄ is favorable, but no direct Y–Ge–Te analogue is provided (CS). Structural reconstruction is therefore only partially supported. Regrinding/reannealing could improve ordering, as monophase rare-earth pnictides required annealing until XRD purity was reached (SS; TS).

#### F2 — Route 2: halide flux

The flux route has lower temperature severity because CS flux growth occurs at 850 °C rather than 1000 °C (CS). However, it receives deductions for evacuated sealed tubes, Te volatility, flux-handling/extraction, and air-sensitive/toxic reagent handling [Reasonable Inference], all consistent with the CS flux setup. SS provides insufficient useful signal for halide flux in this specific Y–Ge–Te chemistry, so it is treated as neutral rather than negative. The remaining concern is reproducibility, since CS notes flux products appeared unexpectedly when stoichiometric synthesis failed for Sm analogues.

#### F3 — Route 2: halide flux

Flux growth improves mass transport and lowers the reaction temperature relative to bulk solid-state synthesis, as demonstrated by 850 °C BaBr₂/KBr flux conditions for rare-earth telluride crystals (CS). This justifies a higher diffusion/kinetics score than Route 1. The structural selectivity score remains moderate because no provided analogue is the same Y₅Ge₂Te₃ structure, and flux growth may favor alternative Y–Te, Ge–Te, or halide-containing phases [Reasonable Inference]. Overall, Route 2 is kinetically most attractive but phase-selectivity limited.

#### F2 — Route 3: arc-melting/direct fusion

SS analogues show high-temperature RF heating of rare-earth antimonides at 1100–2000 °C under high-purity Ar and Ta containment (SS). Applying this to Y–Ge–Te is severe because Te is volatile and the route likely needs pre-reaction, melting/remelting, and post-annealing [Reasonable Inference]. TS/SS support direct fusion and annealing for rare-earth pnictides, but CS provides insufficient direct evidence for arc-melting tellurides. Therefore, this route scores lowest in severity.

#### F3 — Route 3: arc-melting/direct fusion

Melting can homogenize refractory Y-rich mixtures faster than powder diffusion [Reasonable Inference], and SS rare-earth pnictide examples show rapid high-temperature reaction is possible (SS). However, volatile Te loss and the need to reconstruct/order a complex monoclinic octahedral phase during annealing lower the kinetic and structural scores [Reasonable Inference]. SS/TS rare-earth bismuthide systems also report multiphase behavior and phase-purity challenges, which supports a cautious score (SS; TS). This route is better as a screening/homogenization method than as the primary recommended synthesis.

#### F4 — amorphization tendency

The target’s reported octahedral network is ordered and crystalline, and CS/SS/TS analogues all describe crystalline rare-earth chalcogenide/pnictide phases rather than amorphous products (CS; SS; TS). One amorphization risk is the large mismatch between Y and Ge cation sizes/chemical roles [Reasonable Inference]; CS/SS/TS provide insufficient direct radius evidence, so this is an interpretive trigger. The target description shows corner- and edge-sharing octahedra but not face-sharing, reducing topological frustration relative to mixed vertex/edge/face systems. The resulting amorphization risk is low–medium.

### SECTION 6 — Synthesizability Interpretation

Y₅Ge₂Te₃ is likely a thermodynamic ground-state or near-ground-state phase because its reported E_hull is 0.0000 eV/atom, and multiple CS/TS analogues in rare-earth telluride/pnictide chemistry are also stable ground-state compounds (CS; TS). The best support comes from chemically similar Y–Te tellurides synthesized in evacuated tubes at 1000 °C and rare-earth telluride flux growth at 850 °C (CS; TS). Structurally similar SS analogues support rare-earth/main-group octahedral or high-coordinate frameworks, but they do not directly match the Y–Ge–Te target, so structural transferability is moderate rather than strong (SS). The most critical enabling factor is sealed-tube or flux-mediated control of Te chemical potential, which is supported by the telluride analogues (CS). The most critical limiting factor is phase competition and stoichiometry control among Y–Te, Ge–Te, and Y–Ge–Te phases, which is inferred from the phase-control difficulties reported in analogous rare-earth tellurides and pnictides (CS; SS; TS).
